## Walk me through your career so far

### Prompt
"Walk me through your career so far." This usually opens the interview. You have about two minutes to cover your path from the start to now, and to end on why you're moving towards this role.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you can tell a **clear, structured story** in about two minutes, not read your CV line by line.
- The **arc**: how your scope and skills grew, and the thread that connects your choices.
- **Impact**: one or two concrete achievements with numbers.
- **Fit**: why this role is the logical next step, which sets up the rest of the interview.
- Communication: confident, concise, and easy to follow.

#### Key points
- **Structure:** start → key chapters (two or three roles, not every job) → present → why this role. Spend most of the time on the last few years.
- **One thread** that connects your story: for example "making complex products feel fast and simple", "moving from building features to building platforms", or "frontend engineering for data-heavy products".
- **Each chapter:** role and context in one line, what you owned, one result with a number, what you learned that led to the next step.
- **End with the bridge:** what you want next and why this company and role offer it.
- **Length:** about 2 minutes (roughly 250 to 300 spoken words). Offer more detail rather than giving it all.
- **Tailor** to the role: for an AI company, highlight streaming UIs, LLM features or AI-assisted tooling; for a fintech, reliability, security and accessibility.
- **Avoid:** childhood history, a list of technologies, negativity about past employers, a five-minute monologue.

#### Model answer
*Example answer (adapt to your own experience), for a senior frontend role at an AI company:*

"I'm a frontend engineer with about eight years' experience, and the thread through my career is building complex, data-heavy products that still feel fast and simple.

I started at a digital agency, building marketing sites and small React apps for different clients. It taught me to ship quickly and to care about performance and accessibility, because clients measured both.

Then I joined a fintech scale-up as one of its first frontend engineers. Over four years I went from building features to owning the web platform: I led the migration from a jQuery checkout to React behind feature flags, which raised conversion by about 3%, and I set up our design system and testing practices as the team grew from 5 to 30 frontend engineers.

For the last two years I've been a senior engineer at a B2B analytics company, leading the frontend for our dashboards. The highlight was rebuilding our charting and data layer, which cut dashboard load time from around 6 seconds to under 2 on large accounts. Recently I also built our first LLM-powered feature, a natural-language query box with streaming responses, and that's where I realised what I want next.

I'm looking for a role where frontend engineering is central to an AI product, where streaming, real-time interaction and trust in the UI really matter. That's why this role appeals to me: you're building interfaces people use to work with models every day, and I'd bring both the platform experience and the hands-on streaming UI work."

(About 2 minutes spoken.)

#### Another worked example
*Example answer (adapt to your own experience), for a career changer moving into a frontend role at a bank:*

"I started my career as a data analyst at a retailer, where I kept building internal dashboards and found I enjoyed the UI side most. I took on more and more frontend work, then moved into a junior frontend role on the same team, building React tools for our merchandising teams.

Three years ago I joined a travel company as a frontend engineer on the booking flow. I improved accessibility on our checkout to meet WCAG AA, which also reduced form errors by about 20%, and I led the move to TypeScript for our team.

Now I'm looking to work in a regulated, high-trust product where quality and accessibility matter as much as speed. Your focus on accessible, reliable banking experiences matches what I've been most proud of, so this feels like the right next step."

#### A third worked example
**Shaping the same career for different roles.** For a staff platform role, emphasise design systems, migrations and influencing many teams. For a product-focused role, emphasise user impact and collaboration with design and product. For a startup, emphasise breadth, speed and ownership. The facts stay the same; the thread and highlights change.

#### The 30-second version
"I'm a frontend engineer with eight years' experience building data-heavy products that feel fast. I've led a React checkout migration that raised conversion, built a design system for a growing team, and most recently rebuilt our analytics dashboards and shipped our first streaming LLM feature. I'm now looking for a role where frontend is central to an AI product, which is exactly what this team is doing."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Opening line**: who you are and your thread.
2. **Early chapter**: one or two sentences, what it taught you.
3. **Middle chapter(s)**: growth in scope, one result with numbers each.
4. **Current role**: your biggest recent impact.
5. **Bridge**: what you want next and why this role.
6. **Stop** at about two minutes; invite questions.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| Reads every job from the CV | Two or three chapters with a clear thread |
| Lists technologies | Describes scope, ownership and results |
| No numbers | One concrete result per chapter |
| Ends with "so yeah, that's me" | Ends with a bridge to this role |
| Five minutes | About two minutes, offering more detail |

#### Useful phrases
- "The thread through my career has been…"
- "That taught me…, which led me to…"
- "The result I'm proudest of there was…"
- "That's why this role is the natural next step."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Too long** or too much early history.
- **No clear reason** for moves.
- **Negativity** about past employers.
- **Not tailoring** to the role.
- **Rambling** without a structure.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"Why did you leave the fintech?"** — "I'd built the platform and wanted to go deeper on data visualisation and performance at a larger scale, which the analytics role offered."
- **"What's the project you're proudest of?"** — Have your most impactful project ready as a STAR story.
- **"Why move into AI now?"** — "Building our natural-language query feature showed me how much the interface shapes trust and usefulness of models, and I want to work on that full-time."
- **"Where do you see yourself in a few years?"** — "Leading frontend architecture for an AI product, mentoring others, and shaping how users work with models."
- **"What's a gap or change in your CV?"** — Explain briefly and positively: what you did and learned.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Progression** in scope and impact.
- **Self-awareness** about your strengths and direction.
- **A credible reason** for this role.

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** write your thread in one sentence, pick two or three chapters, and one result with a number for each.
- Record yourself and trim to two minutes; prepare the 30-second version too.
- Tailor the ending for each company you interview with.
- Checklist: thread · chapters · numbers · current impact · bridge · two minutes · positive tone.

## Why are you looking to leave your current company?

### Prompt
"Why are you looking to leave your current company?" Recruiters and hiring managers almost always ask this. How do you answer honestly while staying positive and relevant to the new role?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether your **motivation is positive** (moving towards something) rather than only running away from problems.
- **Professionalism**: no badmouthing of colleagues, managers or the company.
- **Consistency** with the rest of your story and with what this role offers.
- **Red flags**: conflict-driven moves, short tenure patterns, unrealistic expectations, purely money-driven answers.
- Whether the **things you want** actually exist in this role, so you'll stay.

#### Key points
- **Frame it as "towards", not "away from"**: growth, scope, domain, impact, technology, mission.
- **Be honest but selective**: if there are problems (reorganisation, stalled product, poor management), mention them briefly and neutrally, then pivot to what you want.
- **Connect to this role**: the reason you're leaving should be something this job provides.
- **Keep it short**: 30 to 60 seconds.
- **Acceptable reasons:** limited growth or scope, wanting a new domain (AI, fintech), wanting a bigger or smaller company, product direction changed, team reorganised, wanting deeper technical challenges, relocation, layoffs.
- **Handling difficult cases:**
  - **Laid off:** say so plainly; it's common and not a failure.
  - **Short tenure:** explain briefly what changed, and what you learned.
  - **Bad manager or culture:** avoid personal criticism; talk about the kind of environment you work best in.
  - **Money:** it's valid, but lead with other motivations; save compensation for the right conversation.
- **Never:** criticise individuals, share confidential information, or sound bitter.

#### Model answer
*Example answer (adapt to your own experience):*

"I've really enjoyed my three years at my current company. I led the rebuild of our dashboard frontend and helped grow the frontend team from four to twelve, so I'm proud of what we've done.

The reason I'm looking now is about what I want to do next. Our product is mature and the roadmap is mostly incremental, so the frontend work is increasingly maintenance. I've found that what excites me most is the newest work we've done: building our first LLM-powered feature, with streaming responses and real-time interaction. I want that to be my main focus, at a company where AI is central to the product rather than an add-on.

That's what drew me to this role: the interface is core to how people use your models, and the problems around streaming, latency and trust are exactly the ones I want to work on."

#### Another worked example
*Example answer (adapt to your own experience), after a layoff at a startup:*

"My last company, a Series B travel startup, went through a round of layoffs in March when funding conditions changed, and the frontend team was reduced from eight to three. My role was one of those cut. I'm proud of my two years there: I owned the booking flow and improved mobile conversion by about 15%.

Since then I've been thinking about what I want next, and it's a product with a strong focus on quality and users, where I can work on complex flows like payments. That's why your team stood out to me."

#### A third worked example
**Reframing difficult reasons.**
```
Away from (don't say)                    → Towards (say instead)
"My manager micromanages"                → "I do my best work with ownership and autonomy, which this role offers"
"The codebase is a mess and nobody cares" → "I want to work somewhere that invests in engineering quality, like your testing culture"
"I'm bored"                              → "I'm ready for bigger technical challenges, like scaling a real-time product"
"They don't pay enough"                  → (lead with growth; discuss pay with the recruiter at the right time)
```

#### The 30-second version
"I've had a great three years and led some work I'm proud of, but the product is now mature and mostly incremental. The work that's excited me most recently is building AI features with streaming interfaces, and I want that to be my main focus at a company where AI is central. That's exactly what this role is."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Positive opening**: one line on what you valued and achieved.
2. **Honest, neutral reason**: brief and factual.
3. **Pivot to what you want**: growth, domain, scope.
4. **Connect to this role** specifically.
5. **Stop** within about a minute.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| Complains about manager or colleagues | Neutral, brief, then moves on |
| "I just want more money" | Leads with growth and fit |
| Vague: "time for a change" | Specific about what you want next |
| Reason unrelated to the role | Reason the role clearly satisfies |
| Long story | 30 to 60 seconds |

#### Useful phrases
- "I've really valued… and I'm proud of…"
- "What I'm looking for now is…"
- "That's what drew me to this role."
- "The company went through a restructure, and my role was affected."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Badmouthing** anyone.
- **Over-explaining** a layoff or conflict.
- **Inconsistency** with your career story.
- **Sounding desperate** or bitter.
- Giving a reason the **new role also has** (e.g. leaving because of on-call for a role with on-call).

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"Have you talked to your manager about this?"** — "Yes, we discussed growth paths, but the opportunities I'm looking for in AI aren't on our roadmap in the next year."
- **"What would make you stay?"** — "Honestly, a role focused on AI products, which isn't likely there; that's why I'm exploring."
- **"Why have you moved jobs often?"** — "Each move was for clear growth: … I'm now looking for a place to stay longer and go deep."
- **"Are you interviewing elsewhere?"** — "Yes, a few roles focused on AI products; this one is my top choice because…"
- **"What are you looking for in your next manager?"** — "Clear goals, autonomy, and regular feedback."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Mature, positive motivation**.
- **Self-awareness** about what environments suit you.
- **Alignment** with the role.

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** write your real reason, then rewrite it as a "towards" statement linked to the role.
- Prepare versions for a layoff or short tenure if relevant.
- Practise saying it in under 45 seconds.
- Checklist: positive opening · honest neutral reason · what you want · link to role · short.

## Why this company specifically?

### Prompt
"Why do you want to work here specifically?" This comes up in almost every loop. Generic answers such as "I love your mission" are marked down. Interviewers want specific reasons that connect the company to your own experience.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Preparation:** have you actually used the product, read the engineering blog, and understood the business, or did you skim the homepage?
- **Genuine motivation:** will you still care about this company in month eight, when the novelty is gone?
- **Fit between you and them:** can you explain why your skills matter to *their* problems, not just why you want any job?
- **Judgement:** do you understand what the frontend actually does for this company: revenue, trust, speed, differentiation?
- **Retention risk:** a candidate with clear, specific reasons is more likely to accept and to stay.

#### Key points
- **Use three layers of reasons**, ideally one of each:
  1. **The product and its users:** something specific you noticed using it, including what you would improve.
  2. **The engineering challenge:** a frontend problem this company has that excites you (real-time collaboration, streaming AI output, huge data tables, global scale, accessibility in a regulated market).
  3. **The team and how it works:** something concrete from their blog, open-source work, talks, or what interviewers have told you so far.
- **Tie each reason to your experience:** "You're doing X; I've done Y, and I want to go deeper on it."
- **Be specific enough that it couldn't be pasted into another company's interview.** Test: swap the company name. If the answer still works, it's too generic.
- **Research sources:** use the product as a real user (sign up, try the free tier, open dev tools), the engineering blog, conference talks, the public GitHub repos, the changelog, job descriptions for other roles (they show where the company is investing), recent funding or earnings news, and Glassdoor with caution.
- **Length:** 60 to 90 seconds. Offer to go deeper on any reason.
- **Honesty matters:** if compensation or location is part of it, that's fine, but lead with reasons about the work.
- **Avoid:** flattery without detail, reciting the mission statement, "I've always wanted to work at a big company", and anything that sounds like a sales pitch for them back to them.

#### Model answer
*Example answer (adapt to your own experience), for a senior frontend role at a company building a collaborative design tool:*

"There are three reasons, and they all connect to what I've been doing.

First, the product. I've used it daily for about two years, first as a developer working with designers and then to sketch my own UI ideas. What strikes me is that it feels like a native app in the browser. When I opened the performance tab out of curiosity, I saw you're rendering most of the canvas yourselves rather than relying on the DOM, which is why it stays smooth with thousands of layers. I've hit the limits of DOM rendering on our dashboards, so I'm very interested in how you made that trade-off.

Second, the engineering problem. Multiplayer editing is one of the hardest frontend problems there is: conflict resolution, presence, offline edits, and keeping everything at 60 frames per second. I read your blog post on how you moved away from operational transforms, and it matched a smaller version of what I built: collaborative comments and live cursors on our reporting tool. I want to work on that problem at full depth rather than as a side feature.

Third, the team. Two things stood out. You publish detailed post-mortems, which tells me the culture is honest about failure, and the engineer I spoke to in the first round described how frontend engineers own features end to end, including the metrics. That's how I work best.

So it's not that I want any frontend job. It's that you have the frontend problem I most want to solve, and I've already built a smaller version of it."

#### Another worked example
*Example answer (adapt to your own experience), for a frontend role at a digital bank:*

"I've been a customer for three years, and I switched from my old bank mainly because the app made money feel understandable. The spending insights changed how I budget.

From an engineering point of view, what interests me is that you're building for trust in a regulated market. Everything has to be fast, but also accessible, auditable and correct. I led WCAG AA work on a travel checkout, which cut form errors by about 20%, and I saw from your accessibility statement that you're aiming for AA across web and app, so that's an area where I'd contribute from day one.

I also noticed from your job listings that you're building out a web platform team and a shared design system across web and mobile. I helped build a design system from scratch at my last company, so I know how much of that work is about adoption and getting teams to agree, not just components. That's the next stage I want to grow into."

#### A third worked example
**Different kinds of company call for different evidence:**
- **AI company:** use the product heavily and comment on its UI: how it streams, how it handles errors, citations, or long conversations. Link to any LLM feature you've built.
- **Big tech:** name the specific team or product area, and the scale problem (millions of users, many locales, low-end devices). "Big company" alone is not a reason.
- **Early-stage startup:** talk about the stage: owning whole features, shaping the frontend from the start, the founders' background, and the problem space. Show you understand the risk and want it.
- **Agency or consultancy:** variety of clients, speed, and craft. Mention specific work of theirs you admire.

#### The 30-second version
"I've used your product for two years and I'm fascinated by how it feels like a native app in the browser. Real-time multiplayer at 60 frames per second is the frontend problem I most want to work on, and I've built a smaller version of it with live cursors and collaborative comments. And from your post-mortems and my conversation with the team, the way you work, owning features end to end, matches how I work best."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Research before the interview:** use the product, read two or three engineering posts, scan job listings and recent news. Note three specific facts.
2. **Pick three reasons:** product, engineering challenge, team or culture.
3. **Attach evidence** to each reason: something you saw, read or heard.
4. **Connect each to your experience:** "I've done a smaller version of this…"
5. **Close with the bridge:** why this role, now, is the right next step.
6. **Run the swap test:** replace the company name. If it still works, add more detail.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "I love your mission and culture" | Names a specific blog post, product detail or team practice |
| Could be said to any company | Fails the swap test (only fits this company) |
| All about what they give you | Balances what you want with what you bring |
| No sign of using the product | Mentions real use, and even an improvement idea |
| Recites the About page | Connects their problem to your past work |

#### Useful phrases
- "Three things drew me here, and they all connect to what I've been working on."
- "When I used the product, I noticed…"
- "I read your post on…, and it matched a problem I solved at…"
- "It's less that I want a frontend job, and more that you have the frontend problem I most want to work on."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Flattery without facts:** "You're the best in the industry."
- **Mission-statement recital:** repeating their words back to them.
- **Only talking about benefits:** salary, remote work, brand name.
- **Getting facts wrong:** misnaming products or quoting an old blog post as current. Check dates.
- **Over-criticising the product:** one thoughtful improvement idea is good; a list of complaints is not.
- **Same answer for every company.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What would you improve about our product?"** — "One thing: on large files, the layers panel becomes slow to search. I'd look at virtualising the list and moving search to a worker. But I'd want to see the data first; it may already be planned."
- **"Who else are you interviewing with?"** — "A couple of companies working on collaborative and data-heavy products. This one is my first choice because of the multiplayer work." Be honest, but no need to name them.
- **"What do you know about our business model?"** — Show you know how they make money and how the frontend contributes: conversion, retention, enterprise features.
- **"What would make you turn down an offer from us?"** — "If the role turned out to be mostly maintenance with no ownership of the core problems, but from what I've heard, that's not the case."
- **"Why not a competitor?"** — Name something concrete that distinguishes this company: technical approach, product focus, or culture.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Depth of research** that goes beyond the first page of results.
- **A clear link** between their challenges and your strengths.
- **Commercial awareness:** understanding how the frontend creates value for this business.
- **Realism:** you know what's hard about the place, and still want to come.

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** for your next interview, write down three specific facts about the company (product, engineering, team) and one experience of yours that connects to each.
- Use the product for 30 minutes as a real user and note one thing you'd improve.
- Run the swap test on your answer.
- Checklist: three reasons · specific evidence · links to your experience · bridge to the role · 60 to 90 seconds · no flattery.

#### Variations you might be asked
- "What do you know about us?"
- "Why this team rather than another team here?"
- "Why are you interested in this role?"
- "What excites you about our product roadmap?"

## Walk me through your most impactful project

### Prompt
"Walk me through your most impactful project." This is a standard senior-level prompt. The interviewer is listening for scale, stakes, complexity, your own contribution, and the business outcome.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Scope and stakes:** how big and important was the problem? Did it matter to users and the business?
- **Your contribution:** what *you* decided and did, separate from what the team did.
- **Technical depth:** can you explain the architecture and the trade-offs, and go two or three levels deeper when pushed?
- **Leadership:** did you influence others, align stakeholders, unblock people, and make calls under uncertainty?
- **Outcome:** measurable results, plus what you learned and would do differently.
- **Level calibration:** this one story often decides whether you are assessed as mid-level, senior or staff.

#### Key points
- **Choose the right project:** high stakes, clear business impact, real technical complexity, and a significant personal role. Recent (last two or three years) is better.
- **Structure it as STAR, with a technical middle:**
  - **Situation:** the product, the users, the problem, and why it mattered now (with numbers).
  - **Task:** your role and what success meant.
  - **Action:** the key decisions, the options you considered, why you chose one, how you brought people along, and what went wrong along the way.
  - **Result:** metrics (performance, conversion, revenue, error rates, team speed), plus what you learned.
- **Use "I" for your decisions and "we" for the team's work**, and be precise about which is which.
- **Prepare two or three levels of depth:** a two-minute overview, then the architecture, then specific details (for example, the caching strategy or how you migrated without downtime).
- **Include a setback:** a project with no problems sounds either small or untrue.
- **Have a diagram in your head** you could draw on a whiteboard.

#### Model answer
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): rebuilding a slow analytics dashboard:*

"**Situation.** I was a senior frontend engineer at a B2B analytics company. Our biggest customers, the accounts paying over 100,000 a year, were complaining that dashboards took 6 to 10 seconds to load and froze when they filtered. Two enterprise renewals were explicitly at risk, worth about 1.2 million combined.

**Task.** I was asked to lead the frontend side of a performance push. Success meant dashboards loading in under 2 seconds for our largest account, and filtering that felt instant, within one quarter, without stopping feature work.

**Action.** First, I measured rather than guessed. I added performance marks and real-user monitoring, and found three causes: each chart fetched its own data, so a dashboard with 20 charts made 20 requests with lots of duplication; we sent raw rows to the browser and aggregated them in JavaScript on the main thread; and every filter change re-rendered every chart.

I proposed a plan in a short design document with three changes. One: a shared data layer using TanStack Query, so charts that needed the same query shared a single request and cache. Two: moving aggregation to the backend, which I agreed with the backend lead, plus a web worker for the remaining client-side transforms. Three: memoising charts by their inputs and virtualising the large tables.

The hardest part wasn't technical. The product manager was worried about pausing features, so I split the work into slices that each shipped behind a feature flag and each showed a measurable gain. We rolled out to internal users, then to one friendly customer, then everyone.

One setback: the first worker version was slower on small dashboards because of the cost of copying data to the worker. I added a size threshold so small datasets stayed on the main thread.

**Result.** Load time for the largest account went from about 8 seconds to 1.6 at the 75th percentile, and filter interactions from over a second to around 100 milliseconds. Both at-risk renewals signed. Support tickets about performance dropped by about 70%. The data layer became the standard pattern for new features, and I wrote the guide other teams used.

What I'd do differently: I'd add performance budgets to CI from the start. We had a regression two months later that a budget would have caught."

#### Another worked example
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): a checkout migration at an e-commerce company:*

"**Situation:** our checkout was a 7-year-old jQuery page with a 68% completion rate, and every change took weeks because nobody dared touch it. **Task:** I led a team of three to rebuild it in React without a big-bang release. **Action:** I proposed a strangler approach: we rebuilt one step at a time (address, delivery, payment) and routed a growing share of traffic to the new step with feature flags, comparing completion rates. I set up end-to-end tests on the old flow first, so we had a safety net, and worked with the payments team on 3-D Secure handling, which was the riskiest part. When the delivery step showed a 2% drop in an experiment, we found an autocomplete bug on mobile Safari before rolling out further. **Result:** completion rose from 68% to 71%, worth about 2 million a year, and release time for checkout changes went from weeks to days."

#### A third worked example
**How the same project sounds at different levels:**
- **Mid-level:** "I built the shared data layer and the worker-based aggregation." Focus on your own implementation.
- **Senior:** "I diagnosed the causes, designed the plan, led three engineers, and agreed the backend changes with another team." Focus on design and delivery.
- **Staff:** "I set up the measurement, made the case to leadership linked to renewals, established the data-layer pattern across four teams, and added performance budgets to the platform." Focus on influence and lasting change across teams.
Pick the framing that matches the level you're interviewing for, and make sure it's true.

#### The 30-second version
"Our enterprise customers' dashboards took 8 seconds to load, and two renewals worth 1.2 million were at risk. I led the frontend fix: I measured first, then introduced a shared data layer, moved aggregation to the backend and a worker, and memoised the charts, shipping it in flagged slices. Load time fell to 1.6 seconds, both renewals signed, and performance tickets dropped 70%."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Pick the project:** stakes, complexity, your role, a clear result.
2. **Open with stakes:** users, money, risk, and numbers.
3. **State your role** and the definition of success.
4. **Explain three key decisions:** the options, the trade-offs and why you chose.
5. **Show leadership:** stakeholders, other teams, unblocking people.
6. **Include a setback** and how you handled it.
7. **Give the results** in numbers, then the lesson.
8. **Prepare deeper layers:** architecture diagram, specific technical details.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "We rebuilt the dashboard" | "I diagnosed three causes and proposed…" |
| Technology list ("React, Redux, D3") | Decisions and trade-offs |
| No business context | Renewals at risk, worth 1.2 million |
| Smooth, no problems | A setback and how it was fixed |
| "It was much faster" | 8 seconds to 1.6 at the 75th percentile |
| Ends at launch | Ends with lasting change and a lesson |

#### Useful phrases
- "The stakes were…"
- "My role was…, and success meant…"
- "I considered A and B; I chose B because…"
- "The hardest part wasn't technical; it was…"
- "What I'd do differently is…"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Picking a project for its technology** rather than its impact.
- **"We" all the way through:** the interviewer can't tell what you did.
- **Too much detail too early:** start with the overview, then go deeper when asked.
- **No numbers**, or numbers you can't explain.
- **Exaggerating your role:** follow-up questions will expose it.
- **No reflection.**

- **Trade-off thinking**, not just implementation.

- Write the two-minute version, then the 30-second version, then prepare an architecture sketch.

## Tell me about a time you used AI to ship something you couldn't have shipped otherwise
- id: 4ffa01f9-4902-4b33-b280-e441674913a6
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- companies: Anthropic, OpenAI, Synthesia, Cohere
- tags: behavioral, AI, story-bank, ownership

### Prompt
"Tell me about a time you used AI to ship something you couldn't have shipped otherwise." This is a favourite at AI companies. It probes your hands-on experience with AI in production, your judgement, and your ownership of the result.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Real experience:** have you actually used AI, either as a product feature or as a development tool, on something that shipped to users?
- **Judgement:** where did you trust the model, where didn't you, and how did you check its output?
- **Ownership:** you remain responsible for correctness, security and quality, whoever (or whatever) wrote the code.
- **Leverage:** did AI change *what* was possible (scope, timeline, team size), not just save a few keystrokes?
- **Honesty about limits:** failures, hallucinations, and cost or latency problems you hit.

#### Key points
- **Two types of story work**, and it helps to have one of each:
  1. **AI as a feature:** you built an LLM-powered capability into the product (natural-language search, summarisation, a chat assistant, content generation).
  2. **AI as a tool:** AI coding assistants or agents let you deliver something that wasn't feasible otherwise (a large migration, a test suite, a prototype in days, working in an unfamiliar language).
- **Make the "couldn't have otherwise" concrete:** "A migration estimated at 6 engineer-weeks took 8 days", or "this feature wasn't possible without a model: the rules were too varied to write by hand."
- **Show your verification process:** tests, evals, reviews, staged rollouts, monitoring, human checks.
- **Frontend-specific details impress:** streaming responses, handling partial output, cancelling requests, showing uncertainty, citations, error and fallback states, rate limits, cost per request, prompt injection risks in rendered output.
- **Include what went wrong:** a hallucination that reached staging, a codemod the model got subtly wrong, latency that needed a redesign.
- **Avoid:** "I use Copilot every day" without a specific outcome, or claiming the AI did it all.

#### Model answer
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): AI as a product feature:*

"**Situation.** I was the frontend lead at a B2B analytics company. Our users, mostly operations managers, weren't analysts, and around 40% of new accounts never built a second dashboard because the query builder was too complex. We'd tried simplifying it twice without much improvement.

**Task.** I proposed a natural-language query box: type 'weekly orders by region for the last quarter', get a chart. We had one quarter and two engineers, me on the frontend and a backend engineer. Without a model, this would have meant building a parser for endless phrasings, which we'd estimated at more than a year.

**Action.** The model translated the question into our existing query format, which is a structured JSON object, not raw SQL. That was a deliberate choice: we validated the output against our schema and the user's permissions before running anything, so the model could never access data the user couldn't.

On the frontend, I built the experience around trust. We streamed the model's interpretation as it was generated ('Showing orders, grouped by region, weekly, last 90 days'), so users could see what it understood and correct it before the chart rendered. Each part of the interpretation was an editable chip, which turned the existing query builder into a correction tool. I handled cancellation when users kept typing, timeouts with a clear fallback to the manual builder, and an empty state with example questions.

To check quality, we built an evaluation set of 300 real questions from support tickets and sales demos, with expected queries, and ran it in CI whenever we changed the prompt. Accuracy started at 71%; by adding schema descriptions and a few examples to the prompt we reached 92%.

The setback: in beta, the model sometimes chose a plausible but wrong date field, 'created' instead of 'shipped', and users didn't notice. We fixed it by always showing the chosen field in the interpretation and adding those cases to the evals.

**Result.** We shipped in 11 weeks. Accounts using the feature built 2.4 times as many dashboards, and the share that never built a second dashboard dropped from 40% to 22%. Cost per query was under a cent with caching of repeated questions. It became a headline feature in sales demos."

#### Another worked example
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): AI as a development tool:*

"**Situation:** our design system had 400 components still using an old styling library, which blocked our move to server components. The migration was estimated at six engineer-weeks, and nobody could be spared. **Task:** I offered to do it alongside my normal work. **Action:** I used an AI coding agent, but with strong guardrails. I migrated ten components by hand first to define the pattern, wrote a short rules document, and had the agent migrate components in batches of twenty. Every batch had to pass visual regression tests in Storybook, type checks and unit tests before I reviewed it. I reviewed every diff myself. The agent was very good at the mechanical changes and poor at components with dynamic styles, so I took those 30 myself. Visual tests caught about a dozen subtle problems, mainly spacing in dark mode. **Result:** the migration took eight working days instead of six weeks, with zero production incidents, and unblocked the server components work a quarter early. I shared the rules document and process, and two other teams reused it."

#### A third worked example
**Smaller but still strong stories:**
- **Prototype speed:** using AI to build three interactive prototypes in two days for user testing, instead of one in a week, which changed the product decision.
- **Unfamiliar territory:** shipping a small Rust-to-WebAssembly module for image processing with AI help, with tests and a benchmark you understood fully before shipping.
- **Test coverage:** generating tests for a legacy module before a refactor, then checking each by deliberately breaking the code to make sure the tests failed.
In each case, say what made it possible and how you checked the result.

#### The 30-second version
"40% of our new accounts never built a second dashboard because the query builder was too hard. I led a natural-language query feature: the model produced our structured query format, which we validated against permissions, and the UI streamed the model's interpretation as editable chips so users could correct it. We built a 300-question eval set and went from 71% to 92% accuracy. We shipped in 11 weeks, something we'd estimated at over a year without a model, and dashboard creation more than doubled."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Situation:** the problem and why it was hard or impossible before.
2. **The "otherwise":** the estimate or limit without AI.
3. **Your approach:** where AI fitted, and the architecture or process.
4. **Guardrails:** validation, evals, tests, review, permissions, rollout.
5. **Frontend experience:** streaming, correction, fallbacks, trust.
6. **Setback:** what went wrong and how you fixed it.
7. **Results:** numbers for impact, quality, time, cost.
8. **Lesson:** what you'd do again and what you'd change.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "I use Copilot all the time" | One specific project with a measurable outcome |
| "The AI wrote it" | "I defined the pattern, reviewed every diff, tests gated each batch" |
| No verification | Evals, visual regression tests, staged rollout |
| Ignores failure | Wrong date-field problem found and fixed |
| No product thinking | Streaming interpretation, editable chips, fallback to manual builder |
| No cost or latency awareness | Cost per query, caching, timeouts |

#### Useful phrases
- "Without a model, this would have meant…, which we'd estimated at…"
- "I treated the model's output as untrusted input: we validated it against…"
- "We built an evaluation set from real questions and ran it in CI."
- "I stayed responsible for every line; the agent did the mechanical work."
- "Where it failed was…, so we…"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Vague tool usage** with no outcome.
- **Over-crediting the AI** or hiding that you used it. Both hurt trust.
- **No evaluation:** "it seemed to work well".
- **Ignoring security:** letting a model generate raw SQL or rendering model output as HTML without sanitising.
- **Missing the user experience:** latency, errors and uncertainty are frontend problems.
- **Hype language** that interviewers at AI companies see through quickly.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How did you handle hallucinations?"** — "Structured output validated against our schema and permissions, the interpretation shown to the user before the chart ran, evals for known failures, and a fallback to the manual builder."
- **"How did you decide which model to use?"** — "We ran our eval set against two models and compared accuracy, latency and cost; the smaller model was accurate enough for most queries, so we used it with the larger one as a fallback for complex questions."
- **"What about prompt injection?"** — "The model only produced a query object, which was validated and permission-checked on the server, so injected text couldn't access other data. We rendered model text as plain text, never HTML."
- **"How did you review AI-generated code at that volume?"** — "Small batches, strict automated gates, and I read every diff. Where I didn't understand something, I rewrote it."
- **"Would you do it differently now?"** — "I'd build the eval set before writing the first prompt, and log real questions from day one."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Judgement** about where AI helps and where it doesn't.
- **Engineering rigour** around non-deterministic systems.
- **Product thinking** about trust and correction.
- **Ownership:** you shipped it, you're accountable for it.

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** prepare one "AI as a feature" story and one "AI as a tool" story. If you don't have a production example, build a small project now (for example, a streaming chat UI with evals) so you can speak from experience.
- Write down the "otherwise" estimate and your verification process for each.
- Checklist: impossible-before · approach · guardrails · user experience · setback · numbers · lesson.

#### Variations you might be asked
- "How do you use AI tools in your day-to-day work?"
- "Tell me about an AI feature that didn't work."
- "How do you make sure AI-generated code is safe to ship?"

## What's the most senior engineering decision you've made?
- id: 4dc16150-d9eb-4b0a-bf6e-8b934640f1d1
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, leadership, risk-management

### Prompt
"What's the most senior engineering decision you've made?" This probes for staff-level signal: did you make a call that affected scope, risk or strategy, beyond your own code?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Scope of impact:** did the decision affect many teams, a product's direction, significant money, or long-term architecture?
- **Decision-making under uncertainty:** how you gathered information, weighed options and committed without perfect data.
- **Risk management:** how you reduced the downside: reversibility, staged rollouts, kill switches, fallback plans.
- **Influence without authority:** how you got others to agree, including people more senior than you.
- **Accountability:** owning the outcome, including when it went partly wrong.

#### Key points
- **What makes a decision "senior":**
  - It affected **other teams** or the whole organisation, not only your feature.
  - It was **hard to reverse** (a framework, a platform, a data model, a vendor).
  - It involved **trade-offs between business goals**, not only technical ones: speed versus quality, building versus buying, stopping a project.
  - Sometimes the most senior decision is **saying no** or **stopping something**.
- **Good frontend examples:** choosing a rendering architecture (moving to server rendering for SEO and performance), stopping a rewrite and choosing incremental migration, choosing micro-frontends or not, adopting or retiring a design system, deciding to delay a launch for accessibility or security, choosing a state management standard for many teams.
- **Show the process:** framing the problem, the options (including doing nothing), criteria, evidence such as prototypes or data, who you consulted, the decision document, and how you'd know if it was wrong.
- **Two-way versus one-way doors:** for reversible decisions, move fast; for irreversible ones, invest in evidence. Saying this shows maturity.
- **The result and the review:** did it work? What would you change?

#### Model answer
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): stopping a full rewrite:*

"**Situation.** At a travel company, leadership had approved a full rewrite of our web app from an old AngularJS codebase into React, planned at 9 months with a team of eight. I was a senior engineer, and three months in I was asked to take over technical leadership after the previous lead left. When I looked closely, we'd rebuilt about 15% of the features, the old app was still getting new features, so the gap was growing, and the new app had nothing in production.

**Task.** I had to decide whether to continue as planned, or change course. Either way, it affected eight engineers, the product roadmap, and a commitment the CTO had made to the board.

**Action.** I spent two weeks gathering evidence. I counted the routes and features remaining and our real pace, which projected 20 months, not 6 more. I interviewed product managers about what was coming on the roadmap. And I built a small proof of concept that ran a React page inside the AngularJS app, sharing login and routing.

I wrote a decision document with three options: continue the rewrite, freeze features on the old app to catch up, or switch to an incremental migration where we moved one route at a time, starting with the highest-traffic pages, and new features were built in React from day one. I recommended the third. Its risks were the complexity of running two frameworks and a slightly bigger bundle for a while, which I proposed to manage with a clear rule: no new AngularJS code, and a target date to remove it.

The difficult part was the CTO, who had promised a launch date. I met with them before the wider review, showed the projection honestly, and reframed it: instead of one big launch in 20 months, we'd have React pages in production within 6 weeks, with improvements visible every month. They agreed, on condition that I reported progress monthly.

**Result.** The search page, which was 40% of traffic, went live in React after 7 weeks, and its load time improved by about 35%. Over the next 14 months we migrated the rest and removed AngularJS completely. Feature work never stopped. Looking back, I'd have pushed harder earlier to agree shared component rules between the two apps, because we had some visual inconsistencies for a few months."

#### Another worked example
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): delaying a launch:*

"**Situation:** two days before a major public sector launch, our accessibility audit found that the main application form couldn't be completed with a screen reader: focus was lost after validation errors. **Task:** as frontend lead, I had to recommend whether to launch. **Action:** I estimated the fix at four days, assessed the legal and reputational risk, since the service was required to meet WCAG AA, and proposed delaying one week. I also offered an alternative: launch only the read-only parts on the original date. I presented both options to the product director with the risks written down. **Result:** we launched the read-only parts on time and the form a week later, fully accessible. That week cost some marketing plans, but the service passed its formal assessment first time, which the previous service had failed."

#### A third worked example
**Smaller-company or earlier-career versions still count** if the scope is right for the context:
- Choosing TypeScript for a startup's whole frontend and leading the migration.
- Deciding to buy a charting library rather than continue building one in-house, and handling the team's disappointment.
- Choosing to remove a feature that 2% of users used but which caused 30% of bugs, with data to support it.
Explain why the decision was significant *for that organisation*.

#### The 30-second version
"I took over technical leadership of a React rewrite that was three months in and 15% complete. My projection showed 20 more months, not 6. I wrote a decision document comparing options, built a proof of concept, and persuaded the CTO to switch to an incremental route-by-route migration. Our highest-traffic page was in production in 7 weeks, 35% faster, and we fully retired AngularJS 14 months later without stopping feature work."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Context and stakes:** who and what the decision affected.
2. **Why it was hard:** uncertainty, irreversibility, politics, competing goals.
3. **Evidence:** data, prototypes, conversations.
4. **Options:** at least three, including doing nothing.
5. **Recommendation and risk reduction:** staged rollout, rules, checkpoints.
6. **Getting agreement:** especially from senior people.
7. **Result and review:** outcome and what you'd change.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "I chose Zustand over Redux" for one feature | A decision affecting eight engineers and a board commitment |
| No alternatives considered | Three options with explicit trade-offs |
| Decided alone | Consulted product, built evidence, aligned the CTO |
| Ignores risk | Proof of concept, rules, monthly checkpoints |
| Everything went perfectly | Honest reflection on what to change |

#### Useful phrases
- "This was a one-way door, so I invested in evidence first."
- "I laid out three options, including doing nothing."
- "I met them one to one before the wider review."
- "I reframed it from one big launch to value every month."
- "Here's how we'd know if I was wrong…"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Picking a small technical choice** (a library for one feature).
- **Claiming a decision your manager actually made.**
- **No evidence:** decisions based only on preference.
- **Ignoring people:** a technically right decision that nobody agreed to.
- **No reflection or follow-up.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if the CTO had said no?"** — "I'd have asked what would change their mind, perhaps agreed a two-week trial of the incremental approach, and if they still said no, committed to the rewrite and made the risks visible in reporting."
- **"How did the team react?"** — "Some were disappointed to keep working with AngularJS, so I made sure everyone got time on React routes and set a firm date to remove AngularJS."
- **"How did you handle the bundle size during the transition?"** — "We loaded the React runtime only on migrated routes at first and set a budget that blocked merges if exceeded."
- **"What decision would you reverse?"** — Show you can admit mistakes: name one and what you learned.
- **"How do you decide when to escalate?"** — "When a decision affects other teams' plans, money or commitments made to others, I bring in the people accountable early."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Scope** beyond your own work.
- **Structured decision-making** with evidence.
- **Courage** to change direction or say no.
- **Influence** and clear communication upwards.
- **Accountability** for the outcome.

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** list decisions you made that affected other teams, money or long-term architecture. Choose the one with the widest scope and clearest evidence.
- Write a one-page decision document for it (context, options, recommendation, risks) to rehearse the structure.
- Checklist: stakes · difficulty · evidence · options · risk reduction · agreement · result · reflection.

#### Variations you might be asked
- "Tell me about a decision with significant risk."
- "Tell me about a time you changed the direction of a project."
- "Describe a time you had to make a decision without enough information."

## Describe a time you disagreed with a senior leader
- id: e09f91db-24a7-4e25-8e03-da996b85838e
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, conflict, communication

### Prompt
"Describe a time you disagreed with a senior leader." The interviewer is looking for whether you can disagree without being abrasive, whether you have the courage of your convictions, and whether you can find middle ground.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Courage:** will you raise concerns with people more senior than you, or stay quiet?
- **How you disagree:** with data and respect, in the right setting, without making it personal.
- **Openness:** did you genuinely try to understand their reasons, and could you have been wrong?
- **Resolution:** did you find a compromise, change your mind, or commit to their decision once it was made ("disagree and commit")?
- **Outcome and relationship:** did the result serve the business, and did the relationship survive or improve?

#### Key points
- **Choose a real disagreement with stakes:** a deadline, scope, a technical direction, quality versus speed, a hiring or process decision. Not a trivial preference.
- **Show the leader's point of view fairly.** They usually had good reasons: a commercial commitment, a board deadline, information you didn't have.
- **How to disagree well:**
  - Raise it **privately first**, not in a big meeting.
  - **Ask questions** to understand the goal behind their position.
  - Bring **evidence:** data, prototypes, estimates, user research.
  - **Offer options**, not just objections.
  - Make the **risk visible** in writing if it's significant.
  - Once a decision is made, **commit fully** (unless it's an ethical, legal or safety issue).
- **Good outcomes come in three forms:** you persuaded them; you found a middle path; or they decided against you and you committed (and perhaps you were later proved right or wrong). All can be strong stories if handled well.
- **Avoid** stories where the leader is a villain, where you went over their head without telling them, or where you "won" by stubbornness.

#### Model answer
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): disagreement about a launch scope:*

"**Situation.** I was a senior frontend engineer at a SaaS company. Our VP of Product wanted to launch a redesigned onboarding flow at a big customer conference in five weeks. They also wanted to include an AI setup assistant that wasn't in the original plan. My estimate was that the redesign alone needed four to five weeks, and the assistant at least another three, including testing.

**Task.** I believed shipping both would mean cutting testing and accessibility, and launching something unreliable in front of our biggest audience of the year. I needed to raise this without looking like I was blocking the launch.

**Action.** I asked for 20 minutes with the VP privately, rather than raising it in the planning meeting. I started by asking what success at the conference looked like for them. It turned out the real goal was a live demo that showed we were investing in AI, because competitors were announcing similar features.

That changed the conversation. I brought my breakdown of the work and showed which parts were risky: the assistant needed streaming responses, error handling and evals we hadn't built. Then I offered three options: ship both with known quality risks; ship the redesign fully and demo the assistant as a clearly labelled preview behind a flag, available to a waitlist; or delay the redesign. I recommended the second.

The VP pushed back. They felt a preview would look weak. So I offered to build a polished prototype of the assistant within a week, so they could see what the preview would look like on stage. That helped: they could see it looked impressive without needing to be fully ready.

**Result.** We launched the redesigned onboarding on time, fully tested. The assistant was demoed on stage as a preview with a waitlist, and 1,200 customers signed up for it that week, which gave us beta users and real questions for our evals. It shipped properly six weeks later. The VP later asked me to join the planning for the next quarter's AI features, which I took as a sign the disagreement had built trust rather than damaged it."

#### Another worked example
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): disagree and commit:*

"**Situation:** our head of engineering decided all teams would move to a new internal component framework built by the platform team. I believed, based on a prototype, it would hurt our checkout's performance, about 40 KB more JavaScript on our most important page. **Action:** I shared my benchmark with them privately and suggested checkout be excluded for a quarter. They explained the goal was consistency across teams and lower maintenance, and that the platform team had performance work planned. They decided to go ahead. I said I'd commit, but asked that we agree a performance budget for checkout and review it after the migration. I then worked with the platform team, contributing two optimisations that reduced the overhead to about 12 KB. **Result:** checkout migrated with no measurable conversion drop, and the budget became standard for all teams. I learned that 'disagree and commit' can still mean shaping the outcome."

#### A third worked example
**Other kinds of disagreement that make good stories:**
- **Security or privacy:** pushing back on storing tokens in local storage, or on adding a tracking script with personal data, with a written risk assessment.
- **Process:** disagreeing with a mandate for 100% test coverage, and proposing coverage targets for critical paths instead.
- **Hiring:** raising concerns about a candidate in a debrief when the hiring manager was keen.
- **When you were wrong:** you argued against something, it went ahead, and it worked. Saying what you learned shows humility.

#### The 30-second version
"Our VP wanted to launch a redesign and an AI assistant at a conference in five weeks; I estimated eight. I met them privately, learned the real goal was showing AI investment, and offered options. I recommended shipping the redesign fully and demoing the assistant as a preview, and built a prototype to show them how it would look. We launched on time with quality, 1,200 people joined the waitlist, and the VP brought me into the next quarter's AI planning."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Context:** who the leader was, what they wanted, and why it mattered.
2. **Your concern:** what you believed and why, with evidence.
3. **How you raised it:** privately, with questions first.
4. **Their perspective:** the real goal or constraint you discovered.
5. **Options:** what you proposed and your recommendation.
6. **Resolution:** agreement, compromise, or disagree and commit.
7. **Result:** for the business and the relationship.
8. **Lesson.**

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "My manager was wrong, and I proved it" | Fairly explains the leader's reasons |
| Raised it in a big meeting | Raised it privately first |
| Only objections | Options and a recommendation |
| Opinion only | Breakdown, benchmark, prototype |
| Went over their head | Escalated openly, or not at all |
| Relationship damaged | Relationship strengthened |

#### Useful phrases
- "Before I shared my concern, I wanted to understand what success looked like for them."
- "Here's what I'm seeing in the estimate, and here are three options."
- "I may be missing context, so tell me if…"
- "I disagree, but I'll commit. Can we agree how we'll measure it?"
- "I wanted to make the risk visible, not to block the decision."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Making the leader a villain.**
- **Being passive:** "I mentioned it once and then did what they said."
- **Stubbornness:** refusing to commit once a decision is made.
- **Trivial disagreements** (tabs versus spaces).
- **No outcome or lesson.**
- **Going around the leader** without telling them.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if they'd insisted on shipping both?"** — "I'd have written down the risks, proposed the minimum testing we couldn't skip, and committed fully. If it had involved user data or legal risk, I'd have escalated openly."
- **"Have you ever been wrong in a disagreement?"** — Have one ready: what you argued, what happened, and what you learned.
- **"When would you go over someone's head?"** — "Only for ethical, legal or safety issues, or when a serious risk is being ignored, and I'd tell them first that I was doing it."
- **"How do you disagree in writing?"** — "A short document: the goal, my concern with evidence, options and a recommendation. It keeps it about the problem, not the person."
- **"How did you feel during it?"** — Be honest; showing you managed nerves or frustration is fine.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Respectful courage** and good timing.
- **Curiosity** about the other person's goals.
- **Options thinking** and evidence.
- **Commitment** once decided, and a healthy relationship afterwards.

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** list two or three disagreements with managers, directors or product leaders. Choose one with real stakes and a respectful resolution; keep a "disagree and commit" story as a backup.
- Write the leader's point of view in their words. If you can't, you don't understand it well enough yet.
- Checklist: stakes · evidence · private first · their goal · options · resolution · relationship · lesson.

#### Variations you might be asked
- "Tell me about a time you pushed back on a product decision."
- "Tell me about a time you had to disagree and commit."
- "Describe a conflict with a stakeholder."

## How do you stay current with frontend in 2026?
- id: 5cbc08af-65ed-45aa-a209-f7345598d419

- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: easy
- levels:

### Prompt
"How do you stay current with frontend in 2026?" This probes for genuine curiosity and self-directed learning. "I read Twitter" is not enough.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Genuine curiosity:** do you learn because you're interested, or only when forced to?
- **A system, not luck:** do you have deliberate habits and good sources?
- **Filtering and judgement:** the frontend world produces endless new tools. Can you tell what matters from what's hype, and decide when to adopt?
- **Applying what you learn:** have you brought new knowledge into your team's work?

## Tell me about a time you mentored someone

### Prompt
"Tell me about a time you mentored someone." This probes for leadership and for your ability to multiply yourself: making other engineers better, not just doing the work yourself.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Multiplying impact:** senior engineers are judged partly on how much better the people around them become.
- **Empathy and adaptation:** did you understand the person's goals, level and learning style, or give everyone the same advice?
- **Method:** did you have a plan (goals, regular check-ins, stretch work, feedback), or just answer questions when asked?
- **Letting go:** did you give them ownership, and resist the urge to take the work back when it got hard?
- **Outcome:** a visible change in the person, such as a promotion, independence, a project delivered, or confidence.

#### Key points
- **Pick a story with a clear before and after:** a junior who became independent, a backend engineer who became productive on the frontend, a new hire onboarded quickly, or a peer who grew into leading a project.
- **Start with their goals, not yours.** Ask what they want to get better at and what's blocking them.
- **Mentoring techniques worth naming:**
  - **Pairing**, with them driving at the keyboard.
  - **Graduated ownership:** small tasks, then a feature, then leading a project.
  - **Code review as teaching:** explaining *why*, linking to resources, asking questions rather than dictating fixes.
  - **Asking before telling:** "What options have you considered?"
  - **Regular one-to-ones** with written goals.
  - **Creating visibility:** letting them present their work, and crediting them publicly.
- **Show you adapted:** someone anxious needs reassurance and small wins; someone overconfident needs harder problems and honest feedback.
- **Mention what you learned yourself**, such as becoming a clearer communicator.
- **Avoid:** "I answered their questions on Slack", stories where you fixed everything for them, or taking credit for their success.

#### Model answer
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): mentoring a junior engineer to independence:*

"**Situation.** At a fintech company, we hired a junior frontend engineer, Sam, who had come through a bootcamp. After two months, they were struggling: pull requests sat open for days, they rarely asked questions, and their manager was worried. I was the senior engineer on the team, and I offered to mentor them.

**Task.** The goal we agreed with their manager was that within three months Sam would deliver a medium-sized feature on their own, from ticket to production.

**Action.** In our first one-to-one I asked what was hardest. It wasn't React; it was our codebase. They didn't understand how data flowed from the API through our query layer to components, so every task felt like starting from zero, and they didn't want to look slow by asking.

So I changed three things. First, I drew our architecture with them on a whiteboard and we turned it into a page in the onboarding docs, which they wrote. Second, we paired twice a week for a month, always with Sam at the keyboard. I asked questions like 'where do you think this data comes from?' rather than giving answers. Third, I changed how I reviewed their code. Instead of a list of fixes, I'd leave a few comments explaining the reason, and mark the rest as optional, so reviews felt like learning instead of judgement.

I also set up graduated ownership: bug fixes in the first month, a small component in the second, and in the third, the account statements download feature, which touched the API, a loading state and accessibility. When they got stuck on file streaming, I resisted rewriting it and instead pointed them to how we'd handled a similar case elsewhere.

**Result.** Sam shipped the statements feature in week eleven with only minor review comments, and presented it at our team demo. Their average time for a pull request to be merged went from about four days to one. Six months later they were mentoring the next new hire using the onboarding page they'd written, and they were promoted within the year. For me, I learned that asking questions works better than explaining, and I now use that architecture whiteboard session with every new joiner."

#### Another worked example
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): helping a backend engineer become productive on the frontend:*

"**Situation:** our team became full-stack, and a very experienced backend engineer, Priya, was suddenly writing React and felt like a beginner again, which was frustrating for someone so senior. **Action:** I treated it as mentoring a peer, not a junior. I mapped frontend ideas to concepts they already knew: React Query's cache was like a read-through cache with invalidation; component state was like local variables versus shared services. I gave them a reading list of four articles rather than a course, and we agreed they'd own the API-integration side of features first, where their strengths showed, while picking up styling and accessibility through review. **Result:** within two months they were shipping full features, and their backend knowledge improved our frontend error handling: they introduced typed API errors that we then used everywhere. Mentoring went both ways; I learned a lot about API design from them."

#### A third worked example
**Other mentoring formats that make good stories:**

- **Mentoring towards promotion:** helping a mid-level engineer build the evidence for a senior promotion by finding them a project with cross-team scope.

"A junior engineer on my team was struggling after two months. I found the real problem was understanding our codebase, not React. I ran an architecture session that they turned into onboarding docs, paired with them driving, changed my reviews to explain the why, and gave them gradually bigger work. #### Step-by-step framework

## What's a technical opinion you hold strongly but most people disagree with?
- id: cdc0a2ef-1848-49d2-b0d6-53a65a60fb12

- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium

- tags: behavioral, opinions, depth

### Prompt
"What's a technical opinion you hold strongly but most people disagree with?" This probes for first-principles thinking. The interviewer wants to see how you defend a position, and whether you can hold it while staying open to being wrong.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Independent thinking:** have you formed views from experience, or do you repeat what's popular?
- **Depth:** can you explain the reasoning behind your opinion, with evidence from real projects?
- **Nuance:** do you know the conditions where your opinion is wrong, and the strongest argument against it?
- **Intellectual honesty:** can you hold a strong view loosely and change your mind with new evidence?
- **Communication:** can you disagree with common wisdom without sounding arrogant or contrarian for the sake of it?

#### Key points
- **Choose an opinion that is:**
  - **Genuinely contrarian** (not "testing is good" or "accessibility matters"),
  - **Defensible with experience** (you've seen it play out),
  - **Not offensive or reckless** (avoid "tests are a waste of time" or "accessibility doesn't matter"),
  - **Relevant to frontend engineering.**
- **Example opinions that work well in frontend:**
  - "Most React apps don't need a global state library; server-state caching plus local state covers almost everything."
  - "End-to-end tests give better value than high unit test coverage for most product UIs."
  - "Single-page apps were overused; many products should be server-rendered with small islands of interactivity."
  - "Design systems should start small and be extracted from real product code, not designed up front."
  - "Micro-frontends are an organisational tool and usually a mistake below about 50 frontend engineers."
  - "Utility-first CSS scales better than CSS-in-JS for large teams." (Or the opposite, if that's your view.)
  - "Code coverage targets make tests worse."
  - "Most memoisation in React is noise, and the compiler should handle it."
- **Structure:** state the opinion clearly → why most people disagree → your reasoning and evidence → where you'd be wrong → how it affects your work.
- **The "where I'd be wrong" part is what separates good answers from great ones.**

#### Model answer
*Example answer (adapt to your own opinions and experience):*

"One opinion I hold strongly is that most React apps don't need a global state library like Redux, and that adding one by default makes apps harder to maintain.

Most people disagree, or at least most codebases I've joined do. Redux or a similar store is often set up on day one because it feels like the 'proper' architecture, and the common argument is that you'll need it eventually.

My reasoning comes from two projects. At my last company, we audited our Redux store and found that about 80% of it was server data: lists, user profiles, settings, each with hand-written loading flags, error handling and cache invalidation. That's the hard part of state management, and Redux doesn't solve it; it just gives you a place to put it. We moved server data to TanStack Query and were left with a handful of truly global client values: the current theme, a feature flag context, and the state of an open dialog. Those fit in React context or a small store. We deleted about 6,000 lines of reducers and selectors, and bugs related to stale data dropped noticeably because the caching was now consistent.

On the second project, I started without a global store and we never needed one. We used URL state for filters, local state for forms, and server-state caching for everything else.

Where I'd be wrong: apps with complex client-side state that isn't from a server. A design tool, a spreadsheet, an offline-first app, or a collaborative editor genuinely has a large, shared, frequently changing client model, and there a dedicated store, or something like signals or a CRDT library, is the right call. And on very large teams, a strict pattern like Redux can help consistency even if it's more code.

So in practice, I start with server cache, URL and local state, and add a store only when I can point to the client state that needs it."

#### Another worked example
*Example answer (adapt to your own opinions and experience): on testing:*

"I believe code coverage targets make test suites worse. Many teams require 80% or 90% coverage, so engineers write tests that check implementation details, like whether a function was called, just to hit the number. Those tests break on every refactor and catch few real bugs. On one team, we had 88% coverage and still shipped a broken checkout, because no test covered the actual user journey. We changed approach: a small number of end-to-end tests on critical journeys, component tests written from the user's perspective with Testing Library, and unit tests for pure logic. Coverage dropped to around 65%, but escaped bugs fell, and refactoring became easier. Where I'd be wrong: libraries and complex pure logic, such as pricing calculations, where very high unit coverage is appropriate and cheap."

#### A third worked example
**How to present an opinion that's become mainstream:** some views that were contrarian a few years ago, such as "server rendering is coming back" or "TypeScript everywhere", are now common. If yours has become mainstream, say so: "I argued for this in 2021 when it was unpopular; here's what I learned from being early." That shows judgement over time. Better still, have one opinion that is still contrarian today.

#### The 30-second version
"I think most React apps don't need a global state library. When we audited our Redux store, 80% of it was server data, which a server-state cache handles far better. We moved to TanStack Query, deleted 6,000 lines and reduced stale-data bugs. I'd be wrong for apps with large client-side models, like design tools or offline-first apps, where a dedicated store is the right call."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **State the opinion** in one clear sentence.
2. **Explain the common view** fairly and why people hold it.
3. **Give your reasoning** from first principles.
4. **Back it with evidence** from real projects and numbers.
5. **Say where you'd be wrong** and the strongest counter-argument.
6. **Explain how it shapes your work** in practice.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Tabs are better than spaces" | A substantive architectural or practice opinion |
| Contrarian for effect | Based on experience and evidence |
| Dismisses the other side | Explains the common view fairly |
| Absolute ("always", "never") | Clear conditions where it's wrong |
| No practical consequence | Shows how it changes decisions |

#### Useful phrases
- "One opinion I hold strongly is…"
- "Most people disagree because… and that's a fair point."
- "My view comes from…"
- "Where I'd be wrong is…"
- "In practice, that means I…"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Picking a safe, non-controversial opinion.**
- **Picking something reckless** that signals poor judgement.
- **Arrogance:** implying others are foolish.
- **No evidence:** just preference.
- **Refusing to concede** any counter-argument when challenged.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What would change your mind?"** — "If I saw a team with mostly client-side state where context became a performance problem, or if a store gave clear consistency benefits across many teams."
- **"How would you handle a team that strongly disagrees?"** — "Share evidence, try it on one feature, measure, and accept the team's decision if it doesn't hold up."
- **"Have you ever changed a strongly held opinion?"** — "Yes: I used to dislike utility-first CSS, but after using it on a large team, I saw how much it reduced dead CSS and naming arguments."
- **"Isn't that just the current trend?"** — Acknowledge it, and show your reasoning is from experience, not following trends.
- **The interviewer argues the other side.** — Engage respectfully, concede good points, and refine your position rather than defending it blindly.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **First-principles reasoning.**
- **Evidence** from real work.
- **Nuance** and known limits.
- **Openness** to changing your mind.

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** write down three technical opinions you hold that your colleagues might not share. For each, add one project that supports it and one situation where it would be wrong. Pick the strongest.
- Ask a colleague to argue the other side, and practise conceding good points.
- Checklist: clear opinion · fair common view · reasoning · evidence · where wrong · practical impact.

- **Mismatch** with the role (wanting to leave engineering, or wanting a different discipline).
- **Too vague** or too detailed.
- **Sounding like you'll leave soon.**
- **Arrogance:** "I'll be running this team."

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What do you need to develop to get there?"** — "Cross-team influence: getting agreement across teams with different priorities, and writing clear technical strategy."
- **"How will you know if you're making progress?"** — "Scope of problems I'm trusted with, teams adopting things I've built, and feedback from my manager against the career framework."
- **"What if there's no staff opening?"** — "I'd focus on the scope rather than the title: the impact usually comes first and the title follows."
- **"Why not management?"** — "I enjoy mentoring, but I get the most energy from technical problems. I'd revisit it if I found people leadership was where I had most impact."
- **"How does this role help?"** — Name specific parts of the role that build the skills you mentioned.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Clear direction** linked to impact.
- **Self-awareness** about gaps.
- **Alignment** with the company's needs and paths.
- **Commitment** to growing here.

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** write down the impact you'd like to have in three years, two skills to develop, and whether you're leaning IC or management. Check the company's career pages or ask the recruiter about paths.
- Practise a 45-second version.
- Checklist: headline impact · specifics · track · link to company · success measure.

#### Variations you might be asked
- "What are your career goals?"
- "What's your next career step?"
- "Do you want to go into management?"

## What's your salary expectation?
- id: dd2a1433-2576-43b0-ad7a-d222be21ab81
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, negotiation, compensation

### Prompt
"What's your salary expectation?" This is a negotiation moment, and it often comes early, sometimes in the first recruiter call. The usual advice is to anchor high and support your number with research.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Budget fit:** recruiters need to know whether you're in range before investing in a full interview loop.
- **Preparation:** do you know your market value, or are you guessing?
- **Professionalism:** can you discuss money calmly and confidently?
- **Negotiation position:** whatever you say first often becomes the anchor for the offer.
- **Seniority signal:** your expectation tells them what level you see yourself at.

#### Key points
- **Research first.** Use sources such as Levels.fyi, Glassdoor, salary surveys, recruiter conversations, published pay ranges in job ads (required by law in some places), and friends at similar companies. Know the range for the **level**, **location** and **company type**.
- **Think in total compensation:** base salary, bonus, equity (and its realistic value, especially at startups), pension or retirement contributions, sign-on bonus, benefits, and remote work.
- **Options for answering, from most to least deferring:**
  1. **Ask for their range first:** "Could you share the range budgeted for this role?" Recruiters often will, and it's increasingly common or required.
  2. **Defer politely:** "I'd like to understand the role and level better first; I'm sure we can find a number that works if it's the right fit."
  3. **Give a researched range:** with your target at or near the bottom of it, so even the low end is acceptable to you.
- **When you give a number, anchor high but credible**, and justify it with market data and the level.
- **Don't reveal your current salary** if you don't have to; in some places it's illegal for employers to ask. Focus on expectations instead.
- **Level matters more than a few thousand:** being levelled correctly (senior versus staff) affects pay far more than negotiating within a band.
- **Final negotiation happens at the offer**, ideally with competing offers or a clear alternative. Early on, you're just making sure you're in range.
- **Stay warm and collaborative:** the person you negotiate with may be your future colleague.

#### Model answer
*Example answer (adapt to your market and level), in an early recruiter call:*

**Recruiter:** "What are your salary expectations for this role?"

**Candidate:** "Thanks for asking early, that's helpful. Before I give a number, could you share the range you've budgeted for the role? I want to make sure we're aligned on the level as well."

**Recruiter:** "We'd rather hear your expectations first."

**Candidate:** "Of course. Based on my research for senior frontend roles at AI companies in this location, including Levels.fyi data and a couple of recent conversations, I'm looking for total compensation in the range of 140 to 160 thousand, with base around 120 to 130 and the rest in equity and bonus. That reflects eight years' experience and leading frontend architecture in my current role. I'm flexible on how it's split, and I'm more focused on the role and the team being right, but that's the range I'm targeting."

**Recruiter:** "That's at the top of our band."

**Candidate:** "That's useful to know. Could you tell me more about how levels work here? If the scope of the role is closer to staff, the top of the band may make sense. And I'd be happy to talk about the overall package, such as equity or a sign-on bonus, once we get to an offer."

(Numbers are illustrative; use real data for your market.)

#### Another worked example
*Example answer (adapt to your situation), when you'd rather not give a number yet:*

"I'm still learning about the role and its scope, so I don't want to give a number that's either unrealistic or that undersells it. I'm confident that if we both agree I'm the right person, we'll find something that works. Could you share the band for the role? That will tell me quickly whether we're in the same area."

And if pressed on current salary: "I'd prefer to focus on what's right for this role, which is different in scope from my current one. Based on market data for this level, I'm targeting around…"

#### A third worked example
**At the offer stage:**
"Thank you, I'm really excited about this offer and the team. Based on my research, and on another offer I'm considering, I was hoping for a base nearer 130. If you can get there, I'd be happy to accept this week." Specific, positive, with a reason, and a clear commitment if they meet it. Ask about every part of the package: base, equity amount and vesting, sign-on bonus, level, start date and remote flexibility. If base is fixed, other parts often aren't.

#### The 30-second version
"Ask for their range first. If they insist, give a researched total-compensation range with your target near the bottom, justified by market data and your level. Don't share your current salary if you can avoid it. Stay flexible on how it's split, focus on getting the level right, and save hard negotiation for the offer, ideally with alternatives."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Research** the range by level, location and company type.
2. **Decide** your target and your walk-away number.
3. **Ask for their range** first.
4. **If needed, give a range** with your target near the bottom.
5. **Justify** with data and level.
6. **Clarify the level** if the numbers don't match.
7. **Negotiate the full package** at the offer stage.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Whatever you think is fair" | A researched, justified range |
| States current salary immediately | Focuses on expectations for this role |
| Lowballs to seem easy to hire | Anchors high but credible |
| Only base salary | Total compensation, flexible on split |
| Confrontational | Warm, collaborative, confident |
| Ignores level | Checks levelling first |

#### Useful phrases
- "Could you share the range budgeted for this role?"
- "Based on market data for this level and location…"
- "I'm looking for total compensation in the range of…"
- "I'm flexible on the split between base, equity and bonus."
- "If you can get to…, I'd be happy to accept."

#### Common pitfalls
- **No research.**
- **Giving a range whose bottom you'd be unhappy with.** They'll offer the bottom.
- **Anchoring to your current, lower salary.**
- **Overvaluing startup equity** without understanding its terms.
- **Negotiating aggressively too early.**
- **Accepting immediately** without considering the full package.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What's your current salary?"** — "I'd prefer to focus on what's right for this role. My target is based on the market for this level."
- **"Would you accept less?"** — "I'd consider the full package and the level. What flexibility is there on equity or a sign-on bonus?"
- **"Do you have other offers?"** — Be honest: "I'm in later stages with a couple of companies." Never invent offers.
- **"Our band tops out below that."** — "Thanks for being open. Could we discuss whether the role could be levelled higher, or other parts of the package?"
- **"When could you start?"** — Give a realistic date, including your notice period.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Preparation** and market awareness.
- **Calm, professional** handling of money.
- **Understanding of levelling** and total compensation.

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** research the range for your target level and location, and write down your target, your ideal and your walk-away number. Prepare one sentence justifying your range.
- Role-play the recruiter call with a friend, including being pushed for your current salary.
- Checklist: research · target and walk-away · ask first · range · justification · level · full package.

#### Variations you might be asked
- "What are you earning now?"
- "What would it take for you to accept an offer?"
- "Is this range acceptable to you?"

## Why should we hire you over another senior frontend engineer?
- id: dbd0c7dd-a3e3-4080-b5d7-43e82fed864e
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: hard
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, differentiation, pitch

### Prompt
"Why should we hire you over another senior frontend engineer?" This is the hardest version of "why you". Keep to three differentiators at most.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Self-awareness:** do you know what genuinely makes you different, not just "I'm hard-working"?
- **Understanding of their needs:** have you listened during the process and understood what this team actually needs?
- **Evidence:** can you back each claim with a concrete result?
- **Confidence without arrogance:** can you sell yourself clearly without putting others down?
- **Closing ability:** this is often near the end; a strong answer leaves a clear, memorable impression.

#### Key points
- **Most senior frontend engineers know React, TypeScript, testing and performance basics.** Those are the minimum, not differentiators. A differentiator is a combination of skills, experience or perspective that's rarer and matters to *this* team.
- **Good sources of differentiation:**
  - **A domain:** AI interfaces, payments, data visualisation, real-time collaboration, accessibility in regulated sectors.
  - **A rare combination:** strong frontend plus backend or data knowledge; engineering plus design skills; technical depth plus product thinking.
  - **Proven outcomes at their stage:** "I've taken a frontend from 5 to 30 engineers", "I've led a migration like the one you're planning".
  - **Leadership style:** mentoring, building consensus, raising the quality bar.
- **Map each differentiator to something they told you** during interviews: their challenges, roadmap or team gaps.
- **Three differentiators at most.** Each gets one sentence of claim and one of evidence.
- **Don't criticise other candidates.** You don't know them; focus on yourself.
- **End with a confident close** that links to the role.

#### Model answer
*Example answer (adapt to your own strengths), for a senior frontend role at an AI company planning a big migration:*

"I can't speak for the other candidates, but I can tell you the three things I'd bring that I think fit what you've told me you need.

First, I've built AI interfaces in production, not just standard product UI. I led a natural-language query feature: streaming the model's interpretation, letting users correct it, and building an evaluation set that took accuracy from 71% to 92%. You mentioned that making model output feel trustworthy is your biggest product challenge, and that's exactly the problem I've worked on.

Second, I've led a large incremental migration before. Your team told me you're moving off an older framework while still shipping features. I led a 14-month migration from AngularJS to React, route by route, with no feature freeze, and the first migrated page was 35% faster. I know where those migrations go wrong, especially around shared state and keeping the two apps visually consistent.

Third, I raise the level of the people around me. I mentored a junior engineer to independence and promotion, and I run a frontend guild. You said you're planning to double the team this year, and you'll need people who can onboard and grow others while still shipping.

So it's that combination: production AI interface experience, a migration I've already led, and helping a growing team scale. I think that's a rare mix for exactly the stage you're at."

#### Another worked example
*Example answer (adapt to your own strengths), for a product-focused fintech role:*

"Three things. First, I think like a product engineer: on my last team I worked directly with product and data, ran checkout experiments, and one change I proposed raised completion by 3%. Second, I have deep accessibility experience in a regulated setting: I led WCAG AA work that passed a formal audit first time, and you mentioned accessibility compliance is a priority this year. Third, I'm comfortable across the stack: I've built backend-for-frontend services in Node and understand payments flows, which means fewer handoffs on your small team. Together, that means I can take a feature from idea to measurable result with less coordination."

#### A third worked example
**If you don't feel you have a rare specialism**, combine smaller strengths:
- "Strong fundamentals plus very fast onboarding: at my last two companies I shipped production code in the first week and led a feature in the first month."
- "Breadth across your exact stack: Next.js, GraphQL and your testing tools, so there's no ramp-up."
- "Ownership: I own outcomes, not tickets. For example, I monitored the metrics after launch and fixed a drop in mobile conversion that nobody else had noticed."
A clear, evidenced combination still stands out.

#### The 30-second version
"Three things that match what you need: I've built production AI interfaces, including streaming, user correction and evals; I've led an incremental migration like the one you're planning, with no feature freeze; and I help teams scale through mentoring and guilds, which matters as you double the team. It's that combination that I think is rare for your stage."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **During the interviews, note** their challenges, roadmap and team gaps.
2. **List your strengths** and evidence for each.
3. **Pick the three** that match their needs most closely.
4. **For each, give one claim, one piece of evidence, and one link to their need.**
5. **Close** with the combination and why it suits their stage.
6. **Don't** compare yourself negatively to others.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "I'm passionate and hard-working" | Specific, rare skills with evidence |
| Generic skills (React, TypeScript) | Domain expertise and proven outcomes |
| Ten reasons | Three differentiators |
| Ignores what they said they need | Links each point to their challenges |
| "I'm better than other candidates" | Focuses on own strengths respectfully |
| Trails off | Clear, confident close |

#### Useful phrases
- "I can't speak for the other candidates, but here's what I'd bring."
- "You mentioned…, and that's exactly what I've…"
- "The evidence for that is…"
- "It's the combination that I think is rare for your stage."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Clichés:** "team player", "fast learner", "passionate".
- **Listing standard skills** as differentiators.
- **Too many points,** which dilutes the message.
- **No evidence.**
- **Arrogance** or criticising others.
- **Not using what you learned in the interviews.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What's your biggest weakness compared with other candidates?"** — "I've worked mostly in React, so if you hire for deep experience in another framework, others may have an edge. I've learned new frameworks quickly before, and the concepts transfer."
- **"What would your current team say is your superpower?"** — "Turning messy, ambiguous problems into clear plans. People bring me in when something's stuck."
- **"What will you deliver in your first 90 days?"** — Give a brief plan: learn the codebase and users, ship something small early, then take ownership of one priority area.
- **"Why haven't you reached staff yet?"** — Answer honestly and constructively, focusing on what you're doing to grow.
- **"What if we need someone more hands-on than a leader?"** — "I'm hands-on: most of my week is coding. The leadership is how I make the team faster, not instead of coding."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Clear, evidenced differentiation.**
- **Listening:** links to what they said they need.
- **Confidence** with humility.
- **A memorable close.**

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** list your five strongest skills or experiences with evidence. For each target company, pick the three that best match what they said they need, and write one sentence of claim and one of evidence for each.
- Practise the 30-second version as a closing statement.
- Checklist: three differentiators · evidence · links to their needs · respectful · confident close.

#### Variations you might be asked
- "What makes you unique?"
- "What would you bring to the team that others wouldn't?"
- "Sell yourself in one minute."

## What questions do you have for me?
- id: 61243a8a-b145-4f6e-bbe0-966ae1e0c704
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, questions, preparation

### Prompt
"What questions do you have for me?" This is asked at the end of almost every interview. Always have five ready. Poor questions can lose you an offer; great ones can help win it.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Genuine interest:** do you care enough about the role to be curious about it?
- **Preparation:** have you researched the company and listened during the interview?
- **Seniority:** senior candidates ask about strategy, decisions, team health and impact, not only about benefits.
- **Priorities:** your questions reveal what you value.
- **Your judgement of them:** good candidates are assessing the company too. Interviewers respect that.

#### Key points
- **Prepare five or more questions, tailored to each interviewer's role:**
  - **Engineers:** how work actually gets done, the codebase, tooling, quality, on-call.
  - **Engineering managers:** team goals, how success is measured, growth, team challenges.
  - **Product managers or designers:** how engineering and product collaborate, how decisions get made.
  - **Senior leaders:** strategy, priorities, risks, culture.
- **Strong question categories for frontend roles:**
  - **The work:** "What's the biggest frontend challenge the team will face in the next six months?"
  - **Success:** "What would make someone successful in this role in the first 90 days?"
  - **Technical health:** "How do you balance product work with technical debt? Can you give a recent example?"
  - **Ways of working:** "How does a feature go from idea to production here? Where does frontend come in?"
  - **Team and culture:** "What's something the team has changed recently based on feedback?"
  - **Growth:** "What does progression look like for frontend engineers here? Can you give an example of someone who grew?"
  - **The interviewer:** "What's kept you here?" or "What surprised you when you joined?"
  - **Concerns:** "Is there anything in my background that makes you hesitant? I'd like the chance to address it."
- **Use what you heard:** "You mentioned the migration earlier; how are you deciding what to migrate first?" This shows listening.
- **Avoid early in the process:** salary, holidays and benefits (save them for the recruiter or the offer), questions answered on the website, and yes/no questions.
- **Never say "No, I think you've covered everything."**

#### Model answer
*Example questions (adapt to the company and interviewer), for a senior frontend role, asked to the hiring manager:*

"Yes, I have a few.

**One:** You mentioned the team is moving parts of the app to server rendering. What's driving that, and what's been hardest so far? I'm curious whether it's mostly performance and SEO or developer experience.

**Two:** What would a great first 90 days look like for this role? What would make you say six months from now, 'that was the right hire'?

**Three:** How does the team balance roadmap work with technical debt? Is there a recent example where you chose to invest in the platform over a feature?

**Four:** How do frontend engineers work with design? Do engineers get involved early in shaping features, or mostly after designs are finished?

**Five:** What's the biggest challenge the team faces right now that isn't technical?

And finally, is there anything from today that makes you unsure about my fit? I'd welcome the chance to address it."

Use two or three depending on time, and listen properly to the answers; follow up on one.

#### Another worked example
*Example questions (adapt), for different interviewers in the same loop:*
- **To a frontend engineer:** "What does your day-to-day look like? What's the best and most frustrating part of the codebase? How long does it take for a change to reach production?"
- **To a designer:** "How do design and engineering resolve disagreements about feasibility? How is the design system maintained?"
- **To a product manager:** "How are priorities set for the quarter? How do you measure whether a feature succeeded?"
- **To a director or VP:** "What are the biggest risks to the company's plan this year? How does frontend contribute to the company's strategy?"
- **At an AI company:** "How do you evaluate changes to model behaviour in the product? How do frontend engineers work with research or model teams?"

#### A third worked example
**Questions that reveal red flags (asked politely):**
- "How often are priorities changed mid-sprint?" (chaos)
- "When was the last time someone was paged out of hours, and what happened next?" (on-call health)
- "What happened to the last person in this role?" (turnover)
- "How are disagreements between engineering and product resolved?" (power balance)
- "How much of your time goes on meetings versus building?" (focus)
The answers, and how comfortable the interviewer is giving them, tell you a lot.

#### The 30-second version
"Always have five tailored questions. Ask about the biggest upcoming challenge, what success looks like in 90 days, how they balance tech debt, how frontend works with design and product, and the interviewer's own experience. Follow up on something they said earlier, and finish by asking if they have any concerns about your fit. Save salary and benefits for the recruiter."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Before the interview:** research and write five or more questions.
2. **Tailor** them to each interviewer's role.
3. **During the interview:** note things to follow up on.
4. **Ask two or three**, depending on time.
5. **Listen** and ask one follow-up.
6. **Close** by asking about concerns and next steps.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "No, you've covered everything" | Five prepared, tailored questions |
| "How many holidays do I get?" | Questions about challenges and success |
| Answered on the website | Builds on what the interviewer said |
| Yes/no questions | Open questions that invite stories |
| Same questions for everyone | Tailored to engineer, PM, manager, leader |
| Doesn't listen to answers | Follows up thoughtfully |

#### Useful phrases
- "You mentioned earlier…, could you tell me more about…?"
- "What would make you say, six months from now, that this was the right hire?"
- "What's something the team has changed recently based on feedback?"
- "Is there anything that makes you hesitant about my fit?"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Having no questions.**
- **Only asking about benefits** early in the process.
- **Asking things that are easy to look up.**
- **Asking too many** and running over time.
- **Asking the same question to everyone** in a loop (sometimes fine for comparing answers, but vary most of them).
- **Not listening** to the answers.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **The interviewer answers your concerns question with a real concern.** — Thank them and address it calmly: "That's fair. On the backend side, I've built two Node services in production; here's one example…"
- **"Do you have any other questions?"** — Ask about next steps and timeline.
- **Running short on time.** — "I have more, but I'll pick the one that matters most to me…"
- **"Why do you ask?"** — Explain your reasoning: "I've seen teams where tech debt never gets time, and I want to know how you handle it."
- **The interviewer is vague.** — Gently ask for an example.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Strategic curiosity** about challenges and impact.
- **Listening:** questions that build on the conversation.
- **Assessing fit** in both directions.
- **Respect for time.**

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** for your next interview, write five questions: one about challenges, one about success, one about technical health, one about ways of working, and one for the interviewer personally. Add a red-flag question you care about.
- After each interview, note which questions got the most useful answers.
- Checklist: five ready · tailored · build on the conversation · open questions · concerns question · next steps.

#### Variations you might be asked
- "Is there anything else you'd like to know?"
- "Anything you'd like to ask about the team?"
- "Any questions about the next steps?"

## Tell me about a time you failed
- id: 60767836-bd1c-4c68-9a4e-af928453f907
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, self-awareness, growth

### Prompt
"Tell me about a time you failed." This probes for self-awareness. Answers like "I work too hard" or "I care too much" disqualify you.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Self-awareness:** can you recognise and admit a real mistake, without excuses or blaming others?
- **Ownership:** did you take responsibility for your part, even if others contributed?
- **Response under pressure:** what you did when it went wrong: communication, containment, recovery.
- **Learning:** what changed afterwards in how you work, and the evidence that the change stuck.
- **Psychological safety:** people who can talk openly about failure tend to build teams that learn rather than hide problems.

#### Key points
- **Choose a real failure with real consequences,** but not a catastrophic one that suggests poor judgement in the role you're applying for (for example, a security breach you caused by ignoring warnings).
- **Good frontend failure types:**
  - A **production incident** you caused (a broken release, a performance regression, a bad cache setting).
  - A **project that missed its goal** (late, abandoned, or launched with poor adoption).
  - A **wrong technical decision** (a library or architecture that had to be reversed).
  - A **people or communication failure** (not raising a risk early, a strained relationship, not delegating).
- **Structure:** what happened → your part in it (own it clearly) → what you did immediately → the root cause → what you changed → evidence the change worked.
- **Spend about a third on the failure and two thirds on the response and learning.**
- **Use "I" for your mistakes.** Mention others' roles only for accuracy, never to shift blame.
- **Show systemic learning:** not just "I'll be more careful", but a change in process, tooling or habit that prevents the whole class of problem.

#### Model answer
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): a release that broke checkout:*

"**Situation.** At an e-commerce company, I led a refactor of our checkout's payment step to use a new form library. It was a large change that touched validation for every payment method.

**What went wrong.** I tested it thoroughly on desktop Chrome and Firefox, and our unit tests passed. We released on a Thursday afternoon. Within an hour, conversion on iOS dropped by about 30%. On Safari, a change in how the new library handled autofill meant the card number field appeared filled but its value was empty, so validation blocked submission and users saw no error.

My mistake was clear: I'd released a high-risk change to the most important page without testing on Safari, without a feature flag, and late in the week. I'd been under deadline pressure and told myself the unit tests were enough.

**What I did immediately.** As soon as the alert fired, I told the team in the incident channel that it was likely my release, and we rolled it back within 25 minutes. I wrote to the product manager with the estimated impact, which was roughly 40,000 in lost orders, and led the post-mortem the next day.

**Root cause and changes.** In the post-mortem, which was blameless, we identified three gaps, and I took on fixing them. First, I added Safari and mobile Safari to our end-to-end test runs using a cloud device service, with a specific autofill test. Second, I proposed that any change to checkout must ship behind a feature flag with a gradual rollout, which became a team rule. Third, we agreed no high-risk releases on Thursday afternoons or Fridays.

**Result.** I re-released the refactor two weeks later behind a flag, rolling out to 5%, then 25%, then 100%, with conversion monitored by browser. It went smoothly. Over the following year, the flag-and-rollout rule caught two other problems at the 5% stage before they affected most users. Personally, I learned that deadline pressure is exactly when I need to slow down on risky changes, and I now write down the risk level of a change before I start it."

#### Another worked example
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): a project that failed through communication:*

"**Situation:** I was asked to build a shared component library for three product teams. **What went wrong:** I spent two months building what I thought was a great library, with excellent documentation, but I'd only spoken to one team at the start. When I launched it, the other two teams didn't adopt it: their designs used different patterns, and their apps used a different styling approach. Adoption after three months was about 10%. **My part:** I'd treated it as an engineering project, not a product with users. **What I changed:** I ran interviews with each team, set up a small group with one engineer and one designer from each, and we rebuilt the roadmap around the five components all teams needed most. I shipped them in small releases with each team's feedback. **Result:** adoption reached about 70% over the next two quarters. Now, before building anything shared, I talk to every intended user first and agree what success means."

#### A third worked example
**Smaller failures that still work, if you're earlier in your career:**
- Underestimating a task badly and not telling anyone until the deadline. Learning: raise risks early and break work down.
- Shipping an inaccessible component that a screen reader user reported. Learning: added automated accessibility checks and manual screen reader testing to the definition of done.
- Over-engineering a simple feature with an abstraction nobody needed. Learning: build the simple version first.
The size of the failure matters less than the depth of ownership and learning.

#### The 30-second version
"I released a checkout refactor on a Thursday afternoon without Safari testing or a feature flag. Autofill broke on iOS and conversion dropped 30%. ## How do you handle ambiguity?
- id: 745ef26e-d8ea-4f24-a216-aaa7f9763e48
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, leadership, tooling

### Prompt
"How do you handle ambiguity?" This is a filter for senior and above. Junior engineers want clear specs; senior engineers create clarity.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Creating clarity:** can you turn a vague goal into a concrete plan, rather than waiting for someone to hand you a spec?
- **Comfort with uncertainty:** do you make progress without having all the answers?
- **Judgement:** do you know which unknowns matter most, and which you can decide on and revisit?
- **Communication:** do you share assumptions and get agreement, so others aren't surprised?
- **Bias to action with safety:** small experiments and reversible steps rather than paralysis or reckless guessing.

#### Key points
- **A repeatable approach:**
  1. **Clarify the goal:** who's the user, what problem are we solving, what does success look like, and what are the constraints (time, budget, compliance)?
  2. **List the unknowns** and sort them by impact and how easy they are to answer.
  3. **Reduce the biggest unknowns first:** talk to users or stakeholders, look at data, build a quick prototype or spike.
  4. **Write down your assumptions** and share them: "Unless I hear otherwise, I'm assuming…"
  5. **Make reversible decisions quickly**, and invest more evidence in irreversible ones.
  6. **Deliver in small steps** to learn early.
  7. **Revisit** as new information arrives.
- **Frontend examples of ambiguity:** a vague feature request ("make the dashboard better"), designs that cover only the happy path, unclear performance or browser support requirements, a new AI feature where nobody knows what good looks like yet, inheriting a codebase with no documentation.
- **Show it as something you enjoy,** or at least handle calmly, not something you complain about.
- **Distinguish ambiguity from chaos:** if priorities change daily without reason, that's a process problem to raise, not just to absorb.

#### Model answer
*Example answer with a STAR story (adapt to your own experience):*

"I try to turn ambiguity into a short list of assumptions and questions, then reduce the biggest ones quickly with small experiments, and share what I'm assuming so nobody is surprised.

A good example: our head of product asked me to 'add AI to the support centre' within a quarter. That was the whole brief: no designs, no defined user problem, and no agreement on what success meant.

First, I clarified the goal. I spent a few days with the support team and the data. Forty per cent of tickets were questions already answered in our help articles, and users weren't finding them. So we framed the goal as: reduce tickets that our existing help content could answer, without making it harder to reach a human.

Then I listed the unknowns. Would answers be accurate enough? Would users trust them? What would it cost per question? How would it affect our support metrics? The biggest risk was accuracy, so I tackled that first: in one week, I built a rough prototype that answered questions using our help articles, with links to sources, and tested it against 100 real past tickets. About 80% of the answers were good enough, which told us the idea was viable.

I wrote a one-page document with our goal, assumptions, risks and a phased plan, and shared it with product, support and legal. It included assumptions like 'we always show a route to a human' and 'answers always cite the article they came from'. Legal raised a concern about billing questions, so we excluded those from the first version, which was exactly the kind of thing I wanted to find early.

We launched to 10% of users with an 'Was this helpful?' button and a clear 'Contact support' option, and improved weekly from feedback.

The result: tickets for questions covered by help content fell by about 25% in the first two months, with satisfaction unchanged. What I took from it: when the brief is vague, the first deliverable is not code; it's a clear problem statement and the riskiest assumption tested."

#### Another worked example
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): inheriting an undocumented codebase:*

"**Situation:** I joined a team whose only frontend engineer had just left, leaving a large app with no documentation, few tests, and a list of urgent bugs. **Action:** I didn't try to understand everything. I listed the most important user journeys with the product manager, added end-to-end tests for the top five as a safety net, and documented each area as I worked in it. For decisions I wasn't sure about, I wrote short notes explaining what I'd decided and why, so we could revisit them. I shared a weekly update on what I'd learned, what I'd assumed, and what was risky. **Result:** within six weeks, the urgent bugs were fixed, the main journeys had tests, and the documentation helped the next two engineers onboard in days instead of weeks."

#### A third worked example
**Where to stop and ask instead:**
- When a wrong assumption would be **expensive to reverse** (a data model, a public API, a legal or security matter).
- When there are **conflicting goals** from different stakeholders that you can't resolve yourself.
- When ambiguity is actually a **missing decision** by someone accountable: name the decision and who should make it, and propose a default with a deadline. "If I don't hear by Friday, I'll go with option A."

#### The 30-second version
"I turn ambiguity into a clear goal, a list of unknowns and stated assumptions, then test the riskiest one quickly. When I was asked to 'add AI to the support centre', I found that 40% of tickets were already answered in help articles, built a prototype in a week and tested it on 100 real tickets, wrote a one-page plan, and launched to 10% of users. Tickets in that category fell by 25%."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Clarify** the goal, users, success and constraints.
2. **List unknowns** and rank by impact.
3. **Test** the riskiest one fast: prototype, data, conversation.
4. **Write down assumptions** and share them.
5. **Decide fast** on reversible choices; invest in irreversible ones.
6. **Deliver in small steps** and learn.
7. **Revisit** as you learn more.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "I ask for clearer requirements" | Creates clarity themselves |
| Waits for certainty | Tests the riskiest assumption quickly |
| Silent assumptions | Writes down and shares assumptions |
| Builds a big solution first | Prototype, then phased rollout |
| Complains about vague briefs | Treats framing as part of the job |

#### Useful phrases
- "The first deliverable is a clear problem statement."
- "The riskiest unknown was…, so I tested it first."
- "Unless I hear otherwise, I'm assuming…"

- **Charging ahead** without checking assumptions.
- **Analysis paralysis.**
- **Not communicating** what you've assumed.
- **Generic answers** with no example.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)

- **"How do you know when you have enough information?"** — "When the remaining unknowns are cheap to fix later, or when waiting costs more than being wrong."

- **Fast learning** through experiments.
- **Clear communication** of assumptions.
- **Calm judgement** about risk.

#### Practise it

- Practise the one-sentence summary of your approach.
- Checklist: clarify goal · unknowns · riskiest first · assumptions shared · reversible versus irreversible · small steps · result.

## How do you give feedback to a peer whose code you think is weak?
- id: 38903dc8-4f27-4363-8b38-b608c9ce652e
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, collaboration, code-review

### Prompt
"How do you give feedback to a peer whose code you think is weak?" This probes your code review style and how you contribute to the health of a team.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Code review style:** do you improve code while keeping people motivated, or do you nitpick, gatekeep, or rubber-stamp?
- **Empathy:** do you consider why the code is weak (time pressure, unfamiliarity, unclear requirements) before judging?
- **Directness:** are you willing to raise real problems, or do you avoid conflict and approve anything?
- **Prioritisation:** can you tell critical issues from preferences?
- **Systemic thinking:** if one person's code is often weak, do you address the cause (pairing, standards, tooling), not just each pull request?

#### Key points
- **Start with curiosity, not judgement.** "Weak" often has a reason: a deadline, an unfamiliar area, a misunderstanding of the requirements, or a different but valid approach.
- **Separate the code from the person:** comment on the code and its effects ("this re-renders the whole list on each keystroke"), never on the person ("you always…").
- **Prioritise your comments:**
  - **Blocking:** bugs, security problems, accessibility failures, data loss, serious performance issues.
  - **Should fix:** maintainability, missing tests, confusing naming.
  - **Optional or "nit":** style preferences. Label them clearly, and leave most to linters and formatters.
- **Explain why** and suggest a direction or example; link to docs or an existing pattern.
- **Ask questions** when you're not sure: "What happens if the request fails here?"
- **Switch channel for big issues:** if the approach is fundamentally wrong, talk in person or on a call instead of leaving 30 comments. Better still, discuss the approach before code is written.
- **Recognise what's good,** sincerely and specifically.
- **For repeated issues,** offer pairing, write down team standards, or add lint rules, and if needed, talk privately with the person.
- **Be open to being wrong:** they may know something you don't.

#### Model answer
*Example answer with a STAR story (adapt to your own experience):*

"My starting point is to assume there's a reason the code looks the way it does, and to make my feedback about the code and its effects, prioritised, with the reasoning attached.

For example, a peer on my team, an experienced engineer who'd recently moved from backend, opened a pull request for a filterable data table. It worked, but there were real problems: every filter change refetched all data and re-rendered the entire 2,000-row table, the filter inputs had no labels for screen readers, and the state logic was spread across several effects that were hard to follow.

Rather than leaving twenty comments, I did three things.

First, I separated what mattered. There were three blocking issues: the missing labels, which were an accessibility failure; a race condition where a slow earlier request could overwrite a newer result; and the re-rendering, which made typing visibly laggy. Everything else, like naming and some structure, I marked as optional or left out.

Second, because the issues were about the overall approach, I messaged them: 'This is really close. I have a few thoughts on the data flow; would a 20-minute call be easier than comments?' On the call, I asked about their thinking first. It turned out they'd modelled the state like a backend service and weren't familiar with our query library's caching. I showed them how our existing list page handled filters with query keys, which also solved the race condition, and suggested virtualising the table.

Third, I followed up in writing on the pull request with a short summary, so the reasoning was recorded, and I mentioned the good parts too: their error handling was more thorough than ours elsewhere.

They updated the code the same day. Afterwards, I noticed two other engineers had made the same race-condition mistake, so I added a short section to our frontend guide on filters and data fetching, and suggested a lint rule for missing input labels. A few weeks later, the same engineer reviewed my code and caught an error-handling gap I'd missed. We had the kind of relationship where honest feedback went both ways."

#### Another worked example
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): repeated quality issues:*

"**Situation:** a teammate's pull requests repeatedly had the same problems: no tests and large, unfocused changes. Reviews were becoming tense, and others had started to avoid reviewing their work. **Action:** I asked for a private chat and shared specific examples, framed around impact: 'Large pull requests take us days to review, and bugs slip through.' I asked what made it hard. They said they felt pressure to ship features fast and saw tests as slowing them down. We agreed to try smaller pull requests and to pair on testing for one feature. I also proposed a team-wide pull request template with a testing checklist, so it wasn't singling them out. **Result:** their pull requests became smaller and reviews faster within a few weeks, and the template helped the whole team. If it hadn't improved, I would have raised it with our manager, but it was much better to try directly first."

#### A third worked example
**Example comments, weak versus strong:**
- Weak: "This is wrong." Strong: "This effect runs on every render because `options` is a new object each time, so we refetch on every keystroke. Could we memoise it, or pass the primitive values instead?"
- Weak: "Why didn't you use the hook?" Strong: "We have a `useDebouncedValue` hook in shared utils that does this; it might save some code. Non-blocking."
- Weak: "Bad naming." Strong: "nit: `data2` is a bit hard to follow; maybe `filteredRows`?"
- Weak: 30 comments on a fundamentally wrong approach. Strong: "I think the approach might need a rethink; can we chat for 15 minutes?"

#### The 30-second version
"I assume there's a reason behind the code, then prioritise: blocking issues like bugs, security and accessibility; should-fix maintainability points; and clearly labelled nits. I explain why and point to examples. If the problem is the overall approach, I switch to a call instead of many comments, ask about their thinking first, and summarise in writing afterwards. If the same issues keep recurring, I fix the system, with guides, templates or lint rules, and talk privately if needed."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Understand** the context and intent first.
2. **Prioritise** issues: blocking, should-fix, nit.
3. **Write specific, reasoned comments** with suggestions.
4. **Change channel** for big or sensitive issues.
5. **Acknowledge** what's good.
6. **Summarise** decisions in writing.
7. **Fix systemic causes:** guides, tooling, pairing.
8. **Escalate** only if direct attempts fail.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "I point out everything that's wrong" | Prioritised: blocking, should-fix, nit |
| Comments on the person | Comments on the code and its effects |
| 30 comments on a wrong approach | A short call, then a written summary |
| Approves to avoid conflict | Raises real problems directly |
| No reasoning | Explains why, with examples |
| Only criticism | Specific, sincere praise too |

#### Useful phrases
- "What was your thinking behind…?"
- "Blocking: this can lose data if…"
- "nit, non-blocking: …"
- "Would a quick call be easier than comments?"
- "I might be missing context here."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Nitpicking style** that a formatter should handle.
- **Harsh or sarcastic tone.**
- **Rubber-stamping** to avoid conflict.
- **Rewriting their code yourself** without discussion.
- **Public criticism** of repeated problems instead of a private conversation.
- **Assuming you're right** without asking.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if they push back on your feedback?"** — "I listen, because they may be right. If it's a preference, I let it go. If it's a real risk, I explain the impact and, if we still disagree, ask a third person or check the team standards."

- **"How do you avoid reviews slowing the team down?"** — "Review within a day, keep pull requests small, automate style, and discuss approaches before coding."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Kind directness.**
- **Prioritisation** of what matters.
- **Raising the team's standard** through systems, not only comments.
- **Healthy relationships** where feedback goes both ways.

#### Practise it

- Rewrite three of your recent review comments using the "effect, reason, suggestion" pattern.
- Checklist: curiosity · prioritised · reasoned · channel · praise · summary · systemic fix.

## How do you decide what to prioritize when everything is urgent?
- id: dc100bc6-6956-48c2-9ca6-6f1c01302d06
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, prioritization

### Prompt
"How do you decide what to prioritise when everything is urgent?" This probes your executive function and your ability to say no.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **A clear method:** do you prioritise by impact and risk, or by whoever shouts loudest?
- **Saying no well:** can you decline or defer work while keeping relationships and trust?
- **Communication:** do you make trade-offs visible to stakeholders, rather than silently dropping things?
- **Focus:** do you avoid spreading yourself across everything and finishing nothing?
- **Escalation judgement:** do you know when the prioritisation decision belongs to someone else?

#### Key points
- **Not everything is truly urgent.** Start by clarifying: what happens if this waits a day, a week, a month? Who is affected, and how badly?
- **Useful criteria:**
  - **Impact:** users affected, revenue, safety, legal or security risk.
  - **Urgency:** is there a real deadline or a cost that grows with time?
  - **Effort:** quick wins can sometimes clear the way.
  - **Dependencies:** what unblocks other people?
  - **Alignment:** what supports the team's agreed goals?
- **A typical order:** production incidents and security issues → things that block others → committed deadlines with real consequences → high-impact planned work → everything else.
- **Make trade-offs explicit:** "If I do A this week, B moves to next week. Is that the right call?" Let the person accountable for priorities (often the product manager or manager) decide when there's a genuine conflict.
- **Protect focus:** limit work in progress, finish things, batch interruptions, and agree on who handles urgent requests (for example, a rotating support role).
- **Saying no:** "Not now" with a reason and an alternative is usually better than a flat "no".
- **Look for patterns:** if everything is always urgent, raise the underlying planning problem.

#### Model answer
*Example answer with a STAR story (adapt to your own experience):*

"First, I check what's actually urgent, because usually not everything is. I ask what happens if each thing waits, and I rank by impact and risk: incidents and security first, then things that unblock others, then real deadlines, then everything else. Where priorities genuinely conflict, I make the trade-off visible and let whoever owns priorities decide.

A real example: in one week, as the frontend lead on our team, I had four 'urgent' requests at once. Sales wanted a demo feature for a big prospect on Friday. Support escalated a bug where some users couldn't download invoices. Our security team flagged an outdated library with a known vulnerability. And my product manager wanted the sprint's main feature finished for a planned launch.

I spent 30 minutes clarifying each. The invoice bug affected about 3% of users, but those included some of our largest customers, and it was blocking their month-end. The library vulnerability was serious in principle, but our analysis showed the vulnerable function wasn't used in our code, so it was important but not an emergency. The sales demo could use a prototype on a staging environment rather than a production feature. And the launch was public, with marketing booked.

So my order was: invoice bug first, which took about half a day. Then I asked another engineer to upgrade the library during the week, with a deadline of Friday. For the sales demo, I spent two hours building a clickable prototype on staging and was clear with the sales lead that it wasn't production-ready; they were fine with that once they understood the trade-off. The sprint feature I protected as our main focus.

The key step was a short message to my product manager and the sales lead together, listing all four items, my proposed order, and what it would mean for each. The product manager agreed, and the sales lead appreciated knowing exactly what they'd get.

Result: the invoice bug was fixed in a day, the vulnerability was patched by Thursday, the demo went well, and the launch shipped on time. Afterwards, I proposed a weekly rotating 'support engineer' role so that urgent requests went to one person, and the rest of the team could focus."

#### Another worked example
*Example STAR story (adapt to your own experience): saying no to a senior stakeholder:*

"**Situation:** a director asked me directly to build a new dashboard widget 'by next week', bypassing our product manager, while we were in the middle of a performance project committed to for the quarter. **Action:** I didn't say no outright. I asked what problem the widget solved and what would happen if it waited. It was for a board presentation, so the date was real, but the need was a single chart, not a reusable widget. I suggested we export the chart from our analytics tool for the presentation, and add the widget to the backlog for the product manager to prioritise. I copied the product manager so priorities stayed in one place. **Result:** the director got their chart in a day, the performance project stayed on track, and the widget was built properly the next quarter."

#### A third worked example
**Simple tools for prioritising:**
- **Impact versus effort grid:** do high-impact, low-effort first; question low-impact, high-effort.
- **Eisenhower matrix:** urgent and important → do; important, not urgent → schedule; urgent, not important → delegate; neither → drop.
- **Cost of delay:** what does waiting a week cost? Useful when comparing revenue, risk and goodwill.
- **RICE scoring** (reach, impact, confidence, effort) for longer backlogs.
Mention one if it fits naturally, but focus on your judgement and communication, not the tool.

#### The 30-second version
"I first ask what happens if each thing waits, because usually not everything is urgent. Then I rank by impact and risk: incidents and security, then things that unblock others, then real deadlines. I make trade-offs visible to whoever owns priorities, offer alternatives instead of a flat no, and protect focus. For example, with four urgent requests in one week, I fixed the customer-blocking bug first, delegated the security patch, gave sales a prototype, and protected the launch."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **List** everything that's competing.
2. **Clarify** impact and the cost of waiting for each.
3. **Rank** by impact, risk, urgency and dependencies.
4. **Look for alternatives:** smaller versions, delegation, workarounds.
5. **Communicate** the proposed order and trade-offs.
6. **Let the owner decide** genuine conflicts.
7. **Protect focus** and finish things.
8. **Fix the pattern** if urgency is constant.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "I just work harder" | Clear criteria: impact, risk, cost of delay |
| Whoever shouts loudest wins | Clarifies what's truly urgent |
| Silently drops things | Makes trade-offs visible |
| Flat "no" | "Not now", with reason and alternative |
| Does everything at once | Limits work in progress, finishes things |
| Absorbs chaos forever | Fixes the underlying pattern |

#### Useful phrases
- "What happens if this waits a week?"
- "If I do A now, B moves to next week. Is that the right trade-off?"
- "I can't do the full version by Friday, but I could do…"
- "Let's agree the order together so priorities stay in one place."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Heroics:** working nights instead of prioritising.
- **No criteria,** just instinct.
- **Not communicating** what's being deferred.
- **Making priority calls that belong to the product manager** without involving them.
- **Saying yes to everything** and missing commitments.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if two senior people both insist theirs is first?"** — "I put both requests side by side with the impact of each, and ask them, or the person they both report to, to decide."
- **"How do you handle interruptions day to day?"** — "Batch messages, keep focus blocks, and use a rotating support role on the team."
- **"Tell me about a time you got prioritisation wrong."** — Have one ready, such as underestimating a 'small' bug that became a big issue.
- **"How do you prioritise technical debt?"** — "By its cost: how much it slows us down or causes incidents. I tie it to product goals and reserve a share of each sprint."
- **"What do you do when you can't meet a deadline?"** — "Tell people early, offer options: reduced scope, more time or more help."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Structured judgement** about impact.
- **Transparent communication** of trade-offs.
- **Saying no** while keeping trust.
- **Improving the system** so urgency becomes rarer.

#### Practise it
- **Find your story:** remember a week with competing urgent demands. Write each item, its real impact, the order you chose and why, how you communicated it, and the result.
- Practise a "not now" response to a senior stakeholder.
- Checklist: list · clarify impact · rank · alternatives · communicate · owner decides · focus · fix pattern.

- "Tell me about a time you had to say no."

## What's the difference between a senior and a staff engineer to you?
- id: 5a087f05-8720-486e-ae14-457286cc03ad
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, leveling, self-awareness

### Prompt
"What's the difference between a senior and a staff engineer to you?" This tests whether you understand levels, and where you honestly sit.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Calibration:** do you understand what each level means, or do you think staff is just "senior with more years"?
- **Self-assessment:** where do you place yourself, and is that consistent with the evidence in your stories?
- **Levelling input:** your answer, and the examples behind it, often influence the level you're offered.
- **Ambition and realism:** if you're aiming for staff, do you know what you need to show?
- **Understanding of organisations:** staff work is about influence across teams, which requires understanding how companies operate.

#### Key points
- **The core difference is scope and how impact happens:**
  - **Senior engineer:** owns complex projects or features end to end within a team. Highly skilled technically, makes good design decisions, mentors others, and makes the team better. Usually given a problem and solves it well.
  - **Staff engineer:** owns problems across several teams or the whole organisation. Finds and frames the problems worth solving, sets technical direction, aligns people without formal authority, and multiplies the impact of many engineers. Often works on things nobody assigned.
- **Common staff archetypes** (from Will Larson's widely used framing): **tech lead** (guides a large area or several teams), **architect** (owns direction for a critical domain), **solver** (goes deep on the hardest problems wherever they are), and **right hand** (extends a senior leader's reach).
- **Frontend staff examples:** setting the rendering and data-fetching strategy for the whole web platform, leading a multi-team migration, creating the design system and its adoption plan, defining performance budgets and accessibility standards across the organisation, or reducing build times for every team.
- **Shift in time:** staff engineers write less code day to day and spend more time on documents, reviews, alignment, mentoring and unblocking. They still need to be technically excellent and credible.
- **Every company defines levels differently;** refer to their career framework if you can.
- **Place yourself honestly,** with evidence. Overclaiming staff without cross-team examples is a red flag; underselling can cost you the right level.

#### Model answer
*Example answer (adapt to your own view and level):*

"For me, the biggest difference is scope and how you create impact.

A senior engineer owns a complex problem within a team and solves it very well. They're trusted to take a vague feature, design it, make good trade-offs, deliver it, and bring others along. They make their team better through reviews and mentoring. For example, a senior frontend engineer might lead the rebuild of a checkout flow, with excellent results.

A staff engineer works on problems across teams, often ones nobody has assigned. Instead of asking 'how do I build this well?', they ask 'what should we be building, and how should all our teams build it?' Using the same example, a staff engineer might notice that four teams are each solving checkout-like flows differently, with inconsistent performance and accessibility, and drive a shared approach: write the strategy, get agreement from four teams and their managers, build the first version of the foundation, and make it easy to adopt. Their impact comes through other people's work.

That changes how the time is spent. Staff engineers write more documents, have more alignment conversations, and review designs across teams, while still being deeply technical, because influence depends on credibility.

Where I am: I'm a strong senior engineer moving into staff-level work. I led a 14-month migration that affected two teams, and I set up our data-fetching pattern, which four teams now use. But most of my work is still within one product area, and the skill I'm developing is setting longer-term direction across teams and getting agreement from people whose priorities differ. So I'd describe myself as senior, with some staff-scope projects, and I'd like to grow into staff fully over the next couple of years."

#### Another worked example
*Example answer (adapt), from someone currently at staff level:*

"The difference I see is between solving problems and choosing them. As a senior engineer, I was given hard problems and solved them well. Now my job is mostly to find the problems that matter most across the frontend organisation and make sure they get solved, often by others. For example, I noticed that our build times had grown to 14 minutes and were slowing every team. Nobody owned it. I wrote a proposal, got time from two teams, led the move to a faster bundler and caching, and builds fell to 3 minutes for about 60 engineers. I wrote some of the code, but most of the impact came from the proposal, the alignment and making it safe to adopt. That's what staff looks like to me: technical judgement applied at the organisation's scale."

#### A third worked example
**Signals that show each level in your stories:**
- **Senior:** "I designed…, I led delivery…, I mentored…, I improved our team's…"
- **Staff:** "I noticed a problem across teams…, I wrote the strategy…, I aligned four teams…, I set a standard that…, the organisation now…"
- **Principal (beyond staff):** influence on company strategy, industry-level expertise, multi-year technical direction.
Make sure your examples match the level you claim, because interviewers check.

#### The 30-second version
"A senior engineer owns complex problems within a team and solves them excellently, making their team better. A staff engineer owns problems across teams, often unassigned: finding the problems worth solving, setting direction, and aligning people without authority, so their impact comes through others. I'm a strong senior engineer with some staff-scope work, like a data-fetching pattern four teams use, and I'm developing cross-team strategy and alignment."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Define the difference** in scope and impact.
2. **Give a concrete example** of the same problem at each level.
3. **Describe how time is spent** differently.
4. **Place yourself** honestly, with evidence.
5. **Name your growth area.**

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)

- **"Give me an example of influence without authority."** — Use your cross-team pattern or migration story.
- **"Do you want to be staff or manager?"** — Explain your preference and reasoning.

#### What senior interviewers look for

## Describe your ideal team culture
- id: 36e8f124-9d56-49ca-b51d-6c2484ffba35
- roles: frontend, fullstack, ai
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: behavioral, culture-fit

### Prompt
"Describe your ideal team culture." This probes for fit. Be honest, but make sure your answer is compatible with their culture.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Fit:** will you thrive in their environment, or be frustrated by it?
- **Self-awareness:** do you know the conditions in which you do your best work?
- **Values:** do you value things that make teams effective, such as ownership, honesty, learning and quality?
- **Contribution:** do you describe culture as something you help create, or only something you receive?
- **Red flags:** wanting no process at all, needing constant direction, or avoiding feedback.

#### Key points
- **Research their culture first:** engineering blog, values page, interview conversations, Glassdoor (with caution), how they talk about on-call, remote work and pace. Then choose the parts of your ideal culture that honestly match.
- **Be honest:** if your ideal is very different from theirs, it's better to find out now. But most people value many things; lead with the ones that overlap.
- **Describe specific behaviours, not slogans:** "people raise risks early without fear of blame" beats "a supportive culture".
- **Themes that resonate in engineering teams:**
  - **Psychological safety:** blameless post-mortems, asking questions freely, admitting mistakes.
  - **Ownership:** engineers own outcomes and metrics, not just tickets.
  - **Honest, kind feedback:** in code review and one-to-ones.
  - **High standards with pragmatism:** quality matters, but shipping matters too.
  - **Learning:** time to learn, sharing knowledge, mentoring.
  - **Close collaboration** with design and product from the start.
  - **Clear goals** with autonomy on how to reach them.
  - **Sustainable pace.**
- **Show how you contribute** to that culture yourself, with an example.
- **Keep it to about a minute.**

#### Model answer
*Example answer (adapt to your own values and the company):*

"Three things matter most to me.

First, a team where it's safe to be wrong. People ask questions in public channels, raise risks early, and post-mortems are about the system, not blame. I've seen how much faster a team learns that way. On my current team, after I caused a checkout incident, the blameless post-mortem led to flagged rollouts that caught two later problems. In a blame culture, I might have been tempted to hide it.

Second, ownership of outcomes, not just tickets. I do my best work when engineers understand why we're building something, are involved early with design and product, and look at the metrics after launch. From what I heard earlier today, your engineers own features end to end including the analytics, which is exactly how I like to work.

Third, high standards delivered pragmatically. I want code review that's honest and kind, and a team that cares about accessibility and performance, but that also knows when good enough is right and ships.

I also try to help build that culture, not just benefit from it. I run a monthly frontend guild session, I write up my own mistakes in our team channel, and I try to give review feedback that explains the why. So my ideal culture is one where those things are normal, and I'd want to help keep them that way."

#### Another worked example
*Example answer (adapt), for a fast-moving startup:*

"I like a team that moves quickly and is comfortable with ambiguity, where people take ownership of whole problems and don't wait to be told what to do. I value directness: if something isn't working, people say so early, kindly. And I like a team that writes things down, short design docs and decision notes, so speed doesn't mean losing context as the team grows. At my last startup, I introduced a one-page decision note template that we used for every significant choice; it kept us fast without repeating debates. From our conversations, that mix of speed and ownership sounds like what you have here."

#### A third worked example
**Matching emphasis to the company, honestly:**
- **Large, established company:** collaboration across teams, clear career growth, quality, and stability.
- **AI research-driven company:** curiosity, rigour, close collaboration with researchers, and comfort with fast change.
- **Regulated industry (finance, health):** care, quality, accessibility, and doing things properly.
- **Remote-first company:** written communication, clear documentation, trust and autonomy.
Choose the true parts of your ideal that fit; don't invent values you don't hold.

#### The 30-second version
"I do my best work in a team where it's safe to be wrong, with blameless post-mortems and open questions; where engineers own outcomes, working closely with design and product and looking at metrics after launch; and where standards are high but pragmatic. I also help build that culture: running a guild, sharing my own mistakes, and giving reviews that explain the why. From what I've heard today, that matches how your team works."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Research** their culture beforehand.
2. **Pick two or three values** you genuinely hold that overlap with theirs.
3. **Describe each as behaviours,** with an example.
4. **Link to what you've learned** about them.
5. **Show how you contribute** to that culture.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| "Friendly people and good vibes" | Specific behaviours: blameless post-mortems, ownership |
| Slogans | Examples from real experience |
| Only what you receive | How you help create it |
| Ignores their culture | Links to what they've shared |
| "No meetings, no process" | Values that make teams effective |
| Dishonest mirroring | Honest overlap |

#### Useful phrases
- "I do my best work when…"
- "In practice, that looks like…"
- "I saw the value of that when…"
- "From what I heard today, that matches…"
- "I try to help build that by…"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Vague answers** ("collaborative, fun").
- **Describing a culture that clearly conflicts** with theirs.
- **Negativity** about your current team's culture.
- **Only focusing on perks.**
- **Pretending** to value things you don't.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What culture do you not work well in?"** — Be honest but measured: "One where mistakes are hidden because people fear blame. Problems get bigger when they're not raised."
- **"How have you improved a team's culture?"** — Give an example, such as introducing blameless post-mortems or a guild.
- **"How do you handle a culture that doesn't match your ideal?"** — "I start by understanding why things are as they are, then improve what I can within my own team, through example first."
- **"What would your current team say about your effect on culture?"** — Share real feedback if you have it.
- **"How do you feel about remote or hybrid working?"** — Answer honestly, and mention how you keep communication strong.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Self-awareness** about working conditions.
- **Healthy values** that support effective teams.
- **Active contribution** to culture.
- **Honest fit.**

- Prepare an honest answer to "What culture don't you work well in?"
- Checklist: research · two or three values · behaviours · examples · link to them · your contribution.
