# Product Owner — Behavioural (continued)

## Tell me about a time you managed competing priorities
- id: po-behavioural-07
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: prioritisation, competing demands, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you had several important priorities competing for your team's time. How did you decide, and what happened?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you **prioritise systematically** under pressure.
- How you **communicate trade-offs** to stakeholders.
- Whether you **protect the team** from overload.
- The **outcome** of your decisions and what you learned.

#### Key points
- Use **STAR**, with most time on **how you decided** (criteria, data, stakeholders).
- Show a **clear framework** (goals, impact, urgency, effort), not gut feel.
- Show **communication**: who you informed, how you handled disappointment.
- Quantify the **result**.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** At a fintech company, in one quarter my team faced three big demands at once: a regulatory change to payment confirmations due in 10 weeks, a request from sales for a reporting feature to close several enterprise deals, and a spike in app crashes after a major release that was hurting ratings.

**Task.** As PO, I needed to decide what the team of six engineers would work on, without failing the regulatory deadline or losing stakeholder trust.

**Action.**
- **Assessed each one with data:**
  - Regulatory change: mandatory, with fines and reputational risk if missed. Estimated at 5 weeks of work.
  - Crashes: affected 8% of mobile sessions; app rating had dropped from 4.6 to 4.2 in a month, and sign-up conversion from the app store had fallen.
  - Reporting: sales estimated £300k of pipeline, but only one deal was confirmed as dependent on it.
- **Prioritised:** (1) crash fixes immediately, because they affected every user and were quick to fix (estimated 1–2 weeks); (2) the regulatory change, with a buffer before the deadline; (3) reporting after that.
- **Looked for creative options** for sales: we offered the key prospect a scheduled data export as an interim solution, which took 3 days.
- **Communicated openly:** I met the Head of Sales to explain the reasoning with data, shared a clear timeline, and sent a written summary to all stakeholders.
- **Protected the team** by stopping smaller requests for the quarter and keeping a buffer for unexpected issues.

**Result.**
- Crash rate fell from 8% to under 1% in 10 days, and the app rating recovered to 4.5 within six weeks.
- The regulatory change shipped a week before the deadline.
- The key prospect signed using the interim export; full reporting shipped the following month.
- I learned the value of quantifying every demand in the same terms. It turned an emotional debate into a clear decision."

#### Another worked example: personal competing priorities
"**Situation.** As PO for two small teams, I was asked to prepare a quarterly strategy presentation, lead discovery for a new feature, and cover for a colleague on leave, all in the same month.
**Action.** I listed everything, estimated time and impact, delegated sprint ceremonies for one team to an experienced Scrum Master, time-boxed discovery interviews to two days, and agreed with my manager to move a lower-priority report by two weeks.
**Result.** The strategy presentation was approved, discovery uncovered a key insight for the next quarter, and neither team's delivery suffered. I learned to raise capacity issues early with my manager rather than trying to do everything."

#### A third example: competing priorities in a small startup
"**Situation.** In a 15-person startup, the CEO wanted a new feature for investors, our biggest customer wanted bug fixes, and the team needed time to fix a slow database before peak season.
**Action.** I listed each with its impact and deadline: the database risk could cause outages during peak season (a revenue risk), customer bugs threatened a renewal in six weeks, and the investor demo was in three weeks but could use a prototype. Plan: prototype for the investors (designer plus one engineer, three days), database work first for the rest of the team, then the customer bugs.
**Result.** The investor demo went well with the prototype, peak season had no outages, and the customer renewed after the fixes shipped."

#### Make it your own
- Pick a period where you had **at least three demands** competing for limited capacity.
- Show your **criteria** (impact, urgency, effort, risk) and **how you communicated** the decision.
- Include at least one **creative option** (prototype, interim fix, phasing).
- Quantify the **result** for each priority.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | The competing priorities and stakes |
| Task | Your responsibility |
| Action | Criteria, data, creative options, communication, protecting the team |
| Result | Outcomes for each priority, and learning |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I worked harder to do everything." | Made explicit trade-offs |
| Gut-feel decisions | Clear criteria with data |
| Didn't communicate | Explained reasoning to stakeholders |
| No result | Quantified outcomes for each priority |

#### Useful phrases
- "I put every demand into the same terms: impact, urgency and effort."
- "I offered an interim solution so the stakeholder wasn't left empty-handed."
- "I explained the reasoning with data and shared a clear timeline."
- "I protected the team by pausing smaller requests."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Claiming you did everything** without trade-offs.
- **Vague criteria.**
- **No mention of communication.**
- **No numbers.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What would you have done if the CEO wanted reporting first?"**
  "Shared the same data and risks, made sure the regulatory deadline was understood, and if they still chose reporting, made the trade-off explicit and adjusted the plan."
- **"How did the Head of Sales react?"**
  "Initially disappointed, but the interim export and clear timeline helped, and the deal closing rebuilt trust."
- **"What would you do differently?"**
  "Set up monitoring that would have caught the crash spike faster after release."

- **"How did you decide what was 'urgent'?"**
  "By asking what would happen if it waited two weeks: real deadlines, customer impact or risk, rather than how loudly it was requested."
- **"What would you do with the same situation and half the team?"**
  "Focus on the highest-risk item first, look for even smaller interim solutions, and agree explicitly with leadership what would slip."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Portfolio-level prioritisation** across teams.
- **Executive communication** of trade-offs.
- **Systems** that reduce future conflicts.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a time you had at least three competing demands.
2. Write how you assessed each (impact, urgency, effort), what you chose, and the result.
3. Practise **out loud in under 3 minutes**.

## Tell me about a time you made a decision with incomplete information
- id: po-behavioural-08
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: ambiguity, decision making, risk, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you had to make an important decision without having all the information you wanted.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Comfort with uncertainty**: can you decide and move forward?
- How you **gather enough evidence** quickly and **manage risk**.
- Whether you make decisions **reversible** where possible.
- Whether you **learn** from the outcome.

#### Key points
- Show you **didn't wait for perfect information**.
- Explain **how you reduced uncertainty** quickly (data you had, quick research, experts).
- Show **risk management**: reversible steps, checkpoints, monitoring.
- Share the **outcome** honestly, including what you'd change.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** At an e-commerce company, our payment provider announced a sudden price increase that would raise our transaction costs by about 30%. We had six weeks to decide whether to stay, renegotiate or switch, and a switch would normally take months to evaluate.

**Task.** As PO for payments, I needed to recommend a path to leadership quickly, without full data on alternative providers' real-world performance with our traffic.

**Action.**
- **Defined what mattered most**: payment success rate, cost, integration effort, and risk to customers.
- **Gathered evidence fast**: requested proposals from two alternative providers, asked our engineers to do a 3-day spike on integration effort, and spoke to two peers at other companies about their experiences.
- **Identified the biggest unknown**: whether payment success rates would hold for our customer mix.
- **Made the decision reversible**: recommended integrating one alternative provider alongside the existing one and routing **5% of transactions** to it, with clear success criteria (success rate within 0.5 points of the current provider).
- **Used the evidence to negotiate**: having a credible alternative also strengthened our position with the existing provider.

**Result.**
- The new provider matched our success rate in the 5% test and was 25% cheaper. We gradually moved most volume, saving about £180k a year.
- Our original provider later offered a reduced price, and we kept them as a backup for resilience.
- Lesson: when information is incomplete, focus on the biggest unknown and design a small, reversible test to answer it."

#### Another worked example: product scope
"**Situation.** We had to decide whether to launch a feature for all customers or a single segment, with limited data on how other segments would use it.
**Action.** I used the data we had (strong demand from small businesses, little from enterprises), launched for small businesses first, and set up tracking and interviews for any enterprise users who tried it.
**Result.** Small-business adoption hit 30% in two months; enterprise feedback showed different needs, which we addressed in a separate project. Launching to everyone would have created confusion and support load."

#### A third example: launching in a new country with limited data
"**Situation.** We needed to decide whether to launch our app in Ireland, with little local data.
**Action.** We used what we had: UK users with Irish addresses, waitlist sign-ups from Ireland, and a quick review of local competitors. The biggest unknown was whether local payment preferences differed. We launched to a waitlist of 500 Irish users with UK features plus one local payment method, with criteria for full launch.
**Result.** Activation and retention matched the UK closely, so we launched fully two months later, adding a second local payment method based on beta feedback."

#### Make it your own
- Choose a decision with a **real deadline** and **genuine gaps** in information.
- Explain how you **identified the biggest unknown**.
- Show how you made the decision **reversible or staged**.
- Be honest about the **outcome**, including anything you'd change.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | The decision, the time pressure, what was unknown |
| Task | Your responsibility |
| Action | What mattered, quick evidence, biggest unknown, reversible step |
| Result | Outcome and lesson |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I went with my gut." | Gathered the best available evidence quickly |
| Waited for full data | Decided within the time available |
| All-or-nothing decision | Reversible, staged decision |
| No reflection | Clear lesson about handling uncertainty |

#### Useful phrases
- "I focused on the single biggest unknown."
- "I made the decision reversible by testing with 5% first."
- "Waiting for perfect information would have meant missing the deadline."
- "We set clear criteria to decide whether to continue."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Reckless decisions** framed as decisiveness.
- **Analysis paralysis** stories.
- **No risk management.**
- **Hiding a bad outcome.** Honest reflection is better.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if the test had failed?"**
  "We'd have stopped routing traffic to the new provider, used the evidence to negotiate, and looked at the second alternative."
- **"How do you know when you have enough information?"**
  "When more information is unlikely to change the decision, or when the cost of delay exceeds the value of more data."
- **"How did you communicate uncertainty to leadership?"**
  "I was explicit about what we knew, what we didn't, and how the staged test would reduce the risk."

- **"How did you communicate the uncertainty?"**
  "I was explicit about what we knew, what we didn't, and how the staged approach limited the downside."
- **"What would you have done if you couldn't make it reversible?"**
  "Invest more time in evidence for the biggest risk, involve more experts, and make sure leadership understood and accepted the risk."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Decisiveness with risk management.**
- **Structuring ambiguity** for others.
- **Learning loops** that improve future decisions.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a decision made under time pressure with missing information.
2. Identify the biggest unknown, and how you reduced the risk.
3. Practise **out loud in under 3 minutes**.

## Tell me about a time you influenced without authority
- id: po-behavioural-09
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: influence, stakeholders, persuasion, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you needed to get people outside your team to do something, without having authority over them.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Influence skills**: Product Owners rarely have direct authority over the people they need.
- Whether you understand **others' goals** and frame requests accordingly.
- Use of **evidence, relationships and collaboration**.
- **Persistence** and outcomes.

#### Key points
- Show how you **understood the other party's priorities**.
- Use **data and customer evidence** to make the case.
- Find a **shared goal** or mutual benefit.
- Build **relationships** and allies.
- Show the **result** and how the relationship continued.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** At a SaaS company, our customers frequently struggled to connect our product to their accounting software. Fixing it required changes from the **platform team**, which had its own roadmap and no obligation to prioritise our request.

**Task.** I needed the platform team to prioritise an improvement to the integration framework within the next quarter.

**Action.**
- **Understood their goals**: I met the platform team's PO. Their key objective was reducing integration-related incidents and support escalations across the company.
- **Built the case with data**: integration issues generated about 600 support tickets a month, 40% of escalations to engineering, and were mentioned in 25% of churn surveys. Most of these tickets came from one fragile part of the integration framework.
- **Framed it as a shared win**: fixing it would cut platform incidents, which was *their* goal, while improving our customers' experience.
- **Reduced their effort**: offered one of our engineers to work with their team for a sprint, and our designer to improve error messages.
- **Built allies**: shared the data with the Head of Customer Support, who supported the case in the quarterly planning discussion.

**Result.**
- The platform team prioritised the work. Together we shipped it in six weeks.
- Integration tickets dropped by 55%, engineering escalations fell significantly, and the platform team hit its incident-reduction goal.
- The relationship became a long-term partnership; we started joint planning each quarter.
- Lesson: influence works best when you help others achieve their goals, and back it with evidence."

#### Another worked example: influencing leadership
"**Situation.** I believed we should invest in accessibility, but leadership saw it as low priority.
**Action.** I gathered evidence: the number of users affected, legal requirements, competitor practices, and a short video of a screen-reader user unable to complete checkout. I proposed a small first step (fixing checkout blockers in one sprint).
**Result.** Leadership approved it after seeing the video. The fixes reduced related support contacts, and accessibility became part of our Definition of Done."

#### A third example: influencing marketing
"**Situation.** Marketing was running campaigns promising features that were still in early discovery, creating customer disappointment.
**Action.** I met the marketing lead to understand their goal (launch announcements to drive sign-ups), shared customer complaints and support tickets caused by mismatched expectations, and proposed a monthly product-marketing sync with a clear 'safe to promote' list. I also offered early access to beta features for campaign content.
**Result.** Campaigns aligned with what was actually available, complaints about missing features dropped, and marketing gained better content from real beta customers."

#### Make it your own
- Think of someone you **needed but didn't manage**: another team, a senior leader, a partner.
- Write down **their goals** and how you linked your request to them.
- Note the **evidence** you used and any **allies** who helped.
- Show how the **relationship continued** afterwards.

#### The 30-second version
"Our customers struggled to connect our product to their accounting software, and fixing it needed the platform team, who had other priorities. I learned their goal was reducing integration incidents, showed that our integration issues caused 40% of engineering escalations, and offered one of our engineers to help. They prioritised it, we shipped it together in six weeks, integration tickets fell 55%, and the platform team hit its incident goal. Since then we plan jointly every quarter."

Use this short version if the interviewer asks for a brief example, or if time is running out, and expand on any part they ask about.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | Who you needed, and why you lacked authority |
| Task | What you needed them to do |
| Action | Their goals, your evidence, shared benefit, reduced effort, allies |
| Result | Outcome and continuing relationship |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I escalated to get it prioritised." | Influenced through shared goals and evidence |
| Focused only on own needs | Framed it around the other team's goals |
| No data | Strong evidence (tickets, churn, escalations) |
| One-off win | Built a lasting relationship |

#### Useful phrases
- "I started by understanding what mattered to them."
- "I showed how it would help them hit their own goal."
- "I offered to reduce their effort by lending an engineer."
- "I built support from other stakeholders who shared the problem."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Escalation as the main tactic.**
- **Ignoring the other party's goals.**
- **No evidence.**
- **Burning relationships** to win.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if they still said no?"**
  "I'd understand why, look for a smaller first step, and if it was important to company goals, escalate together with them, not against them."
- **"How do you build influence over time?"**
  "Deliver on commitments, share useful insights, help others succeed, and be transparent."
- **"Who were your key allies?"**
  "The Head of Support, whose team felt the pain directly, and the platform team's tech lead."

- **"What do you do when influence fails?"**
  "Understand their constraints, offer a smaller first step, and if it's important to company goals, escalate together with them rather than against them."
- **"How do you influence someone more senior?"**
  "Be concise, bring data and a clear recommendation, connect it to their priorities, and respect their time."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Organisation-wide influence.**
- **Coalition building** for strategic initiatives.
- **Win-win** framing as a habit.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a time you needed another team or a senior person to act.
2. Write their goals, your evidence, and how you framed the shared benefit.
3. Practise **out loud in under 3 minutes**.

## Tell me about a time you handled a difficult stakeholder
- id: po-behavioural-10
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: difficult stakeholders, conflict, relationships, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you worked with a difficult stakeholder. What made it difficult, and how did you handle it?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Emotional intelligence** and professionalism.
- Whether you **understand the stakeholder's perspective** rather than labelling them.
- **Practical tactics**: communication, boundaries, involvement.
- A **constructive outcome** and improved relationship.

#### Key points
- Describe the **behaviour**, not the person ("they frequently changed requirements", not "they were impossible").
- Show you **sought to understand** their pressures and goals.
- Show specific **actions**: more involvement, clearer communication, data, agreements.
- Show the **relationship improved** and the business result.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** I worked with a Head of Marketing who frequently changed requirements for a campaign landing-page tool, often late in the sprint, and escalated directly to my manager when things weren't delivered as expected.

**Task.** I needed to deliver the tool for an important product launch, and to build a working relationship that didn't keep disrupting the team.

**Action.**
- **Understood their pressures**: in a one-to-one, I learned they were under intense pressure from the CMO, and campaign plans genuinely changed often. They felt the product team was slow and didn't understand marketing timelines.
- **Involved them earlier**: invited them to refinement for their requests and to sprint reviews, so they saw progress and could give feedback early rather than late.
- **Created a clear process**: agreed that changes after the sprint started would be discussed with me, with the trade-offs made visible (what moves out if this comes in).
- **Built flexibility into the product**: we added configurable templates so marketing could change copy and layout themselves, without needing development for every change.
- **Regular communication**: a short weekly update on progress and upcoming releases.

**Result.**
- Late changes dropped significantly, and escalations stopped.
- The configurable templates meant marketing launched campaigns in days instead of weeks.
- The Head of Marketing later became one of our strongest advocates and asked to collaborate on the next quarter's roadmap.
- Lesson: 'difficult' behaviour usually comes from pressure and frustration. Understanding the cause, and changing the system, works better than pushing back."

#### Another worked example: a sceptical executive
"**Situation.** A finance director questioned every product investment and delayed approvals.
**Action.** I learned they'd been burned by past projects that overpromised. I started sharing concise business cases with clear assumptions, and reporting actual results against forecasts after launches.
**Result.** Approvals became faster as trust grew, and the director started inviting me to budget discussions early."

#### A third example: a demanding client
"**Situation.** In an agency, a client's product manager emailed daily with new requests and criticised the team's speed.
**Action.** In a call, I learned their boss judged them on visible weekly progress. We agreed a weekly demo, a shared backlog they could add to, and a prioritisation session every two weeks where they chose what came next within agreed capacity.
**Result.** Daily emails stopped, the client felt in control, and the project was renewed for another year."

#### Make it your own
- Describe the stakeholder's **behaviour objectively**, without labels.
- Identify the **pressure or goal** behind their behaviour.
- Show **specific actions** (involvement, process, communication) and the **improved relationship**.
- Practise saying it with **empathy**, as if the stakeholder might hear it.

#### The 30-second version
"A Head of Marketing kept changing requirements late in sprints and escalating to my manager. In a one-to-one I learned they were under heavy pressure and felt we didn't understand marketing timelines. I involved them in refinement and reviews, agreed a simple process for late changes, and we built configurable templates so marketing could change pages themselves. Late changes and escalations stopped, campaigns launched in days instead of weeks, and they became one of our strongest advocates."

Use this short version if the interviewer asks for a brief example, then expand on whichever part they're most interested in.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | The behaviour that made it difficult (objectively) |
| Task | What you needed to achieve |
| Action | Understanding, involvement, process, product changes, communication |
| Result | Better relationship and business outcome |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "They were just impossible." | Describes behaviour objectively |
| Avoided them | Engaged and sought to understand |
| Complained to management | Changed the process and the product |
| No improvement | Relationship improved; business result |

#### Useful phrases
- "I wanted to understand what was driving the behaviour."
- "They were under a lot of pressure from…"
- "We agreed a process that made trade-offs visible."
- "They became one of our strongest advocates."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Blaming or criticising** the stakeholder.
- **Passive stories** with no actions.
- **Escalation as the solution.**
- **No positive outcome.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What would you have done if they didn't change?"**
  "Kept documenting trade-offs, involved my manager to align expectations, and continued focusing on shared goals."
- **"How do you stay calm with difficult people?"**
  "Focus on the problem and shared goals, assume positive intent, and take time before responding to emotional messages."
- **"What did you learn about yourself?"**
  "That I sometimes waited too long to address friction. Now I address it early, privately."

- **"How do you protect your team from a difficult stakeholder?"**
  "Channel requests through me, make trade-offs visible, and shield the team from last-minute changes while keeping the stakeholder informed."
- **"Have you ever failed to win over a stakeholder?"**
  Have an honest example ready: what you tried, what you learned, and how you kept working effectively anyway.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Mature relationship management** with senior people.
- **System-level fixes** to recurring friction.
- **Turning critics into allies.**

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a stakeholder whose behaviour made your work harder.
2. Write their pressures, your actions, and how the relationship changed.
3. Practise **out loud in under 3 minutes**, without negative labels.

## Tell me about a time you received critical feedback
- id: po-behavioural-11
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: feedback, self-awareness, growth, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you received critical feedback. How did you respond, and what did you change?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Self-awareness** and **openness** to feedback.
- Whether you respond with **curiosity rather than defensiveness**.
- Whether you **act** on feedback and show lasting change.

#### Key points
- Choose **real, meaningful feedback** (not trivial or a disguised strength).
- Show your **initial reaction honestly**, then how you processed it.
- Show **specific actions** you took.
- Show **evidence of improvement** and ongoing habits.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** About six months into my first Product Owner role, my manager shared feedback from the engineering team in my review: they felt I brought them solutions rather than problems, and that they weren't involved early enough to shape what we built.

**Task.** I needed to understand the feedback and change how I worked with the team.

**Action.**
- **Reacted with curiosity**: my first instinct was to explain that I was trying to save them time. Instead, I asked my manager for examples, and then spoke to two engineers directly to understand how it felt from their side.
- **Understood the impact**: they told me they often saw better technical options but felt the decisions were already made, which was demotivating and led to more complex solutions.
- **Changed my approach**:
  - Brought **problem statements with data** to refinement instead of detailed solutions.
  - Invited the tech lead and designer into discovery and customer interviews.
  - Held short **problem-framing sessions** at the start of new initiatives, where the team proposed solutions.
- **Asked for ongoing feedback** in retrospectives about whether it was working.

**Result.**
- Within two months, the team's ideas simplified two planned features, saving several weeks of work.
- In the next team survey, engineers rated 'involvement in decisions' much higher.
- I still use problem-framing sessions today. It was one of the most valuable pieces of feedback I've received."

#### Another worked example: communication feedback
"**Situation.** A stakeholder told my manager my updates were too long and detailed.
**Action.** I asked a few stakeholders what they needed, then switched to a short weekly update: three bullet points (progress, risks, decisions needed) with links for detail.
**Result.** Engagement with updates improved, and stakeholders said they finally felt informed."

#### A third example: feedback on presentations
"**Situation.** After a quarterly review, a director told me my presentation had too much detail and no clear recommendation.
**Action.** I asked what they'd prefer, studied how well-received presentations were structured, and changed my format: recommendation first, three supporting points, risks, and an appendix for detail. I practised with a colleague before the next review.
**Result.** The next review finished early with a decision, and the director said it was one of the clearest updates that quarter. I now use this structure for all leadership updates."

#### Make it your own
- Pick feedback that **genuinely changed** how you work.
- Be honest about your **initial reaction**, then show **curiosity**.
- Describe **specific changes** and **evidence** of improvement.
- Show it's now a **habit**, not a one-off fix.

#### The 30-second version
"Six months into my first PO role, my manager told me engineers felt I brought them solutions instead of problems. My first instinct was to explain, but I asked for examples and spoke to two engineers. I started bringing problem statements with data, invited engineers into discovery, and ran problem-framing sessions. Within two months, the team's ideas simplified two features and saved weeks of work, and their scores for involvement in decisions rose. I still work this way."

Practise this short version first; it's often all you need, and it makes the longer version easier to tell clearly.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | Who gave the feedback and what it was |
| Task | What you needed to do |
| Action | Your reaction, understanding, specific changes, follow-up |
| Result | Evidence of improvement and lasting habits |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I work too hard" type feedback | Real, meaningful feedback |
| Defensive or dismissive | Curious; sought examples |
| No change | Specific actions and new habits |
| No evidence | Measurable improvement |

#### Useful phrases
- "My first instinct was to explain, but I asked for examples instead."
- "It helped me see the impact from their side."
- "I changed how I ran refinement by…"
- "I still use that practice today."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Disguised strengths** as feedback.
- **Defensiveness.**
- **No concrete changes.**
- **Feedback with no impact** on your work.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How do you ask for feedback?"**
  "Regularly and specifically, e.g. 'What's one thing I could do to make refinement more useful for you?'"
- **"What if you disagree with feedback?"**
  "I'd still seek to understand the perception behind it, because perception affects working relationships, and then discuss my view openly."
- **"How do you give critical feedback?"**
  "Privately, specifically, focused on behaviour and impact, with an offer of support."

- **"How do you ask for feedback from stakeholders?"**
  "After key meetings or launches, I ask one specific question, e.g. 'What's one thing that would have made this update more useful?'"
- **"What's the most useful feedback you've given someone?"**
  Have an example ready showing you give feedback privately, specifically and constructively.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Growth mindset** and coachability.
- Creating a **feedback culture** in teams.
- **Self-reflection** as a habit.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: real feedback that changed how you work.
2. Write your honest initial reaction, what you did, and evidence it worked.
3. Practise **out loud in under 2.5 minutes**.

## Tell me about a time you missed a deadline
- id: po-behavioural-12
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you or your team missed an important deadline. What happened and what did you learn?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Accountability**: do you own your part?
- **Communication** when things go wrong.
- **Recovery**: how you limited the impact.
- **Learning** and process improvement.

#### Key points
- Choose a **real** missed deadline with meaningful stakes.
- Explain **what caused it**, including your part.
- Show **early communication** and **mitigation**.
- Show **specific changes** to prevent it happening again.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** At an insurance company, we committed to launching an online claims feature by the end of the quarter, ahead of a marketing campaign. Three weeks before launch, we discovered that integrating with the claims-handling system was far more complex than estimated: its API didn't support several claim types we needed.

**Task.** As PO, I had committed to the date with stakeholders, and I needed to manage the situation and the impact on the campaign.

**Action.**
- **Owned my part**: I had accepted the estimate without a technical spike on the integration, even though it was the riskiest part of the project. That was a planning mistake on my side.
- **Communicated immediately**: as soon as the tech lead confirmed the problem, I told the marketing lead and my manager, explaining the issue and offering options rather than waiting.
- **Offered options**:
  - Launch on time with only the two most common claim types (about 65% of claims).
  - Delay the full launch by six weeks.
- **Recommended and agreed** the reduced launch, with marketing adjusting the campaign message to focus on those claim types.
- **Delivered the rest** in two further releases over the next six weeks.

**Result.**
- The partial launch went live on the original date. 40% of eligible claims moved online in the first month.
- The full feature followed six weeks later.
- **Lessons and changes**: I now insist on technical spikes for high-risk integrations during discovery, and I communicate dates as ranges with confidence levels until risks are resolved. On my next integration project, a spike uncovered a similar issue in week two, and we planned around it."

#### Another worked example: a missed personal deadline
"**Situation.** I missed a deadline for delivering a business case to leadership because I underestimated the time needed to gather finance data.
**Action.** I told my manager two days before, shared a draft with key assumptions, and agreed a new date.
**Result.** The final case was approved. I now start data requests earlier and build buffers into personal deadlines."

#### A third example: a missed internal deadline
"**Situation.** I committed to delivering a new onboarding flow before a company all-hands demo, but user testing a week before revealed confusing steps.
**Action.** I told my manager immediately and proposed demoing the improved prototype at the all-hands, with real user testing results, while the team fixed the issues properly. I explained that launching a confusing flow would hurt activation.
**Result.** The demo was well received, the improved flow launched two weeks later, and activation rose 12%. I learned to schedule user testing earlier, well before key dates."

#### Make it your own
- Choose a deadline that **mattered**.
- Clearly state **your part** in the cause.
- Show **early communication**, **options** and **mitigation**.
- Share a **specific process change** you've applied since, with an example.

#### The 30-second version
"We committed to launching online claims by the end of the quarter, but three weeks before, we found the claims system's API couldn't support several claim types. I owned that I hadn't insisted on a spike for that risky integration, told stakeholders immediately, and offered options. We launched the two most common claim types on time, covering about 65% of claims, and the rest six weeks later. I now always run spikes on risky integrations and give dates as ranges."

Keep this ready for when the interviewer wants a quick example, and be ready to go deeper on the cause and what you changed.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | The commitment and what went wrong |
| Task | Your responsibility |
| Action | Ownership, early communication, options, recovery |
| Result | Impact limited, lessons, changes applied since |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| Blames another team | Owns their part of the cause |
| Communicated at the last minute | Communicated as soon as the problem was known |
| No options | Offered options and a recommendation |
| No lessons | Specific process changes applied later |

#### Useful phrases
- "My part in it was not insisting on a spike for the riskiest integration."
- "I told stakeholders as soon as we knew, with options."
- "We launched a reduced version on the original date."
- "Since then, I always…"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Blaming others.**
- **Choosing a trivial example.**
- **Late communication** in the story.
- **No lessons applied** since.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How did stakeholders react?"**
  "Disappointed at first, but the early warning and partial launch kept trust intact."
- **"How do you avoid over-committing now?"**
  "Ranges instead of single dates, spikes for risky work, buffers, and regular checkpoints."
- **"What if the deadline couldn't move at all?"**
  "Then scope must flex, so focus on the minimum required and find cheaper ways to meet it."

- **"How do you decide whether to miss a deadline or ship something incomplete?"**
  "Consider customer impact and risk: if shipping would harm customers or trust, a short delay with a clear plan is better; if a smaller version still delivers real value safely, ship that."
- **"How did your team feel about the missed deadline?"**
  "Disappointed, so I made sure there was no blame, and we focused the retrospective on what we'd change."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Accountability** without defensiveness.
- **Risk management** improvements at team or organisation level.
- **Trust-building** through transparency.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a missed deadline where you can own part of the cause.
2. Write the options you offered and the lesson you've applied since.
3. Practise **out loud in under 3 minutes**.
