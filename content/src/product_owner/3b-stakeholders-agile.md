# Product Owner — Stakeholders & Agile Delivery (continued)

## What is the Product Owner's role in each Scrum event?
- id: po-agile-07
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: scrum events, sprint planning, review, retrospective

### Prompt
Walk me through the Scrum events. What is the Product Owner's role in each one?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Solid knowledge of **Scrum** fundamentals.
- Whether you understand the PO's **specific contribution** to each event.
- Practical experience of making events **valuable**, not just attending them.

#### Key points
- The **Sprint** is the container event (usually 1–4 weeks, commonly 2).
- The four events inside it (Scrum Guide):
  - **Sprint Planning**: what can be done and how. The PO brings the Product Goal and ordered backlog; together the team crafts a **Sprint Goal**.
  - **Daily Scrum**: 15 minutes for developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal. The PO is optional; attends to help, not to manage.
  - **Sprint Review**: inspect the outcome with stakeholders and adapt. The PO leads collaboration with stakeholders on what's next.
  - **Sprint Retrospective**: the Scrum Team improves how it works. The PO participates as a team member.
- **Backlog refinement** is an ongoing activity (not a formal event) that the PO leads.

#### Model answer
"Scrum has the **Sprint** as the container, with four events inside it. Here's my role in each.

**1. Sprint Planning** (up to about 2 hours for a 2-week sprint):
- I explain the **Product Goal** and how this sprint can contribute.
- I bring the top of the **ordered, refined backlog**, explaining the why behind each item.
- I propose a draft **Sprint Goal**, e.g. 'Customers can schedule automatic payment reminders for their invoices', which we finalise together.
- The **developers decide how much** they can take on and **how** to do it. I answer questions and negotiate scope, but I don't push them to take on more.

**2. Daily Scrum** (15 minutes):
- It's the developers' event to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adapt their plan.
- I attend when useful, mainly to answer questions or clarify decisions quickly, but I don't run it or turn it into a status report for me.

**3. Sprint Review:**
- A **working session** with stakeholders, not just a demo. The team shows what was done, and we discuss feedback, changes in the market or priorities, and what to do next.
- I share progress toward the Product Goal and metrics, and update the backlog based on feedback.

**4. Sprint Retrospective:**
- The whole Scrum Team, including me, reflects on how we work: what went well, what didn't, and what to improve.
- I contribute honestly, including about my own role (e.g. 'stories weren't clear enough this sprint').
- We agree one or two concrete improvements.

**Ongoing: backlog refinement.** I lead regular refinement sessions so the backlog is ready for planning.

**Making events valuable.** I prepare well (refined backlog, clear goal, data for the review), respect time-boxes, and keep the focus on outcomes rather than ceremony."

#### Another worked example: improving a weak sprint review
"In one team, the sprint review was a 10-minute demo with no stakeholders attending. I changed it:
- Invited the right stakeholders (support, sales, a customer success manager).
- Framed it around the Sprint Goal and metrics.
- Let developers demo their own work.
- Added time for feedback and discussion about next steps.
- Attendance grew, and stakeholders started giving useful input that changed our priorities."

#### Scrum events and the PO's role
| Event | Purpose | PO's role |
|---|---|---|
| Sprint Planning | Plan the sprint; set the Sprint Goal | Bring Product Goal, ordered backlog; co-create the Sprint Goal |
| Daily Scrum | Developers inspect progress | Optional; available for questions |
| Sprint Review | Inspect outcome with stakeholders | Lead the conversation on what's next; update backlog |
| Sprint Retrospective | Improve how the team works | Participate as a team member |
| Refinement (activity) | Prepare upcoming items | Lead and prepare |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Describe the Sprint** as the container.
2. **Explain each event**: purpose, timing.
3. **State the PO's role** in each.
4. **Give an example** of making an event valuable.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "The PO runs the daily stand-up." | Knows the Daily Scrum belongs to developers |
| Sprint Review = demo | Sprint Review = working session with stakeholders |
| PO skips retrospectives | PO participates in retrospectives |
| PO decides sprint scope | Developers decide how much they take on |

#### Useful phrases
- "In planning, I bring the why; the developers decide how much and how."
- "The Daily Scrum is the developers' event. I'm there to help, not to manage."
- "The Sprint Review is a working session, not a show-and-tell."
- "In retrospectives, I reflect on my own role too."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Running the Daily Scrum** as a status meeting.
- **Treating the review as a demo** only.
- **Skipping retrospectives.**
- **Pushing the team** to take on more in planning.
- **Coming unprepared**, e.g. no refined backlog.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How long should each event be?"**
  "For a one-month sprint, the Scrum Guide sets maximums of 8 hours planning, 4 hours review and 3 hours retrospective; shorter sprints usually need proportionally less. The Daily Scrum is always 15 minutes."
- **"Who attends the Sprint Review?"**
  "The Scrum Team and key stakeholders the PO invites."
- **"What if the team doesn't meet the Sprint Goal?"**
  "Discuss it openly in the review and retrospective, understand why, and adapt."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Making events **outcome-focused**, not ceremonial.
- Coaching teams and stakeholders on **effective Scrum**.
- Adapting events **across multiple teams**.

#### Practise it
1. Write the PO's role in each event in one sentence.
2. Describe how you'd improve a poorly run sprint review.
3. Explain the events **out loud in 3 minutes**.

## How do you run sprint planning and set a sprint goal?
- id: po-agile-08
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: sprint planning, sprint goal, scrum

### Prompt
How do you prepare for and run sprint planning? What makes a good sprint goal?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Preparation** and facilitation skills.
- Understanding of the **sprint goal** as a focus for the sprint.
- Respect for the team's **ownership of how much and how**.
- Ability to produce a **realistic, shared plan**.

#### Key points
- **Prepare**: refined backlog, a draft goal, known capacity, dependencies resolved.
- Planning answers three questions (Scrum Guide): **Why** is this sprint valuable? **What** can be done? **How** will it be done?
- A good **sprint goal** is a single, clear **objective** (an outcome or coherent increment), not a list of tickets.
- The team decides how much it can take on, based on **capacity and past throughput**.
- The goal gives **flexibility**: if items change during the sprint, the goal stays the anchor.

#### Model answer
"**Before planning:**
- The top of the backlog is **refined**: clear stories, acceptance criteria, estimates, designs where needed.
- I've drafted a **sprint goal** linked to our product goal and current OKR.
- I know the team's **capacity**: holidays, on-call, other commitments.
- **Dependencies** are identified and resolved, or excluded.

**During planning** (around 2 hours for a 2-week sprint):

**1. Why**: I explain the product goal, current progress and the proposed sprint goal. For example:
> 'Our objective this quarter is to help customers get paid faster. This sprint, I'd like the goal to be: **customers can send their first automatic payment reminder**.'

**2. What**: we look at the backlog items that contribute to the goal. The developers consider their capacity and past throughput, and decide how much they can take on. I answer questions and help adjust scope, e.g. splitting a story so the core part fits.

**3. How**: the developers break items into tasks and plan how they'll work. I stay available for questions but don't direct the how.

**4. Confirm**: we agree the sprint goal and the sprint backlog, and check everyone feels the plan is realistic.

**What makes a good sprint goal:**
- **One clear objective**, not a list of tickets.
- **Valuable**: it delivers something meaningful to users or moves us toward the product goal.
- **Achievable** within the sprint.
- **Gives flexibility**: if one story turns out harder, the team can adjust scope while still meeting the goal.

**Good**: 'New users can complete onboarding on mobile without contacting support.'
**Weak**: 'Complete tickets 123, 145, 178 and 190.'"

#### Another worked example: a technical sprint goal
"Sometimes the goal is technical, with a clear purpose: 'Move payment processing to the new provider with no customer-facing disruption, so we can launch Apple Pay next sprint.' It still states value and a clear outcome."

#### Planning checklist
| Before | During | After |
|---|---|---|
| Refined backlog | Explain why and the draft goal | Sprint goal visible to everyone |
| Draft sprint goal | Team selects items based on capacity | Sprint backlog in the team's tool |
| Capacity known | Team plans how | Stakeholders informed of the goal |
| Dependencies resolved | Agree goal and plan | |

#### A third worked example: a sprint with a hard dependency
"Our sprint goal was 'Customers can pay invoices by bank transfer using a payment link', but the payments team's API would only be ready on day 4 of a 10-day sprint.
- **In planning**, the developers proposed building the link screens and email first against an agreed API contract (a mock), then integrating on day 5.
- **We added a checkpoint**: if the API wasn't ready by day 6, we'd ship the screens behind a feature flag and integrate next sprint, so the goal would change to 'payment link experience ready for integration'. I told stakeholders about this risk up front.
- **Outcome**: the API arrived on day 5, integration took two days, and we met the goal with a day to spare.

The lesson: a good sprint goal can survive uncertainty if planning makes the risk visible and agrees a fallback."

#### Variations you might be asked
- "How long should sprint planning take?" Usually up to 2 hours for a two-week sprint, much shorter when the backlog is well refined.
- "What do you do when the team can't agree on how much to take on?" Use recent throughput, and start with the sprint goal's essential stories, adding more only if capacity remains.
- "Should the PO attend the 'how' part of planning?" Yes, to answer questions, but the developers own the plan.

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Prepare** backlog, goal, capacity and dependencies.
2. **Why**: explain value and the draft goal.
3. **What**: team selects items based on capacity.
4. **How**: team plans the work.
5. **Confirm** the goal and plan.
6. **Share** the goal with stakeholders.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I tell the team which tickets to do." | Team decides how much, based on capacity |
| Goal = list of tickets | Goal = one clear, valuable objective |
| Unrefined backlog | Prepared, refined backlog |
| Plans to 100% capacity | Realistic plan based on throughput |

#### Useful phrases
- "This sprint, the goal I'd propose is…"
- "How much do you feel we can realistically take on?"
- "If this story turns out harder, can we still meet the goal with a smaller version?"
- "The sprint goal is our anchor for decisions during the sprint."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Coming unprepared.**
- **Sprint goals that are lists of tickets.**
- **Pushing the team to over-commit.**
- **No link** to the product goal.
- **Planning sessions that run very long** because refinement wasn't done.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if the team wants to include unrelated work?"**
  "That's fine for small items like bugs, as long as the sprint goal stays the priority."
- **"What if the sprint goal becomes obsolete mid-sprint?"**
  "The PO can cancel the sprint, but that's rare. Usually we adjust scope while keeping the goal."
- **"How do you estimate capacity?"**
  "From the team's average throughput or velocity over the last few sprints, adjusted for absences and other commitments."

- **"What if a stakeholder insists on adding work after planning?"**
  "I'd assess it against the sprint goal. If it's genuinely more valuable, we swap something out with the team's agreement and tell the affected stakeholders. Otherwise it goes to the top of the backlog for the next sprint."
- **"How do you know if your sprint goals are good?"**
  "Look back over several sprints: did the goals describe real value, did the team mostly meet them, and could stakeholders understand them without reading the tickets?"

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Using sprint goals to **connect daily work to strategy**.
- **Coaching** teams on goal-focused planning.
- Planning **across teams** with shared goals.

#### Practise it
1. Write three sprint goals for a product you know: two good, one weak, and explain the difference.
2. Describe how you'd prepare for planning.
3. Explain your approach **out loud in 3 minutes**.

## Scrum versus Kanban — when would you use each?
- id: po-agile-09
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: scrum, kanban, agile frameworks, flow

### Prompt
What are the differences between Scrum and Kanban? When would you use each?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Understanding of **different agile approaches**.
- Whether you choose methods based on **context**, not dogma.
- Practical knowledge of **flow metrics** and **work-in-progress limits**.

#### Key points
- **Scrum**: fixed-length **sprints**, defined **roles** (PO, Scrum Master, developers), **events**, and a **sprint goal**. Good for product development with planned, goal-focused increments.
- **Kanban**: a **continuous flow** of work, visualised on a board, with **work-in-progress (WIP) limits**, and focus on **flow metrics** (cycle time, throughput). No fixed sprints or prescribed roles. Good for unpredictable, continuous work.
- Many teams use a **hybrid** ("Scrumban").
- Choose based on **type of work**, **predictability** and **team needs**.

#### Model answer
"Both are agile approaches, but they organise work differently.

**Scrum:**
- Work happens in **fixed-length sprints**, typically two weeks.
- Each sprint has a **sprint goal**, planned at the start and reviewed at the end.
- Defined **roles**: Product Owner, Scrum Master, developers.
- Defined **events**: planning, daily scrum, review, retrospective.
- **Strengths**: rhythm, focus on goals, regular stakeholder feedback, built-in improvement.
- **Best for**: product development where work can be planned in increments, e.g. building new features toward a product goal.

**Kanban:**
- Work **flows continuously**. Items are pulled from a prioritised queue as capacity frees up.
- A **visual board** shows each stage (e.g. To do → In progress → Review → Done).
- **WIP limits** cap how many items can be in each stage, which reduces multitasking and exposes bottlenecks.
- Focus on **flow metrics**: **cycle time** (how long an item takes from start to finish), **throughput** (items completed per week), and **work item age**.
- **Strengths**: flexibility, handles unpredictable incoming work, continuous delivery.
- **Best for**: support and operations, maintenance, or teams with lots of unplanned work and frequent priority changes.

**How I'd choose:**
- A team building a new product toward quarterly goals → **Scrum** (or Scrum with Kanban practices).
- A platform or support team handling many unpredictable requests → **Kanban**.
- A team with both → a **hybrid**: sprints for planned work, with a Kanban lane and capacity reserved for urgent requests, and WIP limits to keep flow healthy.

**As a PO in Kanban**, I keep the queue **prioritised** continuously, review flow metrics with the team, and use regular **replenishment** meetings instead of sprint planning. I still hold regular reviews with stakeholders.

**Example.** A team I worked with handled both feature work and frequent integration requests from partners. Strict two-week sprints kept being disrupted. We moved to Kanban with WIP limits and a weekly replenishment meeting. Average cycle time for partner requests dropped from 12 days to 5, and predictability improved."

#### Another worked example: staying with Scrum
"A new product team considered Kanban because sprints felt 'too rigid'. Looking closer, the real problem was poor refinement, not the sprint structure. We improved refinement and kept Scrum. The rhythm of sprint goals and reviews was valuable for stakeholder alignment during the early product phase."

#### Scrum versus Kanban
| | Scrum | Kanban |
|---|---|---|
| Cadence | Fixed sprints | Continuous flow |
| Roles | PO, Scrum Master, developers | No prescribed roles |
| Planning | Sprint planning | Continuous replenishment |
| Change during work | Limited within a sprint | Any time (as capacity frees) |
| Key metrics | Sprint goal, velocity | Cycle time, throughput, WIP |
| Best for | Planned product increments | Unpredictable, continuous work |

#### A third worked example: a support-heavy team adopting Kanban practices
"A team owning both a customer-facing app and internal tools spent about 40% of every sprint on unplanned requests, so sprint commitments were meaningless.
- **Visualised the work**: a board with columns Ready → In progress → Review → Done, and separate swimlanes for 'planned' and 'urgent'.
- **Introduced WIP limits**: no more than 4 items in progress across the team, and 1 urgent item at a time.
- **Kept some Scrum elements**: a fortnightly review with stakeholders and a retrospective, but replaced sprint planning with a weekly 30-minute replenishment meeting.
- **Measured flow**: tracked cycle time and throughput weekly.
- **Result**: median cycle time fell from 9 days to 4, urgent requests were handled within 2 days on average, and stakeholders found forecasts based on throughput more reliable than the old sprint commitments."

#### Variations you might be asked
- "Can you be a Product Owner in a Kanban team?" Yes. The role still owns value and ordering of the queue; there's just no sprint backlog.
- "What metrics would you track in Kanban?" Cycle time, throughput, work item age, and work in progress.
- "What's Scrumban?" A hybrid that keeps Scrum's cadence of reviews and retrospectives while using Kanban's flow, WIP limits and continuous pull.

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Explain Scrum** briefly.
2. **Explain Kanban** briefly.
3. **Compare** key differences.
4. **Explain when to use each**, and hybrids.
5. **Describe your role** in each.
6. **Give an example.**

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "Scrum is better." | Chooses based on context |
| Doesn't know WIP limits | Explains WIP limits and flow metrics |
| No mention of hybrids | Knows teams often combine approaches |
| No example | Example of choosing or switching |

#### Useful phrases
- "Scrum gives rhythm and goals; Kanban gives flexibility and flow."
- "WIP limits help us finish work rather than start more."
- "I'd choose based on how predictable the team's work is."
- "In Kanban, I keep the queue prioritised continuously."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Dogmatism** ("we must do Scrum by the book").
- **Kanban without WIP limits**, which is just a to-do board.
- **Switching methods** to avoid fixing real problems.
- **Not knowing flow metrics.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What's cycle time?"**
  "How long an item takes from when work starts to when it's done. Shorter, more predictable cycle times mean faster delivery."
- **"What's Little's Law?"**
  "Average cycle time = average work in progress ÷ average throughput. Reducing WIP reduces cycle time."
- **"Can you have a sprint goal in Kanban?"**
  "Kanban doesn't require one, but teams can still set goals or themes for a period."

- **"How do you forecast delivery in Kanban without story points?"**
  "Using historical throughput: if the team finishes 6–8 items a week and there are 30 items left, that's roughly 4–5 weeks. Monte Carlo simulations can turn that into probabilities."
- **"How do you stop urgent work taking over in Kanban?"**
  "Set a WIP limit for the urgent lane, define what counts as urgent, and review the urgent work regularly to fix root causes."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Choosing **ways of working** based on team context.
- Using **flow metrics** for forecasting and improvement.
- **Coaching** teams on agile principles rather than rituals.

#### Practise it
1. List two teams (real or imagined) and explain which approach suits each.
2. Draw a Kanban board with WIP limits for a team you know.
3. Explain the differences **out loud in 3 minutes**.

## How do you break down a large initiative into epics, features and stories?
- id: po-agile-10
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: epics, story mapping, backlog structure, slicing

### Prompt
You have a large initiative that will take several months. How do you break it down into manageable pieces of work?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Ability to **decompose** big, vague initiatives into deliverable pieces.
- Whether you slice **vertically** (end-to-end value) rather than by technical layer.
- Use of tools like **story mapping**.
- Whether you plan for **early, incremental value**.

#### Key points
- A common hierarchy: **initiative / theme → epics → features → user stories → tasks**. (Terminology varies by company and tool.)
- **Story mapping** (Jeff Patton): map the user journey horizontally, list stories under each step, then slice **releases** horizontally.
- Slice **vertically**: each piece delivers end-to-end value, rather than "build the database first, then the API, then the UI".
- Prioritise slices to deliver a **walking skeleton / MVP** first, then enhance.
- Involve the **team** in breaking work down.

#### Model answer
"I'd break it down step by step, aiming to deliver value early rather than all at the end.

Say the initiative is **'Launch a self-service customer portal for an insurance company'**.

**1. Understand the goal.** Reduce call-centre volume for common requests by 30% within six months.

**2. Identify epics** (large chunks of value):
- Account access and login.
- View policy documents.
- Update personal details.
- Make a claim.
- Payments and billing.

**3. Story map the user journey.** With the team, I'd map the journey horizontally: *log in → view policy → make a change → confirm → receive confirmation*. Under each step, we list user stories, from essential to nice-to-have.

**4. Slice releases horizontally across the map:**
- **Release 1 (MVP)**: log in, view policy documents, update address. The most common call reasons, and simple to build.
- **Release 2**: update payment details, add a named driver.
- **Release 3**: start a claim online.
- **Later**: claim tracking, chat support, document upload.

**5. Break into user stories**, each **vertically sliced**. For example, 'As a policyholder, I want to update my address, so that my documents go to the right place.' That includes the UI, the API and the database changes needed, so it delivers working value on its own.

**6. Refine and split further** with the team until stories fit in a sprint, using techniques like splitting by workflow step, business rule or user type.

**7. Track progress** at epic level against the goal (call volume), not just stories completed.

**Why vertical slicing matters.** If we built all the database work first, then all the APIs, then all the screens, customers would see nothing for months, and we'd learn nothing. Vertical slices let us release, measure and adjust early."

#### Another worked example: a technical initiative
"Initiative: 'Migrate to a new payments provider.'
- Epics: card payments, refunds, recurring payments, reporting.
- Slicing: migrate **one payment type for a small percentage of customers** first, with monitoring, then expand.
- This keeps risk low and lets us learn about the new provider early."

#### Work hierarchy (typical)
| Level | Example | Timescale |
|---|---|---|
| Initiative / theme | Self-service portal | Months to a year |
| Epic | Update personal details | Weeks to months |
| Feature | Change address | Weeks |
| User story | Update address with confirmation | Days |
| Task | Build address validation | Hours |

#### A third worked example: breaking down a platform migration
"Initiative: 'Move customer notifications (email, SMS, push) to a new messaging service.'
- **Epics by channel and capability**: email migration, SMS migration, push migration, preference centre, monitoring.
- **Vertical first slice**: migrate one low-risk email type (password resets) end to end for 5% of users, with monitoring and a rollback switch. This proves the whole path works before scaling.
- **Next slices**: all transactional emails, then marketing emails, then SMS, then push, each with its own success criteria (delivery rate, latency).
- **Story example**: 'As a customer, I want to receive my password-reset email within 1 minute, so that I can get back into my account quickly' (covers template, sending, tracking and fallback to the old service).
- **Tracking**: an epic-level dashboard of delivery rate and percentage of traffic migrated, rather than counting stories."

#### Variations you might be asked
- "What's the difference between an epic and a feature?" Terminology varies; typically an epic is a large body of work spanning several releases, and a feature is a shippable capability within it.
- "How do you keep a story map up to date?" Review it in refinement and after each release, moving completed slices and adding new learning.
- "Who should be in a story-mapping session?" The product trio at minimum, plus key engineers, and stakeholders for context.

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Clarify the goal** of the initiative.
2. **Identify epics.**
3. **Story map** the user journey with the team.
4. **Slice releases**, starting with an MVP.
5. **Write vertically sliced stories.**
6. **Split** until stories fit in a sprint.
7. **Track progress** against the goal.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "Break it into tasks." | Clear hierarchy and story mapping |
| Slices by technical layer | Vertical slices delivering end-to-end value |
| Everything released at the end | Incremental releases starting with an MVP |
| PO breaks it down alone | Team involved in mapping and splitting |

#### Useful phrases
- "I'd story map the user journey with the team first."
- "Each slice should deliver end-to-end value on its own."
- "Release 1 covers the most common needs with the simplest scope."
- "We'd track progress against the goal, not just stories completed."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Horizontal slicing** by technical layer.
- **Huge epics** with no incremental releases.
- **Breaking down too far ahead**, wasting effort on work that may change.
- **Losing sight of the goal** in the detail.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What's a walking skeleton?"**
  "The thinnest end-to-end version of a system that works, connecting all the main parts, which you then flesh out."
- **"How far ahead do you break work down?"**
  "Epics for the next quarter or two; detailed stories only for the next couple of sprints."
- **"How do you track progress on large initiatives?"**
  "Epic-level progress and, more importantly, outcome metrics, with regular reviews against milestones."

- **"How do you decide what goes in the first release?"**
  "The thinnest slice that delivers real value and tests the riskiest assumption, often the most common user journey with the simplest scope."
- **"What if a vertical slice is too big for one sprint?"**
  "Split it further by business rule, data type or user path, while keeping each piece end to end. If that's impossible, use a short spike to reduce uncertainty first."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Decomposing large programmes** across multiple teams.
- **Release strategy** that balances value, risk and learning.
- Connecting work breakdown to **outcomes and roadmaps**.

#### Practise it
1. Pick a large initiative (e.g. "add multi-currency support") and break it into epics.
2. Create a simple story map with three releases.
3. Explain your breakdown **out loud in 4 minutes**.

## What is a spike, and when do you use one?
- id: po-agile-11
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: spike, uncertainty, research, estimation

### Prompt
What is a spike in agile development? When would you use one, and how do you make sure it's useful?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Understanding of how to **handle uncertainty** in agile.
- Whether you **time-box** research and define **clear questions**.
- Whether you know when a spike is **appropriate** versus wasteful.

#### Key points
- A **spike** is a **time-boxed investigation** to answer a specific question or reduce uncertainty, so the team can estimate or decide.
- Types: **technical spikes** (can we do this? how?) and **functional spikes** (what should it do? how will users react?).
- Define a **clear question**, a **time-box** (e.g. 1–3 days), and the **expected output** (a recommendation, prototype, estimate).
- Use spikes when **uncertainty blocks estimation or decisions**, not as an excuse to delay.

#### Model answer
"A spike is a **short, time-boxed investigation** to answer a specific question, usually because uncertainty is stopping us from estimating or deciding.

**When I use one:**
- **Technical uncertainty**: 'Can our system integrate with this bank's API, and what are its limitations?'
- **Choice between approaches**: 'Should we build search ourselves or use a hosted service?'
- **Functional uncertainty**: 'Will users understand this new flow?' (a quick prototype test).
- When estimates vary wildly in refinement, e.g. one engineer says 3 points and another says 20, a spike can explain why.

**How I make it useful:**
1. **A clear question**: e.g. 'Can we retrieve transaction data from Bank X's API in under 2 seconds, and what limits apply?'
2. **A time-box**: e.g. 2 days. When it's up, we stop and share what we found, even if it's incomplete.
3. **A defined output**: a short summary with findings, a recommendation, and if possible an estimate for the real work.
4. **Plan it like other work**: it goes in the sprint backlog with its time-box.
5. **Share results** with the team and use them to refine the next stories.

**Example.** Before committing to an open banking integration, we ran a 3-day spike. It revealed that one major bank's API had rate limits that would slow down syncing for large accounts. We adjusted our design (background syncing with progress updates) before building, which avoided a likely failure in production."

#### Another worked example: a functional spike
"We weren't sure whether customers would understand a new pricing page layout. A 2-day spike created two clickable prototypes, tested with 5 users each. One layout was clearly understood, so we built that version and avoided redesigning after launch."

#### Spike checklist
| Element | Example |
|---|---|
| Question | "Can we sync Bank X transactions in < 2 seconds?" |
| Time-box | 2–3 days |
| Output | Findings, recommendation, estimate |
| Owner | One or two engineers (or designer for functional) |
| Follow-up | Refine and estimate the real stories |

#### A third worked example: a spike that changed the plan
"We planned a 'live chat with support' feature, estimated at 3 sprints. Engineers were unsure whether our existing helpdesk tool's chat widget could be customised to match our app.
- **Spike question**: 'Can the helpdesk chat widget be embedded in our mobile app with our branding and pass the logged-in customer's ID securely?'
- **Time-box**: 2 days, one engineer.
- **Findings**: the widget could be embedded, but customising it fully required a higher pricing tier, and passing customer IDs needed a small backend change.
- **Decision**: use the widget with light branding (no tier upgrade), plus the backend change. New estimate: 1 sprint instead of 3.
- **Value**: 2 days of investigation saved about 4 weeks of building a custom chat."

#### How to describe a spike in an interview
- Name the **question**, the **time-box** and the **decision** it enabled.
- Quantify the **value**: time saved, risk avoided, or a better choice made.
- Mention what you did with the **results** (refined stories, updated estimates, shared with stakeholders).

#### Variations you might be asked
- "Who decides when a spike is needed?" Usually the team proposes it in refinement; the PO agrees because it affects capacity.
- "How do you stop spikes becoming a habit?" Only use them when uncertainty blocks a decision, and always time-box them.

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Identify the uncertainty** blocking a decision or estimate.
2. **Write a clear question.**
3. **Set a time-box** and expected output.
4. **Plan it** in the sprint.
5. **Share results** and refine follow-up work.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "A spike is research." | Time-boxed, with a clear question and output |
| Open-ended investigation | Strict time-box |
| No output | Findings, recommendation and estimate |
| Used to delay decisions | Used when uncertainty blocks progress |

#### Useful phrases
- "The question we're answering is…"
- "We'll time-box this to two days and share whatever we learn."
- "The output will be a recommendation and an estimate for the real work."

#### Common pitfalls
- **No time-box**, so spikes drag on.
- **Vague questions** that don't lead to decisions.
- **Spikes for everything**, slowing delivery.
- **Not sharing results.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"Should spikes have story points?"**
  "Some teams estimate them, others just time-box them. I prefer a clear time-box, so the team knows how much capacity it uses."
- **"What if the spike doesn't answer the question?"**
  "Share what was learned, and decide whether another short spike is worth it or whether we can proceed with the remaining risk."
- **"Spike versus prototype?"**
  "A prototype can be one output of a spike. The spike is the time-boxed investigation; the prototype is a tool to answer the question."

- **"Should spike code go to production?"**
  "Usually not. Spike code is for learning and is often thrown away. If parts are reused, they should be rewritten to production standards."
- **"How would you explain a spike to a stakeholder who wants progress?"**
  "It's a small investment to avoid a big mistake: two days now can save weeks of building the wrong thing or hitting a surprise later."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Managing **uncertainty systematically** in planning.
- Balancing **learning and delivery**.
- Using spikes to **de-risk** large initiatives early.

#### Practise it
1. Write three spike questions for uncertain work in a product you know.
2. Add a time-box and expected output for each.
3. Explain what a spike is **out loud in 90 seconds**.
