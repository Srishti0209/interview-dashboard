# Product Owner — Behavioural (continued)

## Tell me about a time you improved a process
- id: po-behavioural-13
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: process improvement, continuous improvement, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you noticed a process wasn't working well and improved it.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Initiative**: do you notice problems and act, rather than accept them?
- **Diagnosis**: did you understand the root cause before changing things?
- **Collaboration**: did you involve the people affected?
- **Measurable impact** and whether the change stuck.

#### Key points
- Pick a process with **visible pain**: slow releases, chaotic intake, unclear requirements, long meetings.
- Show how you **measured the problem** before fixing it.
- Involve the **people who use the process** in designing the fix.
- **Pilot** the change, then **measure** the result.
- Show the change **lasted**.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** In my previous team, feature requests arrived from everywhere: emails, Slack messages, hallway conversations and meetings. Stakeholders didn't know what had happened to their requests, the team was regularly interrupted, and I spent hours each week chasing and re-explaining decisions.

**Task.** I wanted to make intake predictable for stakeholders and protect the team's focus, without adding bureaucracy.

**Action.**
- **Measured the problem**: for two weeks, I logged every request: 47 requests from 12 people across five channels, and at least 9 developer interruptions.
- **Talked to stakeholders and the team**: stakeholders wanted visibility and a fair hearing; developers wanted fewer interruptions.
- **Designed a simple process together**:
  - One short **request form** (problem, who's affected, impact, deadline).
  - A shared **board** showing every request's status (new, reviewing, planned, not now).
  - A **fortnightly 30-minute review** with key stakeholders.
  - A **fast track** for genuine emergencies, with clear criteria.
- **Piloted it** for one month with the three busiest stakeholder groups, then adjusted (e.g. we shortened the form after feedback).
- **Communicated** the new process and why it helped everyone.

**Result.**
- Developer interruptions dropped by about 70%.
- Stakeholders said in a survey they felt more informed, and duplicate requests fell because they could see what already existed.
- I saved about 4 hours a week on chasing and re-explaining.
- A year later, other teams had adopted the same intake process."

#### Another worked example: speeding up releases
"**Situation.** Releases needed sign-off from four people and often waited days.
**Action.** I mapped the release steps with the team, found that two approvals were duplicates, and agreed with QA and compliance to replace manual sign-off for low-risk changes with automated checks.
**Result.** Release lead time dropped from about 5 days to 1 day for most changes."

#### A third example: improving customer research
"**Situation.** Our team only spoke to customers occasionally, through big research projects every few months, so decisions relied on assumptions.
**Action.** I introduced a lightweight habit: two 30-minute customer conversations every week, recruited through an in-app invitation, with an engineer or designer joining each one. Notes went into a shared board, summarised monthly.
**Result.** Within a quarter, every team member had joined at least three interviews, two planned features were changed based on insights, and the team said decisions felt more grounded."

#### Make it your own
- Pick a process that caused **visible pain** (time wasted, errors, frustration).
- Show how you **measured** it, **involved others** and **piloted** the change.
- Quantify the **improvement** and whether it **spread** or lasted.

#### The 30-second version
"Requests reached my team through emails, Slack and corridor conversations, interrupting developers constantly. I logged them for two weeks (47 requests from 12 people), then designed a simple process with stakeholders and the team: one request form, a shared status board, a fortnightly review and a fast track for emergencies. After a month's pilot, developer interruptions dropped about 70%, stakeholders felt better informed, and other teams later adopted it."

Lead with the numbers in the short version, because they make the impact memorable.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | The broken process and its impact |
| Task | What you wanted to achieve |
| Action | Measurement, involvement, design, pilot, communication |
| Result | Measured improvement and adoption |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I introduced Jira." | Diagnosed the problem before choosing a fix |
| Imposed a new process | Designed it with the people affected |
| No measurement | Measured before and after |
| Didn't last | Change stuck and spread |

#### Useful phrases
- "I logged the problem for two weeks to understand its size."
- "We designed the process together with the people who'd use it."
- "We piloted it before rolling it out."
- "Interruptions dropped by about 70%."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Adding bureaucracy** instead of removing pain.
- **Imposing** changes without input.
- **No evidence** of improvement.
- **Trivial examples.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What resistance did you face?"**
  "Some stakeholders felt the form was extra work, so we shortened it and showed them how much faster they got answers."
- **"How did you keep it going?"**
  "Regular reviews, and showing results in retrospectives and stakeholder meetings."
- **"What would you do differently?"**
  "Involve the team in measuring the problem from the start, which builds ownership."

- **"How do you get buy-in for process changes?"**
  "Show the cost of the current process with data, involve the people affected in designing the change, and start with a pilot."
- **"What process change didn't work, and why?"**
  Have an honest example: what you tried, why it didn't stick, and what you learned.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Systems thinking** and scaling improvements across teams.
- **Change management** skills.
- **Continuous improvement** as a habit.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a process you made better.
2. Write the before and after metrics.
3. Practise **out loud in under 2.5 minutes**.

## Tell me about a time you dealt with ambiguity
- id: po-behavioural-14
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: ambiguity, structure, problem framing, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you were given a vague or ambiguous goal. How did you make progress?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you can **create structure** from ambiguity.
- How you **clarify goals** with stakeholders.
- Whether you **make progress** rather than waiting for clarity.
- Comfort with **uncertainty**.

#### Key points
- Show how you **clarified the goal**: questions, stakeholders, success criteria.
- Show how you **broke the problem down** and gathered evidence.
- Show **early progress** and iteration.
- Show how you **kept stakeholders aligned** as things became clearer.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** Shortly after I joined a retail company, the CEO asked my team to 'make the app more engaging'. There was no specific metric, scope or deadline.

**Task.** I needed to turn this into a clear, measurable goal and a plan the team could act on.

**Action.**
- **Clarified intent**: I met the CEO and asked what prompted the request and what success would look like. The real concern was that app users bought less frequently than web users, and app usage was dropping.
- **Defined a measurable goal**: we agreed on 'increase monthly purchase frequency among app users by 10% within six months', with app retention as a supporting metric.
- **Gathered evidence quickly**: analysed app data (drop-offs, feature usage), read reviews, and ran 8 customer interviews in two weeks.
- **Framed opportunities**: three themes emerged: slow product browsing, irrelevant notifications, and a poor reorder experience.
- **Started small**: prioritised improving reorders first (quick to build, clear link to purchase frequency), and tested it with 10% of users.
- **Kept stakeholders aligned**: shared a one-page summary of the goal, insights and plan, and gave fortnightly updates.

**Result.**
- The reorder improvement increased purchase frequency in the test group by 7%.
- Combined with better notifications, we reached a 12% increase over six months.
- The CEO said the structured approach turned a vague concern into visible progress.
- Lesson: ask 'what would success look like?' early, then start with a small, measurable step."

#### Another worked example: an ambiguous new role
"**Situation.** I joined a newly formed team with no defined product area.
**Action.** I interviewed stakeholders, mapped customer journeys to find gaps no team owned, and proposed a mission and three focus areas to leadership.
**Result.** Leadership approved the mission within a month, and the team had a clear backlog by week six."

#### A third example: an ambiguous technical goal
"**Situation.** Leadership asked my team to 'make the platform more scalable' ahead of expected growth.
**Action.** With the tech lead, I clarified the expected growth (five times current traffic in 18 months), identified which parts would fail first through load testing, and defined measurable targets (response times under load). We prioritised the two biggest bottlenecks.
**Result.** After fixing them, load tests passed at five times current traffic, and a big marketing campaign later in the year ran without performance issues."

#### Make it your own
- Choose a request that was **genuinely vague**.
- Show how you **clarified intent** and turned it into a **measurable goal**.
- Describe the **first small step** and how it built momentum.
- Explain how you **kept stakeholders aligned**.

#### The 30-second version
"The CEO asked us to 'make the app more engaging' with no metric or scope. I asked what prompted it and learned app users bought less often than web users. We agreed a goal of increasing app purchase frequency by 10% in six months, ran quick research, and started with an improved reorder experience tested on 10% of users. That raised purchase frequency 7% in the test, and with better notifications we reached 12% over six months."

The short version shows the key skill quickly: turning a vague request into a measurable goal and a first step.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | The vague goal |
| Task | What you needed to define |
| Action | Clarify intent, measurable goal, evidence, small first step, alignment |
| Result | Progress and lesson |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| Waited for clearer instructions | Proactively clarified intent |
| Started building immediately | Defined a measurable goal first |
| Big plan, no early progress | Small first step with measurable results |
| No stakeholder alignment | Regular communication |

#### Useful phrases
- "I asked what prompted the request and what success would look like."
- "We turned 'more engaging' into a measurable goal."
- "We started with the smallest step that could show progress."
- "I shared a one-page summary to keep everyone aligned."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Waiting** for someone else to clarify.
- **Over-planning** without action.
- **Building** without understanding the goal.
- **Not checking back** with the stakeholder.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if the CEO hadn't agreed on a metric?"**
  "I'd propose one based on evidence and ask for feedback; having a concrete proposal usually speeds up agreement."
- **"How do you stay productive in ambiguity?"**
  "Break the problem into questions, answer the most important ones first, and make reversible progress."
- **"What do you find hardest about ambiguity?"**
  "Resisting the urge to jump to solutions too early, which is why I focus on defining the goal first."

- **"What do you do if stakeholders disagree on what the goal means?"**
  "Bring them together, show the options and evidence, and agree a single measurable goal, escalating to a shared leader if needed."
- **"How do you avoid over-analysing in ambiguous situations?"**
  "Time-box discovery and aim for a reversible first step that produces real learning."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Strategic framing** of ambiguous problems.
- **Leading others** through ambiguity.
- **Comfort** with incomplete information.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a vague request you turned into a clear plan.
2. Write the original request, the measurable goal you agreed, and the first step.
3. Practise **out loud in under 3 minutes**.

## Tell me about a time you had to learn a new domain quickly
- id: po-behavioural-15
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: learning, domain knowledge, adaptability, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you had to get up to speed quickly in an unfamiliar domain or industry.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Learning agility**: how quickly and effectively you learn.
- Your **learning strategy**: sources, people, practice.
- Whether you **delivered value** while learning.
- **Humility**: willingness to ask questions.

#### Key points
- Describe the **domain** and why it was challenging.
- Explain your **learning plan**: experts, customers, documents, hands-on use.
- Show **early contribution** despite being new.
- Show how quickly you became **effective**, with evidence.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** I moved from e-commerce to a role as Product Owner for a pensions platform. Pensions involve complex regulations, tax rules and terminology I'd never worked with.

**Task.** I needed to become credible with the team, compliance and stakeholders quickly, and start making good product decisions within a couple of months.

**Action.**
- **Made a learning plan** for my first six weeks:
  - **Experts**: weekly sessions with a compliance specialist and a senior pensions administrator, preparing questions in advance.
  - **Customers**: listened to 15 customer support calls and interviewed 6 customers about how they understood their pensions.
  - **Documents**: read regulator guidance summaries and our internal training material.
  - **Hands-on**: went through every customer journey in our product, and shadowed the operations team for a day.
- **Created a glossary** of key terms and shared it with the team, which helped other new joiners too.
- **Asked 'naive' questions** openly. They often exposed assumptions others had stopped noticing.
- **Contributed early** where my background helped: applying e-commerce experience in conversion and simplicity, while relying on experts for regulatory detail.

**Result.**
- Within two months, I led the redesign of the pension transfer journey, which had a 40% drop-off. Using customer insight and clearer explanations (reviewed by compliance), completion improved by 22%.
- Stakeholders said my outsider's view brought useful simplicity.
- Lesson: combine learning from experts and customers, and bring your existing strengths while you learn."

#### Another worked example: a technical domain
"**Situation.** I became PO for a data platform used by internal analysts.
**Action.** I paired with engineers for a week, took a short online course on data pipelines, and interviewed analysts about their daily work.
**Result.** Within a month, I prioritised a data-freshness improvement that analysts said saved them hours each week."

#### A third example: learning a B2B domain from a consumer background
"**Situation.** Moving from consumer apps to software for logistics companies, I knew nothing about warehouse operations.
**Action.** I spent two days on-site at a customer's warehouse shadowing pickers and supervisors, listened to customer success calls, and asked engineers to walk me through the data model.
**Result.** I spotted that supervisors spent hours re-assigning tasks manually, which became a high-value feature saving each warehouse several hours a week."

#### Make it your own
- Pick a domain, tool or industry that was **truly new** to you.
- Describe a **structured learning plan** (people, customers, documents, hands-on).
- Show an **early contribution** and how your existing skills helped.
- Mention how you **shared what you learned** with others.

#### The 30-second version
"I moved from e-commerce to owning a pensions platform with no pensions experience. I made a six-week learning plan: weekly sessions with compliance and pensions experts, listening to support calls, interviewing customers, and going through every journey myself. Within two months, I led a redesign of the pension transfer journey, using my e-commerce experience in simplicity with compliance's guidance, and completion improved by 22%."

Use the short version to show your learning approach and early impact; expand on your learning plan if asked.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | The new domain and its complexity |
| Task | How quickly you needed to be effective |
| Action | Learning plan (experts, customers, documents, hands-on), early contributions |
| Result | Evidence of impact and learning speed |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I read a lot." | Structured plan: experts, customers, hands-on |
| Waited until expert to contribute | Contributed early using existing strengths |
| Afraid to ask questions | Asked naive questions openly |
| No evidence | Measurable result within weeks |

#### Useful phrases
- "I made a learning plan for my first six weeks."
- "I combined learning from experts with time listening to customers."
- "Asking 'naive' questions exposed assumptions others had stopped noticing."
- "I applied my e-commerce experience in simplicity while experts covered regulation."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Passive learning** only (reading).
- **Pretending to know** more than you do.
- **No early impact.**
- **Generic answers** without a specific domain.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How would you learn our domain?"**
  "Talk to customers and frontline teams, pair with domain experts, use the product deeply, and build a glossary, with specific steps for your industry."
- **"How do you know when you know enough?"**
  "When I can explain key concepts simply, anticipate stakeholder concerns, and make decisions that experts agree with."
- **"What's the risk of an outsider's view?"**
  "Missing important constraints, so I always validate ideas with experts."

- **"What's your approach to learning our product in the first month?"**
  "Use it deeply, talk to customers and frontline teams, study key metrics, and pair with experts on the trickiest parts."
- **"What's something you learned recently outside work that helps you as a PO?"**
  Have a genuine example (a course, book or hobby) and connect it to your work.

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Rapid credibility** with experts and leadership.
- **Transferable skills** applied to new contexts.
- **Sharing knowledge** to help others learn.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a time you learned a new domain, tool or industry fast.
2. Write your learning plan and the first impact you made.
3. Practise **out loud in under 2.5 minutes**.

## Tell me about a time you kept a team motivated
- id: po-behavioural-16
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: motivation, team morale, leadership, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time your team's morale was low. What did you do as Product Owner?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Empathy** and awareness of team health.
- Whether you understand what **motivates** product teams (purpose, autonomy, progress).
- Specific **actions** within a PO's influence.
- **Results** for both morale and delivery.

#### Key points
- Identify the **cause** of low morale (unclear purpose, constant changes, no visible impact, pressure, failed launch).
- Act on things a PO can influence: **purpose and context**, **protecting focus**, **showing customer impact**, **involving the team in decisions**, **celebrating progress**.
- Work with the **Scrum Master and managers** on wider issues.
- Show the **result**.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** After a major launch was delayed twice and then received lukewarm customer feedback, my team's morale was low. In the retrospective, engineers said they felt their work didn't matter, priorities kept changing, and they never heard from customers.

**Task.** As PO, I wanted to rebuild a sense of purpose and stability, and get the team back to delivering confidently.

**Action.**
- **Listened properly**: held one-to-ones with each team member to understand what was frustrating them, together with the Scrum Master.
- **Reconnected the team with customers**: invited engineers to join customer interviews, and started sharing short customer clips and quotes in sprint reviews, including positive feedback from users who did benefit from the launch.
- **Stabilised priorities**: agreed with stakeholders a quarter of focused goals, and protected the team from mid-sprint changes using a clear intake process.
- **Gave the team more ownership**: framed work as problems to solve rather than specifications, and let the team propose solutions for the next improvement.
- **Made progress visible**: set achievable sprint goals and celebrated reaching them, plus shared metric improvements as they happened.

**Result.**
- Within two sprints, the team was meeting sprint goals consistently.
- The team's solution to a key launch problem improved activation for the feature by 18%.
- In the next team health check, scores for 'purpose' and 'clarity' improved significantly.
- Lesson: as a PO, the biggest motivators I can influence are clear purpose, stable priorities and visible customer impact."

#### Another worked example: crunch time
"**Situation.** Before a regulatory deadline, the team was exhausted.
**Action.** I cut non-essential scope, shielded them from new requests, explained the importance of the deadline, and made sure they took time off afterwards. I also publicly credited the team to leadership.
**Result.** The deadline was met without weekend work, and the team felt supported rather than pressured."

#### A third example: morale after a reorganisation
"**Situation.** After a reorganisation, my new team had lost two respected colleagues and felt uncertain about their purpose.
**Action.** With the engineering manager, I ran a session to define the team's mission and the customer problems we owned, shared a draft roadmap and asked for input, and set a quick, achievable goal for the first sprint.
**Result.** The team delivered its first goal, felt ownership of the mission, and engagement scores recovered within two months."

#### Make it your own
- Identify **signs and causes** of low morale.
- Focus on what **a PO can influence**: purpose, clarity, stability, ownership, visible impact.
- Mention **partnering** with the Scrum Master or engineering manager.
- Show **results** for morale and delivery.

#### The 30-second version
"After a delayed and lukewarm launch, my team felt their work didn't matter and priorities kept changing. With the Scrum Master, I held one-to-ones, brought engineers into customer interviews and shared positive feedback, stabilised priorities for the quarter, and gave the team ownership of the next problem to solve. Within two sprints we were meeting goals again, the team's solution improved activation by 18%, and our team health scores rose."

Focus the short version on causes, the actions within your control as PO, and the result.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | Signs and causes of low morale |
| Task | What you aimed to change |
| Action | Listening, purpose, stability, ownership, visible progress |
| Result | Morale and delivery outcomes |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I organised team drinks." | Addressed root causes (purpose, stability, ownership) |
| Ignored causes | Listened to understand them |
| Pushed harder | Protected the team's focus |
| No result | Improved morale and delivery |

#### Useful phrases
- "I wanted to understand what was really behind the low morale."
- "We reconnected the team with the customers they were helping."
- "I protected the team from mid-sprint changes."
- "I framed work as problems for the team to solve."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Superficial fixes** (perks, pep talks).
- **Ignoring PO-side causes** like changing priorities.
- **Taking credit** for the team's recovery.
- **No measurable outcome.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What's the difference between your role and the manager's in morale?"**
  "Managers handle individual growth and wellbeing; as PO I influence purpose, clarity, stability and visible impact, and I work with managers on the rest."
- **"How do you spot low morale early?"**
  "Quieter retrospectives, less challenge in refinement, slipping commitments, and one-to-one conversations."
- **"How do you celebrate success?"**
  "Share results and customer feedback, credit the team publicly, and mark milestones."

- **"How do you keep motivation up during long projects?"**
  "Break work into milestones, show progress against customer outcomes, share customer feedback early, and celebrate steps along the way."
- **"How do you handle your own frustration in front of the team?"**
  "Acknowledge challenges honestly but stay focused on solutions, and share frustrations privately with the right people."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Leadership through purpose** across teams.
- **Partnering with engineering managers** on team health.
- **Sustainable pace** as a priority.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a time a team struggled and you helped.
2. Write the causes, your actions, and evidence of improvement.
3. Practise **out loud in under 3 minutes**.

## Tell me about a time you made an unpopular decision
- id: po-behavioural-17
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: tough decisions, courage, communication, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you made a decision that was unpopular with your team or stakeholders. How did you handle it?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Courage** to make the right call even when it's unpopular.
- Whether the decision was **grounded in evidence and goals**.
- How you **communicated** and handled pushback.
- Whether you **maintained relationships** and reviewed the outcome.

#### Key points
- Choose a decision that was **genuinely right for the business or customers**, despite resistance.
- Show you **listened** to objections and considered them.
- Explain the **reasoning** clearly and transparently.
- Show **empathy** for those affected.
- Share the **outcome**, and whether you'd make the same call again.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** At a B2B software company, our team had spent three months building an advanced reporting module that several stakeholders, including the Head of Sales, were excited about. During the beta, usage data showed only 6% of beta customers used it more than once, and interviews revealed most customers exported data to their own tools instead.

**Task.** I had to decide whether to launch it fully, which would require another two months of work, or stop and redirect the team.

**Action.**
- **Gathered evidence**: beta usage data, 10 customer interviews, and support tickets.
- **Listened to objections**: the Head of Sales argued it was a selling point in demos; some engineers were proud of the work and frustrated about stopping.
- **Considered alternatives**: we could keep a simplified version, or invest in a robust data export and integrations, which customers actually asked for.
- **Made the decision**: stop further investment in the reporting module, keep the two most-used reports, and redirect the team to build **data export and BI integrations**.
- **Communicated openly**: explained the data and reasoning to stakeholders and the team, acknowledged the effort the team had invested, and gave sales a clear story about the integrations.
- **Followed up**: tracked results and shared them.

**Result.**
- The export and integration features were adopted by 35% of accounts within two months, and sales used them as a selling point for larger customers.
- Maintenance burden stayed low because we didn't build the full reporting module.
- The Head of Sales later agreed it was the right call.
- Lesson: an unpopular decision is easier to accept when the evidence is shared openly and people feel heard."

#### Another worked example: an unpopular process decision
"**Situation.** I decided to stop accepting ad-hoc requests from one department directly, which annoyed them.
**Action.** I explained the impact on delivery, set up a weekly slot for their requests, and responded quickly.
**Result.** After a few weeks, they preferred the predictability, and the team's delivery improved."

#### A third example: an unpopular sunset
"**Situation.** I decided to retire a legacy mobile app version still used by about 4% of customers, because maintaining it slowed every release.
**Action.** I shared usage and cost data with stakeholders, planned a three-month transition, contacted affected users with clear instructions, and worked with support on a help guide. Some stakeholders worried about complaints, so we monitored feedback weekly.
**Result.** Over 90% of affected users updated during the transition, complaints were minimal, and release cycles sped up because we stopped testing the old version."

#### Make it your own
- Choose a decision that **disappointed some people** but was right.
- Show the **evidence**, the **objections you heard**, and how you **communicated**.
- Mention **empathy**: acknowledging effort or impact on others.
- Share the **outcome** and what you'd do differently.

#### The 30-second version
"We'd spent three months on a reporting module that stakeholders loved, but beta data showed only 6% of customers used it more than once; most exported data to their own tools. I stopped further work on it, kept the two most-used reports, and redirected the team to exports and BI integrations, explaining the data openly and acknowledging the team's effort. The integrations were adopted by 35% of accounts in two months, and the sales lead later agreed it was the right call."

The short version should make the evidence and the outcome clear; expand on how you handled objections if asked.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | The decision and why it was unpopular |
| Task | Your responsibility |
| Action | Evidence, listening, alternatives, decision, communication, empathy |
| Result | Outcome and reflection |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I just told them it was my decision." | Explained with evidence and listened |
| No empathy | Acknowledged effort and disappointment |
| Decision based on opinion | Decision grounded in data and goals |
| No outcome | Results and reflection shared |

#### Useful phrases
- "The data showed customers weren't using it the way we'd hoped."
- "I listened to the objections and considered alternatives."
- "I acknowledged the effort the team had put in."
- "Sharing the evidence openly helped people accept the decision."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Stories where you were simply stubborn.**
- **No evidence** behind the decision.
- **Ignoring people's feelings.**
- **No follow-up** on the outcome.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How did the team feel?"**
  "Disappointed at first. I acknowledged their work publicly and involved them in designing the new direction, which helped."
- **"Would you make the same decision again?"**
  "Yes, but I'd run the beta earlier to avoid three months of investment before learning."
- **"What if the decision had turned out wrong?"**
  "I'd own it, share what we learned, and adjust. That's why I set up tracking to review the outcome."

- **"How do you know an unpopular decision is right?"**
  "When it's grounded in evidence and goals, alternatives were considered, and the people affected were heard. And I review the outcome afterwards."
- **"Have you ever reversed an unpopular decision?"**
  "Yes, when new evidence showed it was wrong. Changing course based on evidence is a strength, not a weakness."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Courage** combined with **empathy**.
- **Evidence-based leadership.**
- **Owning outcomes** of tough calls.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a decision that disappointed people but was right.
2. Write the evidence, objections, and how you communicated.
3. Practise **out loud in under 3 minutes**.

## Tell me about a time you went above and beyond for a customer
- id: po-behavioural-18
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: customer focus, empathy, ownership, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you went above and beyond to solve a problem for a customer.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Customer empathy** and ownership.
- Whether you solve the **root cause** rather than one-off fixes.
- Whether you **scale** the solution to help other customers too.
- Your judgement about **when** going above and beyond is worth it.

#### Key points
- Choose a story where you **personally engaged** with a customer problem.
- Show you **understood the real need**.
- Show **ownership**: coordinating across teams to fix it.
- Ideally, show how you **turned it into a product improvement** that helped many customers.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** At an invoicing company, a small bakery owner contacted support for the third time because invoices sent to one large supermarket client were being rejected by the client's accounts system. Each rejection delayed her payments by weeks, and she was close to cancelling.

**Task.** It wasn't technically my job to handle individual support cases, but it looked like a product issue, and I wanted to understand it.

**Action.**
- **Called the customer directly** with a support agent. She explained that the supermarket required a specific purchase-order reference format and a particular invoice layout.
- **Investigated with engineering**: our invoices didn't support a dedicated purchase-order field; customers were typing references into notes, which the supermarket's system ignored.
- **Fixed her immediate problem**: worked with an engineer to create a temporary invoice template for her within two days, so her pending invoices could be reissued.
- **Looked for the wider pattern**: searched support tickets and found over 200 similar complaints about large clients rejecting invoices due to missing purchase-order numbers.
- **Turned it into a product improvement**: prioritised a proper purchase-order field and invoice-format options in the next sprint.

**Result.**
- The bakery owner was paid within a week and stayed a customer; she later wrote a glowing review.
- The purchase-order feature was used by 18% of customers within three months, and related support tickets dropped by 80%.
- Lesson: one customer's problem is often a signal of a wider issue worth fixing properly."

#### Another worked example: an accessibility issue
"**Situation.** A visually impaired customer emailed that they couldn't complete a payment using a screen reader.
**Action.** I met them over a video call, observed the issue, and worked with the team to fix the inaccessible form within a sprint, then added accessibility checks to the Definition of Done.
**Result.** The customer completed their payment, and similar issues were prevented across the product."

#### A third example: a vulnerable customer
"**Situation.** An elderly customer called support repeatedly because they couldn't complete identity verification in our banking app.
**Action.** I joined a call to observe, found the camera-based verification was hard for people with shaky hands, and worked with the team to add an alternative verification option in a branch or by video call.
**Result.** The customer completed verification, and the alternative helped hundreds of other customers who had previously abandoned onboarding."

#### Make it your own
- Pick a moment you **personally engaged** with a customer problem.
- Show the **immediate fix** and the **wider improvement** that followed.
- Quantify the **broader impact** where possible.
- Show **judgement**: why this was worth going above and beyond.

#### The 30-second version
"A bakery owner's invoices kept being rejected by a large supermarket client, delaying her payments by weeks. I called her with support, found our invoices lacked a proper purchase-order field, and got her a temporary template within two days. Then I found 200 similar complaints in support tickets and prioritised a purchase-order feature. She got paid within a week, 18% of customers used the new field within three months, and related tickets fell 80%."

The short version highlights both the immediate help and the wider product improvement.

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Include |
|---|---|
| Situation | The customer and their problem |
| Task | Why you stepped in |
| Action | Direct engagement, immediate fix, root cause, wider pattern, product change |
| Result | Customer outcome and broader impact |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I gave them a discount." | Solved the real problem |
| One-off fix | Found the pattern and fixed it for everyone |
| Handed off to support | Personally engaged |
| No impact beyond one customer | Measurable impact across customers |

#### Useful phrases
- "I called the customer directly to understand the problem."
- "I fixed her immediate issue, then looked for the wider pattern."
- "We found over 200 similar complaints."
- "One customer's problem was a signal of a wider issue."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Heroics that don't scale** or ignore priorities.
- **Discounts** instead of solutions.
- **No root-cause thinking.**
- **No measurable result.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How do you decide when to go above and beyond?"**
  "When the issue reveals a product problem, affects a vulnerable customer, or has wider impact. I avoid building one-off custom solutions."
- **"How do you stay close to customers as a PO?"**
  "Regular interviews, listening to support calls, reading tickets and reviews, and joining customer visits."
- **"Did this affect other priorities?"**
  "The temporary fix took two days of one engineer's time; the full feature was prioritised normally because the data showed its impact."

- **"How do you make sure the whole team understands customers?"**
  "Invite team members to interviews, share recordings and quotes, and review support themes together regularly."
- **"When should you not go above and beyond for one customer?"**
  "When it means building custom solutions that add complexity without helping others, or when it distracts from bigger priorities."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Customer obsession** that scales into product improvements.
- **Systems** that surface customer problems early.
- **Judgement** about one-off versus scalable solutions.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: a time you personally helped a customer and improved the product as a result.
2. Write the immediate fix and the wider improvement.
3. Practise **out loud in under 2.5 minutes**.
