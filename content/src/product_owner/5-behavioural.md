# Product Owner — Behavioural

## Tell me about a product or feature you took from idea to launch
- id: po-behavioural-01
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: end-to-end ownership, delivery, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a product or feature you owned from the initial idea through to launch. What was your role, and what was the result?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you can actually **do the whole job**: discovery, prioritisation, delivery and measuring results.
- **Your personal contribution**, not just what the team did.
- Whether you make decisions based on **evidence**.
- Whether you **measure outcomes** and **learn** from them.

#### Key points
- Use **STAR**: **S**ituation, **T**ask, **A**ction, **R**esult.
- Spend about **55–60% of the time on Action**, where your skills show.
- Say **"I"** for your actions and **"we"** for team actions, and make the difference clear.
- Choose a story that shows **discovery → scoping → delivery → launch → measurement**.
- End with **numbers** and a **reflection** on what you learned.
- Aim for about **2–3 minutes**, then let the interviewer ask follow-ups.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** At a mid-sized insurance company, customers had to call us to make even simple policy changes, like updating an address or adding a driver. These calls made up about 40% of call-centre volume, cost roughly £1.2m a year, and customer satisfaction for these journeys was falling.

**Task.** As Product Owner for the customer portal, I was asked to reduce call volume for policy changes by enabling self-service. I had a team of five engineers, a designer and a QA engineer, and a target of a 25% reduction in these calls within six months.

**Action.**
- **Discovery**: I analysed three months of call reasons with the operations team and found that five change types made up 75% of the calls. I listened to 20 recorded calls and interviewed eight customers. A key insight was that customers didn't just want a form. They wanted **confirmation** that the change had been applied and what it meant for their premium.
- **Scoping**: I prioritised **address changes** and **adding a named driver** first, because they had the highest volume and the lowest regulatory complexity. I deliberately deferred changes to cover levels, which needed underwriting rules and more compliance work.
- **Working with compliance early**: I involved our compliance team in refinement from the start, so the wording and confirmation emails were approved before development finished, avoiding last-minute surprises.
- **Delivery**: we worked in two-week sprints. I wrote the stories with the team, made sure each had clear acceptance criteria, and we released to **10% of customers after six weeks** behind a feature flag.
- **Iteration**: beta data showed that 35% of users abandoned when asked to log in again. I worked with the designer and engineers on a **magic-link login** from the reminder email, which increased completion by 18%.
- **Launch**: I worked with marketing and the call centre on messaging, so agents could direct callers to the portal, and we added a link in renewal emails.

**Result.**
- Within three months of full launch, **62% of address changes** happened online.
- Calls for those change types dropped by **35%**, beating our 25% target and saving about **£300k a year**.
- Satisfaction for those journeys rose from **3.6 to 4.3** out of 5.
- Looking back, I'd involve the call-centre team even earlier. Their insights from customer calls were the most valuable input we had, and I now include frontline teams in discovery from day one."

#### Another worked example: a B2B SaaS feature
"**Situation.** In a B2B scheduling product, larger customers kept churning in their second year. Exit interviews mentioned 'reporting' repeatedly.

**Task.** As PO, I owned reducing churn among accounts with more than 50 users. The target was to cut churn in that segment from 18% to 12% a year.

**Action.**
- I interviewed 10 customers, including 4 who had left. I found they didn't need complex reports. They needed to **show their managers that the tool saved time**.
- I proposed a simple **monthly 'value summary' email** (hours saved, meetings scheduled automatically) instead of a full reporting suite, which cut the estimated effort from 3 months to 4 weeks.
- I tested a manual version first: our analyst emailed summaries to 20 accounts, and 85% forwarded them to managers.
- We built an automated version, launched to all large accounts, and I set up a dashboard tracking churn and email engagement.

**Result.** Over the next two quarters, churn in the segment fell from 18% to 13%. The email became one of our most-mentioned features in renewal calls. I learned that the requested solution ('reports') often hides a simpler underlying need ('prove value to my boss')."

#### Step-by-step framework (STAR)
| Part | Time | What to include |
|---|---|---|
| Situation | ~15% | The product, users, the problem, with a number if possible |
| Task | ~10% | Your responsibility and the goal or target |
| Action | ~55% | Discovery, decisions, trade-offs, collaboration, delivery, iteration |
| Result | ~20% | Metrics, impact versus target, and what you learned |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "We built a portal using Scrum." | Shows discovery, scoping decisions and iteration |
| Says "we" throughout | Clearly separates "I" (own actions) from "we" (team) |
| No numbers | Quantified problem, target and result |
| Only delivery | Covers the full cycle from idea to measured outcome |
| No reflection | Ends with a specific lesson applied since |

#### Useful phrases
- "The problem we were trying to solve was…, which was costing…"
- "I made the decision to prioritise… because…"
- "I deliberately deferred… to reduce risk and ship sooner."
- "We beat our target of… by…"
- "Looking back, I'd… and I've done that in every project since."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Only talking about delivery** ("we built it in Scrum"), without discovery or measurement.
- **"We" throughout**, so the interviewer can't tell what you did.
- **No numbers** in the result.
- **A story that's too old or too small**: choose something recent and meaningful.
- **Too long**: rambling for 6+ minutes before reaching the result.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What was the hardest decision you made?"**
  "Deferring cover-level changes. Stakeholders wanted them, but they would have doubled the timeline and compliance risk. I showed that address and driver changes alone covered most call volume."
- **"What would you do differently?"**
  "Involve the call-centre team from day one, and set up the beta measurement dashboard before development started."
- **"How did you handle disagreement in the team?"**
  Have a short example ready, e.g. how you resolved a design-versus-engineering debate with a quick user test.
- **"How did you know the calls dropped because of your feature?"**
  "We compared call volumes for the self-service change types against other call types, and against the beta group versus the rest during rollout."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Larger scope**: multiple teams, a product area or a business outcome.
- **Strategic reasoning**: why this problem mattered to the company.
- **Influence across the organisation**: leadership, operations, compliance, marketing.
- **Building capability**: processes or practices that outlasted the project.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: list 3 things you've launched. For each, note the problem, your role, one hard decision and one number.
2. Choose the one with the strongest **discovery-to-measurement** arc.
3. Write it out in STAR with bullet points (not a script), keeping Action as the longest part.
4. Practise **out loud in under 3 minutes**, recording yourself. Check: clear "I", numbers, a decision, a lesson.
5. Prepare answers to the four follow-ups above for your own story.

## Tell me about a time you said no to a senior stakeholder
- id: po-behavioural-02
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: saying no, influence, stakeholder management, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you had to push back on or say no to a senior stakeholder. How did you handle it, and what happened?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Courage**: will you push back on senior people when it's the right thing to do?
- **Diplomacy**: can you do it while keeping the relationship strong?
- Whether your pushback is based on **evidence and goals**, not ego or preference.
- Whether you offer **alternatives** and focus on the **underlying need**.

#### Key points
- Show that you **listened** and understood their goal first.
- Use **data** to make the trade-off visible.
- Offer an **alternative** that meets the underlying need.
- Describe the **outcome** for the business **and** the **relationship** afterwards.
- A good outcome isn't necessarily "I won". It might be a better shared decision, or disagreeing and committing.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** In my previous role at a B2B software company, our Sales Director asked us to build a custom reporting dashboard for one large prospect. It was a six-week piece of work, right in the middle of a quarter focused on reducing customer churn.

**Task.** I needed to decide whether to change priorities, and if not, say no in a way that kept a good relationship with Sales, who were under real pressure to hit their targets.

**Action.**
- **Understand the need**: I met the Sales Director the same day. The deal was worth about £150k a year, and the client's real need was to share monthly usage figures with their board.
- **Check the trade-off**: our churn work affected over 2,000 customers and around £1.1m of at-risk annual revenue. Pausing it for six weeks would cost far more than the deal was worth.
- **Find an alternative**: with an engineer, I found we could provide a **scheduled CSV export** plus a ready-made spreadsheet template in about two days, which met the board-reporting need.
- **Present it together**: I brought both options to the Sales Director and our VP of Product, showed the numbers side by side, and recommended the export. I made it clear I wanted to help close the deal, just not at the cost of our churn goals.

**Result.**
- The Sales Director agreed. The client accepted the export, and the deal closed a month later.
- The churn project stayed on track and reduced churn by **12%** that quarter.
- The relationship got better, not worse: the Sales Director started bringing client requests to me **early**, and we set up a fortnightly sync to review deal-related requests together."

#### Another worked example: pushing back on the CEO
"**Situation.** Our CEO wanted to launch a new loyalty feature in time for a conference, just three weeks away.

**Task.** The feature wasn't ready: payment edge cases hadn't been tested, and launching risked customers being charged incorrectly.

**Action.** I asked for 20 minutes with the CEO and explained the specific risk ('in testing, 2% of reward redemptions failed to apply the discount, which would mean incorrect charges'). I acknowledged why the conference mattered, and proposed an alternative: announce the feature at the conference with a **waitlist**, and launch to waitlist customers two weeks later once testing was complete.

**Result.** The CEO agreed. The announcement got 4,000 waitlist sign-ups, and the launch two weeks later went smoothly with no billing incidents. The CEO later told me she appreciated being given the facts and an option, rather than just a no."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Situation**: who the stakeholder was and what they asked for.
2. **Task**: why it was a problem.
3. **Action**: listen → quantify the trade-off → find an alternative → present it respectfully.
4. **Result**: the business outcome and the relationship afterwards.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I told them no because it wasn't on the roadmap." | Explains the trade-off with data and goals |
| Comes across as stubborn | Shows empathy for the stakeholder's pressures |
| No alternative | Offers a solution to the underlying need |
| Escalates immediately | Tries to resolve it directly first |
| Ignores the relationship | Shows the relationship improved afterwards |

#### Useful phrases
- "I wanted to understand what was really driving the request."
- "When I put the numbers side by side, the trade-off was clear."
- "I proposed an alternative that met their underlying need."
- "I made it clear I wanted to help them succeed, just not at the cost of…"
- "Afterwards, they started involving me earlier."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Coming across as stubborn** or dismissive of the business.
- **A story where you were wrong**, without reflection.
- **No alternative offered.**
- **Escalating straight away** instead of trying to resolve it first.
- **Choosing a trivial disagreement** that doesn't show courage.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if they had insisted?"**
  "I'd have made sure the risks were understood and documented, then disagreed and committed: executing the decision fully and reporting back on the results."
- **"Tell me about a time you were wrong to push back."**
  Have an honest example ready, e.g. resisting a request that turned out to be valuable, and what you learned about listening more carefully.
- **"How do you build relationships with senior stakeholders?"**
  "Regular one-to-ones, understanding their goals and pressures, sharing data early, and delivering on commitments."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Executive influence**: comfortably pushing back on very senior people.
- Framing decisions in **business terms**.
- Building **processes** (e.g. a request-intake sync) that make one-off conflicts rarer.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: think of times you disagreed with a manager, executive or client. Pick one where you used evidence and offered an alternative.
2. Write it in STAR, with the stakeholder's underlying need, your data and your alternative clearly stated.
3. Practise **out loud in about 2.5 minutes**.
4. Prepare a second story where you disagreed but committed to their decision, as interviewers often ask for both.

## Tell me about a product decision that turned out to be wrong
- id: po-behavioural-03
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: failure, learning, ownership, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time a product decision you made turned out to be wrong. What happened, and what did you learn?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Self-awareness and honesty**: can you admit real mistakes?
- **Ownership**: do you take responsibility or blame others?
- **Response**: how quickly you noticed, and what you did about it.
- **Learning**: a concrete lesson that changed how you work.

#### Key points
- Pick a **real** mistake with **real** impact. "I worked too hard" or "I'm a perfectionist" are red flags.
- But choose one where the impact was **recoverable** and the **lesson is strong**, not a career-ending disaster.
- Show **ownership**: use "I", and don't blame engineers, stakeholders or customers.
- Spend real time on **how you responded** and **what you changed**.
- Show you've **applied the lesson since**, ideally with an example.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** I was Product Owner for a B2B scheduling tool. Several of our larger customers were asking loudly for a complex recurring-appointments feature, and I prioritised it, about eight weeks of work.

**Task.** My goal was to increase usage and retention among larger customers.

**Action (the mistake).**
- I relied mainly on what a handful of vocal customers said in calls. I didn't check how many customers actually needed recurring appointments.
- I also agreed the full scope up front, instead of releasing a smaller version to test demand first.

**What happened.**
- After launch, only **4% of accounts** used the feature. The customers who'd asked for it used it heavily, but they were a small minority.
- Meanwhile, we'd delayed a **calendar sync integration** that our usage data suggested would help far more customers.

**How I responded.**
- I shared the usage data openly in our next product review, explained my reasoning at the time, and what I'd missed. I didn't blame the customers who'd asked for it, since the decision was mine.
- We moved calendar sync to the next sprint.
- I simplified the recurring-appointments settings to reduce support questions, rather than investing more.

**Result and learning.**
- Calendar sync launched six weeks later and was adopted by **38% of accounts**.
- My lesson: **loud requests aren't the same as widespread needs**. Since then, before committing to any big feature, I check requests against usage data and a quick survey, and I release a thin first version to a beta group.
- For example, on my next large feature, a beta with 15 accounts showed we needed only about half the planned complexity, which saved roughly four weeks of development."

#### Another worked example: a launch decision
"**Situation.** At an e-commerce company, I decided to launch a redesigned checkout to all users at once, because early tests looked positive and we wanted it live before the holiday season.

**What went wrong.** Within two days, conversion on older Android devices dropped by 9%. A payment component rendered incorrectly on some screen sizes we hadn't tested well.

**Response.** I worked with engineering to roll back for affected devices within hours, communicated the impact to leadership, and led a blameless post-mortem.

**Learning.** I now insist on **staged rollouts** (1% → 10% → 50% → 100%) with automatic monitoring by device and platform, especially before busy periods. On our next major release, staged rollout caught a similar issue at 1%, before it affected meaningful numbers of customers."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Situation**: the context and the decision.
2. **Task**: what you were trying to achieve.
3. **The mistake**: what you did and why it was wrong, owned with "I".
4. **What happened**: the impact, with numbers if possible.
5. **Response**: how you noticed and what you did.
6. **Learning**: the lesson and how you've applied it since.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I care too much about quality." | A real mistake with real consequences |
| Blames engineers or stakeholders | Owns the decision personally |
| Spends most of the time on the failure | Focuses on response and learning |
| Vague lesson ("communicate better") | A specific lesson with an example of applying it |
| A catastrophic, unresolved failure | A recoverable mistake with a strong recovery |

#### Useful phrases
- "The decision was mine, and in hindsight I relied too much on…"
- "Once the data showed…, I shared it openly with the team and stakeholders."
- "The main lesson I took was…"
- "Since then, I always… For example, on my next project…"

#### Common pitfalls
- **A fake weakness** or a mistake with no consequence.
- **Blaming others** (engineering, stakeholders, customers).
- **No clear lesson**, or a lesson you haven't applied since.
- **Too much detail on the failure**, too little on the response.
- **Choosing a mistake that raises serious doubts** about your judgement or ethics.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How did your stakeholders react?"**
  "They appreciated the transparency. Sharing the data proactively, with a plan, built more trust than if I'd tried to defend the decision."
- **"How do you decide when to stop investing in something?"**
  "When the evidence shows the hypothesis was wrong and further investment won't change that. I try to set those criteria before launch."
- **"What would you do if a team member made a similar mistake?"**
  "Focus on learning, not blame: understand the reasoning, help them share it openly, and improve the process together."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Self-awareness** and comfort discussing failure.
- Creating **psychological safety** for the team to learn from mistakes.
- Changing **processes**, not just personal habits, as a result.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: list 3 decisions that didn't go as planned. Choose one with real but recoverable impact and a clear lesson.
2. Write it out, making sure you use "I" for the decision and the response.
3. Write one concrete example of applying the lesson later.
4. Practise **out loud in under 3 minutes**, and check that at least half the time is on the response and the learning.

## Tell me about a time you used data to change a decision
- id: po-behavioural-04
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: data-driven, influence, analysis, STAR

### Prompt
Give me an example of when you used data to change your own mind, or someone else's, about a product decision.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Analytical curiosity**: do you look for evidence before acting?
- Whether you follow **evidence over opinion**, including your own.
- Your ability to **analyse and interpret data**, and to design tests.
- Your ability to **influence** others with evidence.

#### Key points
- Make the data **concrete**: what you looked at, how you analysed it, what it showed.
- Combine **quantitative** data with **qualitative** insight.
- Show the **impact** of the changed decision, with numbers.
- Present it as **finding a better answer together**, not proving someone wrong.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** At a meal-kit subscription company, leadership believed customers were leaving mainly because of **price**, and the plan was to offer a 20% discount to customers at risk of cancelling.

**Task.** As Product Owner for retention, I was asked to design the discount campaign. Before spending money on discounts, I wanted to confirm the real cause of churn.

**Action.**
- **Analysis**: I worked with our data analyst on six months of cancellation data.
  - The cancellation survey showed 'too expensive' as the top reason.
  - But behavioural data told a different story: **58% of customers who cancelled had skipped three or more weeks** before cancelling, and many had complained about **repetitive recipes**.
- **Segmentation**: customers in months 3–5 churned the most, which was exactly when they'd seen most of our recipe range.
- **Qualitative check**: I interviewed eight recently cancelled customers. Several said 'too expensive' really meant 'not worth it any more, because I was bored of the meals'.
- **Test**: rather than arguing, I proposed an experiment:
  - Group A: received the 20% discount.
  - Group B: received a 'new recipes' email with a personalised menu based on their past choices.
  - Control group: nothing.

**Result.**
- After six weeks, the discount reduced churn by **3%** but cost a significant amount of margin.
- The recipe-variety approach reduced churn by **9%** at almost no cost.
- Leadership dropped the broad discount and invested in expanding recipe variety and personalisation.
- Over the next two quarters, overall monthly churn fell from **11% to 8.5%**.
- I learned that what customers *say* in surveys can differ from what their *behaviour* shows, so I now always look at both."

#### Another worked example: changing your own mind
"**Situation.** I was convinced that adding a social login (sign in with Google or Apple) would significantly increase sign-ups for our app.

**Action.** Before prioritising it, I looked at the sign-up funnel. Only 8% of users dropped off at the account-creation step. The biggest drop (40%) was at the next step, where we asked for detailed profile information. I ran a quick test making most profile fields optional.

**Result.** Completion of sign-up rose by 22%. Social login, which I'd assumed was the answer, would have addressed a much smaller drop-off. We built it later, but as a lower priority. It reminded me to test my own assumptions as rigorously as anyone else's."

#### A third example: data in a public-sector service
"**Situation.** On a government online service for renewing permits, managers believed the online form was too long and wanted to remove questions.
**Action.** Analytics showed most drop-off happened not on long questions, but on a page asking for a reference number from an old paper letter, which many users no longer had. Support call logs confirmed it was the top reason for calls. We added a way to find the reference number using personal details instead, and kept the other questions.
**Result.** Completion rose from 61% to 78%, and calls about reference numbers fell by about 60%. The original plan (shortening the form) would have missed the real problem."

#### Make it your own
- Think of a moment when **a number surprised you**, or someone else.
- Note **what data you looked at**, **how you analysed it**, and **what changed** as a result.
- If you don't have analytics experience, use **support data, sales data, survey results or spreadsheets**: the principle is the same.
- Prepare a **one-sentence summary**: "The data showed X, so we changed Y, which led to Z."

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Situation**: the belief or plan at the time.
2. **Task**: your role and why you questioned it.
3. **Action**: the data you analysed, the segmentation, the qualitative check, and the experiment.
4. **Result**: what the data showed, the changed decision, the impact and the lesson.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I looked at analytics and changed the plan." | Specific data, analysis and findings |
| Data confirmed what everyone thought | Data changed the decision |
| Numbers only | Combines data with customer interviews |
| "I proved my boss wrong." | Framed as finding a better answer together |
| No impact shown | Quantified impact of the changed decision |

#### Useful phrases
- "Before committing, I wanted to check the assumption with data."
- "The survey said one thing, but the behavioural data told a different story."
- "Rather than debating opinions, I proposed a quick experiment."
- "The results led us to change direction, which…"
- "It taught me to look at what customers do, not just what they say."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Data that confirmed** what everyone already thought, with no change.
- **Vague data** ("I looked at analytics").
- **Ignoring qualitative evidence.**
- **Framing it as proving someone wrong.**
- **Confusing correlation with causation** without testing.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if the data had been inconclusive?"**
  "I'd have proposed a small, time-boxed test of both approaches, or gathered more qualitative evidence, rather than delaying the decision indefinitely."
- **"How do you handle stakeholders who ignore data?"**
  "Understand their concerns (they may have context the data doesn't show), present data visually and simply, and use experiments so the evidence speaks for itself."
- **"What tools did you use?"**
  Be ready to mention your actual tools (e.g. SQL, Amplitude, Mixpanel, Looker, Excel), and how you worked with analysts.

- **"What if leadership had ignored the data?"**
  "I'd have proposed a small experiment so the evidence came from real customer behaviour, which is harder to dismiss than a chart."
- **"How did you make sure the data was correct?"**
  "Cross-checked against another source (e.g. the order database versus analytics) and checked definitions with the data analyst."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Combining **quantitative and qualitative** evidence.
- Designing **experiments** to establish causation.
- Spreading **data-informed decision-making** across the team and organisation.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: list times data surprised you or someone else. Pick one where it led to a different decision with measurable impact.
2. Write down the specific data, how you analysed it, and what the experiment or check was.
3. Practise **out loud in about 2.5 minutes**.
4. Prepare a second, shorter example where data changed **your own** mind.

## Tell me about a conflict with your engineering team and how you resolved it
- id: po-behavioural-05
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: conflict resolution, engineering partnership, teamwork, STAR

### Prompt
Tell me about a time you disagreed with your engineering team or tech lead. How did you resolve it?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you **respect engineering expertise** and treat the team as partners.
- How you handle **disagreement** constructively.
- Whether you look for **win-win solutions** rather than "winning".
- Whether the conflict **improved how you work** together.

#### Key points
- Choose a **genuine** disagreement where both sides had good reasons.
- Show you **listened** and tried to understand the other perspective.
- Focus on the **problem, not the person**.
- Find a **better solution together**, often one neither side started with.
- Show a **lasting improvement** in the working relationship or process.
- Avoid stories where you "won" by overruling engineers.

#### Model answer
"**Situation.** On a payments product, my tech lead wanted the team to spend the next sprint migrating to a new version of our payment provider's API. I wanted the team to deliver **Apple Pay**, which our largest client was waiting for.

**Task.** We needed to agree on priorities without damaging our relationship. We both had good reasons.

**Action.**
- **Understanding their view**: I asked the tech lead to walk me through why the migration was urgent. The old API would be **deprecated in four months**, and our current integration was causing about **2% of payments to fail intermittently**, which I hadn't fully appreciated.
- **Sharing my view**: I explained the client context. Apple Pay was part of their contract renewal, due in six weeks, and the account was worth a significant share of our revenue.
- **Finding a better option together**: when we looked at it together, we discovered the new API **supported Apple Pay natively**. Building Apple Pay on the old API would mean building it twice.
- **Agreeing a plan**: migrate first (two sprints), then add Apple Pay on the new API (one sprint), which still met the client's deadline.
- **Managing stakeholders**: I explained the plan to the client's account manager, who confirmed the timeline worked.

**Result.**
- We delivered both on time.
- Payment failures dropped from **2% to 0.4%** after the migration, which on its own recovered around **£40k a month** in failed transactions.
- The client renewed.
- The experience changed how we worked: the tech lead and I started a **fortnightly session** to review technical risks against the roadmap, so these conflicts came up much earlier."

#### Another worked example: design versus engineering
"**Situation.** Our designer wanted a highly animated onboarding flow; the engineers felt it would take three times longer than a simpler version and hurt performance on older phones.

**Action.** Rather than deciding myself, I suggested a quick test: the designer created two prototypes (animated and simple), and we tested both with eight users. Engineers joined the sessions.

**Result.** Users completed onboarding equally well with both, but preferred one specific animation that helped them understand a key concept. We built the simple version plus that one animation. It shipped in half the time, the designer felt heard, and the team adopted 'test it with users' as the way to settle design debates."

#### A third example: QA and delivery speed
"**Situation.** Our QA lead insisted on a full manual regression test before every release, which took three days. I wanted to release weekly.
**Action.** I asked what the QA lead was worried about: past releases had broken payments, and there were few automated tests. Instead of arguing about release frequency, we agreed to invest one sprint in automated tests for the payment and sign-up journeys, the highest-risk areas, and keep manual testing for new features only.
**Result.** Regression testing dropped from three days to half a day, we moved to weekly releases, and production bugs fell. The QA lead became a strong advocate for automation."

#### Make it your own
- Choose a disagreement where **both sides had a valid point**.
- Write the other person's view **in their words**, to check you truly understood it.
- Highlight the **better option you found together**, and what changed in how you work.
- Avoid stories where you "won" by authority.

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Situation**: what the disagreement was about.
2. **Task**: why it needed resolving.
3. **Action**: understand their view → share yours → explore options together → agree a plan → align stakeholders.
4. **Result**: business outcome, relationship outcome, process improvement.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I explained the business priority and they agreed." | Genuinely explores and learns from the engineer's view |
| Engineers portrayed as obstructive | Both sides had valid reasons |
| Resolved by escalating to a manager | Resolved together through collaboration |
| One side wins | A better solution neither side started with |
| No lasting change | Improved the working process afterwards |

#### Useful phrases
- "I asked them to walk me through their reasoning, and learned something I hadn't appreciated."
- "We both had good reasons, so the question was how to meet both needs."
- "When we looked at it together, we found an option neither of us had considered."
- "Since then, we've had a regular session to catch these trade-offs earlier."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Portraying engineers as obstructive** or yourself as the hero.
- **A trivial conflict**, or one that was never really resolved.
- **Resolving it by escalation** or by overruling.
- **No lasting change** in how you work together.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What would you have done if there was no win-win?"**
  "I'd have made the trade-off explicit with data (risk of failures versus contract risk), made a decision as the PO, explained it clearly to the tech lead, and made sure the other need was scheduled soon after."
- **"How do you build trust with a new engineering team?"**
  "Involve them early in discovery, explain the why behind priorities, respect their estimates, protect them from chaos, and act on their concerns about technical health."
- **"How technical do you need to be as a PO?"**
  "Technical enough to understand trade-offs and ask good questions, without needing to write production code."

- **"How do you keep disagreements from becoming personal?"**
  "Focus on shared goals and evidence, discuss in private when emotions run high, and acknowledge the other person's expertise."
- **"What do you do when the tech lead and designer disagree?"**
  "Facilitate: clarify the user problem and constraints, explore options together, and settle it with a quick test where possible."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- A **true partnership** with engineering leadership.
- Understanding **technical trade-offs** well enough to make good decisions.
- Creating **mechanisms** that prevent recurring conflicts.

#### Practise it
1. **Find your story**: think of disagreements with engineers or designers. Choose one where both sides had valid points and the result was better than either starting position.
2. Write down the other person's reasoning in their own words, to check you really understood it.
3. Practise **out loud in about 2.5 minutes**.
4. Prepare a short answer for "what if there had been no win-win?"

## Why do you want to be a Product Owner? Walk me through your background
- id: po-behavioural-06
- round: BEHAVIORAL_BAR_RAISER
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: motivation, career story, opening

### Prompt
Walk me through your background and tell me why you want this Product Owner role.

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you can tell a **clear, concise story** about your career.
- Your **motivation** for product ownership, shown through experience, not just claimed.
- Whether you've **researched** the company and role.
- Your **communication** in the first few minutes, which sets the tone for the whole interview.

#### Key points
- Usually the **first question**: keep it to about **2 minutes**.
- Structure it as a **narrative**: present → past → why product → why here.
- Highlight the **2–3 experiences** most relevant to this specific role.
- Show motivation through **things you've done**, not just statements like "I'm passionate about products".
- Make "why here" **specific** to the company's product, customers or challenges.

#### Model answer
"I'm currently a **Product Owner at a mid-sized insurtech**, where I own the customer self-service portal used by about 400,000 policyholders.

I started my career as a **business analyst at a bank**. That taught me to understand complex processes and turn them into clear requirements, and to work closely with both business teams and engineers. But I noticed I enjoyed the questions of *why* and *what should we build* far more than documenting what someone else had decided.

So three years ago I moved into a **Product Owner role**. My biggest achievement has been launching **self-service policy changes**, which cut related call volume by 35% and raised customer satisfaction for those journeys from 3.6 to 4.3 out of 5. That project is where I really fell in love with product work: combining customer insight and data to solve a real problem, then working with designers and engineers to solve it in the simplest possible way.

What I enjoy most about product ownership is being the link between customers, the business and the team: understanding a problem deeply, making clear decisions about what matters most, and seeing the impact in the numbers.

I'm excited about **this role** for two reasons. First, I understand you're moving from a sales-led roadmap to a more product-led one. I've been through that transition in my current company, and I know how much difference it makes. Second, your focus on **small businesses** really appeals to me. I think there's a huge opportunity in simplifying their finances, and my experience with self-service and reducing customer effort feels very relevant to that."

#### Another worked example: a career changer
"I spent six years as a **customer success manager** at a SaaS company, looking after around 40 business customers.

In that role, I became the person who collected and organised customer feedback for the product team. I started analysing churn reasons and presenting them to product leadership, and one of my analyses led to a redesign of our onboarding, which reduced early churn by 15%.

That's when I realised I wanted to be on the other side, deciding what to build. Over the last year I've worked part-time as a **product owner for our internal tools**, run discovery interviews with customers, and completed a product management course.

I'm applying for this role because your product serves customer success teams like the one I came from. I deeply understand those users' problems, and I'd bring that customer empathy, plus hands-on experience turning feedback into product decisions."

#### Step-by-step framework
| Part | Length | What to include |
|---|---|---|
| Present | 1 sentence | Your current role and scope |
| Past | 3–4 sentences | 2–3 key roles or achievements, each with a relevant skill |
| Why product | 1–2 sentences | What draws you, shown through something you've done |
| Why here | 2–3 sentences | Specific to the company's product, mission or challenges |

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| Recites the whole CV chronologically | A focused narrative with 2–3 highlights |
| Talks for 5+ minutes | About 2 minutes |
| "I'm passionate about products." | Shows motivation through real achievements |
| Generic "why here" | Specific to the company's situation and customers |
| Negative about the current employer | Positive and forward-looking |

#### Useful phrases
- "I'm currently… where I…"
- "That taught me… and I noticed I enjoyed…"
- "My biggest achievement there was… which…"
- "What I enjoy most about product ownership is…"
- "I'm excited about this role because… and my experience with… feels directly relevant."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Reciting your entire CV** chronologically.
- **Going on too long.**
- **A generic "why here"** that could apply to any company.
- **Being negative** about your current or past employers.
- **No evidence** for your motivation.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"Why are you leaving your current role?"**
  Frame it positively: "I've learned a lot, and I'm looking for broader scope and a product where I can have more impact on a whole customer journey." Avoid criticising your employer.
- **"What do you know about our product?"**
  Show you've used it (sign up for a trial if possible), and share one or two thoughtful observations, e.g. a strength and an opportunity.
- **"Where do you see yourself in a few years?"**
  "Owning a larger product area or leading other product people, while staying close to customers."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- A clear **product philosophy**: how you believe good products are built.
- A career story showing growing **scope, ownership and influence**.
- A **well-researched view** of the company's challenges and how you'd help.

#### Practise it
1. Write your answer as **four short bullet groups**: present, past (2–3 highlights with numbers), why product, why here.
2. Rewrite the "why here" section for **each company** you interview with.
3. Practise **out loud with a timer**, aiming for 1:45–2:15.
4. Record yourself and check: is it a story, not a list? Is there at least one number? Is "why here" specific?
