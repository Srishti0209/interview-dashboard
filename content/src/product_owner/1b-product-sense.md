# Product Owner — Product Sense (continued)

## How would you improve our product?
- id: po-product-sense-07
- round: PRODUCT_SENSE
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: company research, product improvement, preparation

### Prompt
You've had a look at our product. What would you improve, and why?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you've **prepared**: used the product, researched the company and its customers.
- Your **product judgement** applied to *their* real context.
- Whether you can **critique respectfully**. The interviewer may have built the thing you're criticising.
- Whether you make **realistic suggestions** that fit the company's strategy and constraints.

#### Key points
- **Use the product before the interview**: sign up, complete key tasks, try the mobile app, read reviews.
- **Research**: company strategy (annual reports, blog posts, recent launches), customers, competitors, app store reviews, job descriptions (they reveal priorities).
- Show **humility**: acknowledge you're seeing it from the outside, with limited data.
- Use the same structure as any improvement question: **goal → user → pain point → solution → metrics → risks**.
- **Start with something positive**, then make one or two focused, well-reasoned suggestions.

#### Model answer
"I spent some time with the product this week. I signed up, completed the onboarding, created a few invoices, tried the mobile app and read a few hundred app store reviews. I'm seeing it from the outside, so I'm sure there's context I don't have, but here's what stood out.

**What works well.** Creating the first invoice is impressively quick, under two minutes, and the templates look professional. Reviews consistently praise how simple it is compared with traditional accounting tools.

**Where I see an opportunity.** I'd focus on **getting paid**, because I assume that's the core reason small businesses use you, and your recent announcements about payments suggest it's a strategic priority.

- **User**: a sole trader or small business owner who invoices 5–20 clients a month.
- **Pain point**: after sending an invoice, there's little support for chasing late payment. In reviews, 'late payments' and 'reminders' came up repeatedly, and I couldn't find a way to set automatic reminders on the mobile app.
- **Why it matters**: late payment is one of the biggest problems for small businesses, and it's directly connected to the value you promise.

**Suggestion.** Automatic, polite payment reminders (e.g. 3 days before due, on the due date, 7 days after), with a one-click 'pay now' link and editable wording. A first version could reuse the existing email system, with default schedules and a simple on/off switch.

**How I'd measure it:**
- **Primary**: average days to payment for invoices with reminders on, compared with off.
- **Supporting**: share of users enabling reminders, share of invoices paid via the link.
- **Guardrail**: complaints or unsubscribes from invoice recipients, since we don't want users' clients to feel spammed.

**Risks and questions I'd want to explore:**
- Do users worry reminders will damage client relationships? Tone and control matter.
- Is this already on your roadmap, or did you deliberately choose not to do it?

I'd love to hear whether that matches what you see in your data."

#### Another worked example: a consumer app
"For a fitness app:
- **Positive**: workout videos are high quality, and the progress screen is motivating.
- **Opportunity**: reviews mention losing streaks when ill or travelling, then giving up. One-star reviews often say 'I lost my 60-day streak and stopped using it'.
- **Suggestion**: 'streak protection': a limited number of rest days per month, and lighter 'travel workouts'.
- **Metrics**: 30-day retention after a missed day, workouts per week. Guardrail: total workout volume shouldn't fall."

#### How to prepare for this question
| Research | What to look for |
|---|---|
| Use the product | Onboarding, core tasks, mobile vs web, friction points |
| App store and review sites | Repeated complaints and praise |
| Company blog, press, annual reports | Strategy, recent launches, priorities |
| Job descriptions | Which areas they're investing in |
| Competitors | What others do better or differently |
| Social media and forums | How users talk about the product |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Show preparation**: what you did to learn the product.
2. **Start with a genuine strength.**
3. **Pick one focus area** tied to the company's strategy.
4. **Define the user and the pain point**, with evidence.
5. **Suggest a focused solution** and a small first version.
6. **Metrics and risks.**
7. **Invite discussion** ("does that match what you see?").

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| Hasn't used the product | Clearly used it and researched reviews |
| "The design looks dated." | Specific, evidence-based pain point |
| Lists 10 complaints | One or two focused suggestions |
| Arrogant tone | Humble: acknowledges the outside view |
| Ignores company strategy | Connects to the company's priorities |

#### Useful phrases
- "I'm seeing this from the outside, so I'm sure there's context I'm missing."
- "One thing that really impressed me was…"
- "In the reviews, a theme that came up repeatedly was…"
- "I'd focus on this because it seems closely linked to your strategy around…"
- "Does that match what you're seeing in your data?"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Not using the product** before the interview.
- **Harsh criticism** without acknowledging constraints.
- **Too many ideas**, none developed.
- **Suggestions that ignore strategy** or are obviously impractical.
- **No metrics.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"We've already considered that. Why do you think it'll work?"**
  "That's useful to know. I'd love to understand what you found. If it didn't work before, I'd look at whether the problem was the idea, the execution or the timing."
- **"How would you prioritise this against other work?"**
  "I'd estimate its impact on payment times and retention against effort, and compare it with current priorities using the same criteria."
- **"What would you do in your first 30 days here?"**
  "Learn the data, talk to customers and the team, and validate whether the pain point is as big as reviews suggest."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Insight into the company's **strategic challenges**, not just UI issues.
- Understanding of the **business model** and how improvements drive revenue or retention.
- **Diplomacy** and curiosity about constraints.

#### Practise it
1. For every company you interview with, spend at least an hour using the product and reading 50–100 reviews.
2. Write down 2 strengths, 2 pain points (with evidence) and 1 detailed suggestion with metrics.
3. Practise the answer **out loud in 5 minutes**, starting with a strength and ending with a question to the interviewer.

## A product you think is badly designed — how would you fix it?
- id: po-product-sense-08
- round: PRODUCT_SENSE
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: product critique, UX, problem solving

### Prompt
Tell me about a product or experience you think is badly designed. Why is it bad, and how would you fix it?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Your ability to **diagnose why** something doesn't work, beyond "it's annoying".
- **Empathy for users**, and for the people who built it (constraints).
- Whether you can **propose and justify** improvements.
- **Communication**: a clear, engaging critique.

#### Key points
- Choose something **everyday and relatable**: a car park payment machine, a hotel TV remote, a government form, a self-checkout.
- Explain **who the users are** and **what they're trying to do**.
- Diagnose the **root cause** using design principles: **unclear feedback, too many steps, poor error handling, inconsistency, hidden actions, ignoring context**.
- Consider **why it might be that way** (constraints, cost, legacy).
- Propose **specific improvements** and how you'd measure them.

#### Model answer
"I find **car park payment machines** frustrating, especially the ones at hospitals.

**Who uses them and what they're trying to do.** Often people who are stressed, in a hurry or unwell, sometimes visitors who've just had difficult news. They just want to pay quickly and leave.

**What goes wrong:**
1. **Too many steps in an unclear order**: enter your registration, choose a tariff, insert a ticket. Every machine is different, and instructions are tiny and dense.
2. **Poor feedback**: after tapping a card, there's often no clear sign it's processing, so people tap again or think it failed.
3. **Bad error handling**: 'Error 47' with no explanation of what to do.
4. **Ignoring context**: hospitals have many elderly visitors, and machines are placed in dark corners with small screens and bright glare.
5. **Limited payment options**: coins only, or an app that requires creating an account in a stressful moment.

**Why it might be like this.** Machines are expensive to replace and are often designed for many different sites, so they're generic. Operators may optimise for cost and enforcement, not experience.

**How I'd fix it:**
- **Pay on exit using number-plate recognition**, so nothing needs to be done at a machine at all, with contactless payment at the barrier.
- If a machine is needed: **one clear, large instruction per screen**, a progress indicator, and immediate feedback ('Processing… Paid. You have 15 minutes to leave').
- **Plain-language errors** with a next step ('Card declined: try another card or press Help to talk to someone').
- **Accessibility**: high contrast, large text, physical buttons, audio support, sensible placement.
- **Hospital-specific**: concession options for frequent visitors, clearly explained.

**How I'd measure success:** average time to pay, share of failed or repeated transactions, help-button calls, and complaints."

#### Another worked example: a digital product
"Many **utility company websites** make it hard to submit a meter reading:
- **Problem**: the option is buried under 'My account → Energy → Usage → Submit reading'; logging in requires a password most people have forgotten; error messages are unclear.
- **Root cause**: navigation organised by internal departments, not user tasks.
- **Fix**: a prominent 'Submit a meter reading' button on the homepage, a secure link sent by text or email so no password is needed, a photo upload option, and clear confirmation.
- **Metrics**: completion rate, time to submit, calls to customer service about readings."

#### Common design problems to look for
| Problem | Example | Principle |
|---|---|---|
| Unclear feedback | No sign a payment is processing | Visibility of system status |
| Too many steps | Five screens to do one task | Reduce effort |
| Bad error messages | "Error 47" | Help users recover |
| Hidden actions | Key action buried in menus | Discoverability |
| Inconsistency | Every machine works differently | Consistency and standards |
| Ignoring context | Small screens for elderly users | Design for real users and environments |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Choose a relatable product.**
2. **Describe the users and their goal.**
3. **Diagnose 3–5 problems** using design principles.
4. **Consider constraints** (why it's like this).
5. **Propose improvements**, from ideal to practical.
6. **Define success metrics.**

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "It's just really annoying." | Diagnoses specific problems and why they happen |
| Ignores who the users are | Considers user context (stress, age, environment) |
| Mocks the designers | Acknowledges constraints and trade-offs |
| Vague fix ("make it simpler") | Specific improvements and metrics |

#### Useful phrases
- "The people using this are usually… and what they want is…"
- "The core problem is that it doesn't give clear feedback."
- "It's probably like this because… which makes sense from a cost point of view, but…"
- "The ideal fix would be…, but a practical first step would be…"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Ranting** without structured analysis.
- **Choosing something obscure** the interviewer can't relate to.
- **Ignoring constraints** and suggesting unrealistic fixes.
- **No measurement.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"If you could only change one thing, what would it be?"**
  "Clear feedback after payment, because uncertainty causes repeated attempts, errors and stress, and it's cheap to fix."
- **"How would you validate your redesign?"**
  "Observe people using the current machine, test a prototype with a mix of users including older visitors, then pilot it in one car park and compare metrics."
- **"How would you convince the operator to invest?"**
  "Show the cost of the current experience (help calls, failed payments, complaints) and the benefits (faster exits, higher payment success)."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Using **design principles** confidently.
- Balancing **ideal versus practical** solutions.
- Building a **business case** for experience improvements.

#### Practise it
1. Keep a note on your phone of frustrating products you encounter for a week.
2. Pick two and analyse each with the framework above.
3. Practise one **out loud in 4 minutes**.

## How do you run a customer interview?
- id: po-product-sense-09
- round: PRODUCT_SENSE
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: user research, customer interviews, discovery

### Prompt
How do you plan and run a customer interview? What questions would you ask, and what would you avoid?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Practical **user research skills**: do you know how to learn the truth from customers?
- Whether you avoid **leading questions** and focus on **past behaviour**.
- How you **turn interviews into insights** and decisions.
- Whether you make customer contact a **regular habit**.

#### Key points
- Start with a clear **research goal**: what do we need to learn, and what decision will it inform?
- **Recruit the right people**: the specific segment you care about, including non-users and churned users.
- Ask about **past behaviour and real stories**, not hypotheticals ("Tell me about the last time…").
- Avoid **leading questions**, **pitching**, and asking people to design the solution.
- **Listen more than you talk**; use silence and follow-up questions ("Why was that?", "What happened next?").
- **Capture and synthesise**: notes, recordings (with consent), and patterns across interviews.
- **Involve the team**: engineers and designers listening in builds shared understanding.

#### Model answer
"**1. Set a goal.** Before any interview, I write down what we want to learn and which decision it informs. For example: 'Understand how small business owners currently chase late payments, so we can decide whether to build automatic reminders.'

**2. Recruit the right people.** I'd aim for **5–8 interviews per segment**, e.g. sole traders who invoice regularly, including some who use competitors and some who left us. I'd recruit through our customer list, support contacts and a short screener survey, and offer a small thank-you, like a voucher.

**3. Prepare a discussion guide**, a flexible list of topics, not a strict script:
- **Warm-up**: 'Tell me a bit about your business and your role.'
- **Context**: 'Walk me through how you handle invoicing in a typical month.'
- **Specific past stories**: 'Tell me about the last time a client paid late. What happened? What did you do? How did it make you feel?'
- **Current solutions**: 'What do you use to keep track? What's frustrating about it?'
- **Impact**: 'What does a late payment mean for your business?'
- **Wrap-up**: 'Is there anything I should have asked but didn't?'

**4. Run the interview well:**
- Explain the purpose, ask for consent to record, and reassure them there are no wrong answers.
- **Ask open questions** ('How…', 'Tell me about…'), not yes/no questions.
- **Focus on the past, not the future.** People are poor at predicting what they'd do. Instead of 'Would you use automatic reminders?', ask 'What did you do the last time a client paid late?'
- **Don't lead**: avoid 'Don't you find chasing payments frustrating?'
- **Dig deeper** with follow-ups: 'Why was that?', 'What happened next?', 'Can you show me?'
- **Use silence**: people often add the most useful details after a pause.
- **Don't pitch or defend the product.** If they criticise it, ask for more detail.
- Have a **note-taker**, ideally an engineer or designer from the team.

**5. Synthesise.** After each interview, write key observations and quotes within a few hours. After the set, look for **patterns** (affinity mapping: grouping notes by theme), and summarise them as insights with evidence: 'Six of eight participants chase late payments manually by text, and feel awkward doing it, because they worry about damaging the relationship.'

**6. Share and act.** Share a short summary with key quotes and video clips with the team and stakeholders, and link insights to decisions. In this example, the 'awkwardness' insight would shape the feature: reminders should come from the product, with a friendly tone that users can control.

I also try to make this **continuous**, e.g. talking to at least one or two customers every week, rather than only running big research projects."

#### Another worked example: interviewing churned customers
"To understand why customers cancelled:
- **Recruit**: customers who cancelled in the last 60 days, offering a voucher for 20 minutes.
- **Key questions**: 'Take me back to when you first signed up. What were you hoping for?' 'When did you first start thinking about leaving?' 'What did you switch to, and why?'
- **Avoid**: defending the product or trying to win them back during the interview.
- **Insight example**: several customers left after a price change, but deeper conversation revealed they'd stopped using key features months earlier. The price change was the trigger, not the cause."

#### Good versus bad questions
| Bad question | Why | Better question |
|---|---|---|
| "Would you use a reminders feature?" | Hypothetical; people say yes politely | "What did you do the last time a client paid late?" |
| "Don't you find this frustrating?" | Leading | "How do you feel about that part of the process?" |
| "Do you like our app?" | Yes/no; vague | "Tell me about the last time you used the app." |
| "What features should we build?" | Asks users to design | "What's the hardest part of managing your invoices?" |
| "How much would you pay for this?" | Unreliable | "What do you currently spend on solving this?" |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Goal and decision** the research informs.
2. **Recruit** the right segment (5–8 per segment).
3. **Discussion guide**: context → past stories → current solutions → impact.
4. **Run it**: open questions, past behaviour, follow-ups, silence, no pitching.
5. **Capture** notes and quotes quickly.
6. **Synthesise** patterns into insights.
7. **Share and act**, and make it continuous.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I'd ask customers what they want." | Focuses on past behaviour and real stories |
| Leading questions | Open, neutral questions |
| Pitches the product | Listens and digs deeper |
| No synthesis | Finds patterns and links insights to decisions |
| One-off research | Continuous discovery habit |

#### Useful phrases
- "Tell me about the last time you…"
- "What happened next?"
- "Why was that important to you?"
- "Can you show me how you do that today?"
- "There are no wrong answers. I'm here to learn from your experience."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Asking hypothetical questions** about future behaviour.
- **Leading questions.**
- **Pitching or defending the product.**
- **Talking too much.**
- **Only interviewing happy customers.**
- **Not synthesising or sharing** findings.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How many interviews are enough?"**
  "Usually 5–8 per segment reveal the main patterns. When you stop hearing new themes, you have enough for that question."
- **"How do you avoid confirmation bias?"**
  "Write hypotheses down before interviews, actively look for disconfirming evidence, involve others in synthesis, and use neutral questions."
- **"How do interviews fit with quantitative data?"**
  "Interviews explain why. Data shows how many and how often. I use interviews to generate hypotheses, then check them against data or with a survey or experiment."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Building a **continuous discovery** practice in the team.
- Involving **engineers and designers** in research.
- **Combining qualitative and quantitative** evidence for decisions.

#### Practise it
1. Write a discussion guide (8–10 questions) for a problem in a product you know.
2. Check every question: is it open? About the past? Non-leading?
3. Interview a friend for 15 minutes about how they do something (e.g. plan their weekly food shopping), then write three insights.

## What makes a good MVP, and how do you define one?
- id: po-product-sense-10
- round: PRODUCT_SENSE
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: MVP, scope, lean, learning

### Prompt
What does "minimum viable product" mean to you? How would you define the MVP for a new product or feature?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you understand that an MVP is about **learning**, not just shipping less.
- Your ability to **cut scope** ruthlessly while keeping it **valuable and usable**.
- Whether you define the MVP around a **hypothesis** and **success criteria**.
- Whether you avoid both extremes: **too big** (not minimal) and **too poor** (not viable).

#### Key points
- An **MVP** is the smallest thing you can build to **test your most important assumptions** with real users and **start learning**.
- "**Minimum**": only what's essential to test the hypothesis.
- "**Viable**": it must still **solve a real problem well enough** that people use it, and meet basic quality, security and legal standards.
- An MVP doesn't have to be software: **concierge** (doing it manually), **Wizard of Oz** (looks automated, people run it behind the scenes), **landing page**, **prototype**.
- Think "**slice the cake**" (a thin, complete experience), not "**build one layer at a time**".
- Define **success criteria** and what you'll do next based on results.

#### Model answer
"To me, an MVP is the **smallest version of a product that lets us learn whether our key assumptions are true**, with real users, as quickly and cheaply as possible.

The word 'viable' matters as much as 'minimum'. If it's too basic to actually solve the problem, users won't use it, and we'll learn the wrong lesson ('nobody wants this') when the real problem was a poor product.

**How I'd define an MVP**, using an example: a meal-planning feature for a grocery app.

**1. State the hypothesis.** 'Busy families will plan their weekly meals in our app, and that will increase their weekly basket size and order frequency.'

**2. Identify the riskiest assumptions:**
- Families want to plan meals in a grocery app (value).
- They'll find it easy enough to use weekly (usability).
- It increases basket size (business viability).

**3. Define the core user journey**, the minimum path to deliver value:
- Choose 3–5 recipes for the week from a small selection.
- Add all ingredients to the basket in one tap.
- Check out as usual.

**4. Cut everything not essential to that journey:**
- **In**: 30 curated family recipes, one-tap 'add ingredients to basket', simple portion adjustment.
- **Out for now**: dietary filters, personalised recommendations, nutrition tracking, saving favourite plans, sharing with family members.

**5. Keep it viable:** recipes must be good, ingredient quantities accurate, and products in stock. A poor experience here would ruin the test.

**6. Consider a non-software MVP first.** Could we test demand with a weekly email of three recipes with a 'shop these ingredients' link, before building anything in the app? That might tell us within two weeks whether families engage.

**7. Define success criteria.** For example: at least 15% of targeted families use it in the first month, at least 40% of those use it again the next week, and their basket size is at least 10% higher than a control group.

**8. Decide what happens next:**
- **Success**: invest in personalisation, filters and favourites.
- **Mixed**: interview users to understand why (recipes? effort?), and iterate.
- **Failure**: stop, and share what we learned."

#### Another worked example: a B2B MVP
"For a feature that automatically matches bank payments to invoices:
- **Hypothesis**: automatic matching saves small businesses time and reduces errors, increasing retention.
- **Concierge MVP**: for 20 customers, an analyst matches payments manually each week and the results show in the product as 'suggested matches'.
- **Learning**: do customers accept the suggestions? How much time do they say it saves?
- **Then**: build simple rule-based matching for the most common cases, and later smarter matching if the value is proven."

#### Types of MVP
| MVP type | Description | Good for testing |
|---|---|---|
| Landing page / fake door | Describe the product; measure sign-ups | Demand |
| Prototype | Clickable design | Usability, understanding |
| Concierge | Deliver the service manually | Value, before building |
| Wizard of Oz | Looks automated; humans behind the scenes | Value and experience |
| Single-feature product | One core feature built properly | Real usage and retention |
| Pilot | Full feature for a small group | Operational and business viability |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Hypothesis**: what we believe and why.
2. **Riskiest assumptions.**
3. **Core user journey**: the minimum path to value.
4. **Cut** everything else, explicitly.
5. **Ensure viability**: quality, security, legal.
6. **Consider non-software MVPs.**
7. **Success criteria** and next steps.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "An MVP is version 1 with fewer features." | An MVP is about learning, tied to a hypothesis |
| Cuts quality to go faster | Keeps it viable: solves the problem properly |
| Builds one technical layer at a time | Delivers a thin, complete user journey |
| No success criteria | Clear criteria and next steps |
| Always builds software | Considers concierge, Wizard of Oz and prototypes |

#### Useful phrases
- "An MVP is the fastest way to learn whether our key assumptions are true."
- "'Viable' matters: if it doesn't solve the problem well enough, we'll learn the wrong lesson."
- "Here's what's in, and what's deliberately out for now."
- "Could we test this without building software first?"
- "If we hit these numbers, we'll invest further; if not, we'll learn why."

#### Common pitfalls
- **An MVP that isn't minimal**: months of work before any learning.
- **An MVP that isn't viable**: too poor to test the real idea.
- **No hypothesis or success criteria.**
- **Never moving beyond the MVP**, or never fixing its shortcuts.
- **Confusing an MVP with a prototype** or a beta in conversation, without being clear.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How do you stop stakeholders adding features to the MVP?"**
  "Keep everyone focused on the hypothesis: 'Does this feature help us test whether families will plan meals with us?' If not, it goes on the 'later' list."
- **"What if the MVP embarrasses the brand?"**
  "Viable includes brand quality. We can limit the audience (beta users, a small region) and label it clearly as early access."
- **"What's the difference between an MVP and an MLP (minimum lovable product)?"**
  "An MLP emphasises that the first version should be delightful, not just functional, especially in competitive consumer markets where users have alternatives."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Using MVPs as a **learning strategy** across a portfolio of bets.
- **Communicating MVP trade-offs** to executives.
- Planning the path **from MVP to scalable product**, including paying down shortcuts.

#### Practise it
1. Pick a product idea (e.g. "a pet-sitting marketplace for your neighbourhood").
2. Write the hypothesis, the riskiest assumption, a non-software MVP, and a software MVP with "in" and "out" lists.
3. Define success criteria and explain the plan **out loud in 4 minutes**.

## What's the difference between outputs and outcomes?
- id: po-product-sense-11
- round: PRODUCT_SENSE
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: outcomes, outputs, impact, product thinking

### Prompt
What's the difference between outputs and outcomes? Why does it matter for a Product Owner?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you think in terms of **value and impact**, not just delivery.
- Whether you can explain a **core product concept** clearly.
- How this changes the way you **set goals, plan and measure success**.

#### Key points
- **Output**: what the team produces: features, releases, stories completed.
- **Outcome**: the **change in user behaviour** that results, which drives business results (e.g. more users completing onboarding).
- **Impact**: the higher-level **business result** (e.g. revenue, retention, cost savings).
- Outputs are **means**; outcomes are **ends**. Shipping a feature isn't success; changing behaviour is.
- Outcome focus changes **roadmaps, OKRs, team empowerment and how you measure success**.
- Reference: Joshua Seiden's book *Outcomes Over Output*.

#### Model answer
"**Outputs** are the things we make: features, releases, completed stories. **Outcomes** are the changes in **user behaviour** those things create, which in turn drive **business impact** like revenue or retention.

For example:
- **Output**: 'We launched a new onboarding checklist.'
- **Outcome**: 'New users complete account setup 40% faster, and 15% more of them send their first invoice within a day.'
- **Impact**: 'Trial-to-paid conversion increased, adding £X in annual revenue.'

**Why it matters for a Product Owner:**
1. **Shipping isn't success.** A team can deliver every feature on time and still fail if nothing changes for users. Focusing on outcomes keeps us honest about whether our work matters.
2. **It changes how we set goals.** Instead of 'deliver features A, B and C this quarter', we set goals like 'increase activation from 35% to 45%'. The team then decides which features (outputs) will best achieve that, and can change course if something doesn't work.
3. **It empowers the team.** Giving engineers and designers a problem to solve, rather than a list of features, uses their creativity and usually leads to better solutions.
4. **It improves prioritisation.** We can compare work by its expected effect on outcomes, rather than by who asked for it.
5. **It encourages learning.** If an output doesn't change the outcome, we try something else, instead of moving on to the next feature on the list.

In practice, I frame roadmap items and OKRs as outcomes, measure them after every release, and celebrate outcomes, not just launches."

#### Another worked example: reframing a stakeholder request
"A stakeholder asked: 'We need a new dashboard for account managers.'
- I asked what they hoped would change. The answer: account managers spend too long preparing for client calls and miss early signs of churn.
- **Outcome reframed**: 'Account managers spot at-risk clients earlier, and spend less time preparing for calls.'
- **Measures**: preparation time per call, and the share of churned accounts flagged as at-risk at least 30 days earlier.
- **Result**: the team found a simple weekly 'at-risk accounts' email solved most of the problem faster than a full dashboard."

#### Output versus outcome versus impact
| | Output | Outcome | Impact |
|---|---|---|---|
| Definition | What we build | What users do differently | Business result |
| Example | "Launched search filters" | "Users find products 30% faster; more add to basket" | "Revenue up 4%" |
| Team control | High | Medium | Low (many factors) |
| Measures | Features shipped, velocity | Behaviour metrics | Revenue, retention, costs |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Define each term** with an example.
2. **Show the chain**: output → outcome → impact.
3. **Explain why it matters**: success, goals, empowerment, prioritisation, learning.
4. **Show how you apply it**: roadmaps, OKRs, measurement.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "Outputs and outcomes are both results." | Clearly distinguishes what we build from what changes |
| Success = shipping on time | Success = behaviour and business change |
| No example | Concrete example of output, outcome and impact |
| Doesn't connect it to practice | Explains how it shapes roadmaps, OKRs and team empowerment |

#### Useful phrases
- "Shipping a feature is an output. Users behaving differently is the outcome."
- "I'd rather give the team a problem to solve than a list of features to build."
- "What do we hope users will do differently once this is live?"
- "We measure success by the outcome, not by whether we shipped."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Measuring success by delivery** (velocity, features shipped).
- **Confusing outcomes with impact**: impact is often too far removed for a team to steer by.
- **Outcome goals that are too vague** ("improve experience").
- **Never checking** whether launched features changed anything.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How do you get stakeholders to think in outcomes?"**
  "When they bring a feature request, I ask 'what do you hope will change?' and reframe it together. Over time, showing results in outcome terms helps them think that way too."
- **"Aren't some things just outputs, like compliance work?"**
  "Some work is required (e.g. regulation), but there's still an outcome: avoiding fines or keeping our licence. We should still deliver it in the way that minimises user friction."
- **"How quickly can you measure outcomes?"**
  "Some quickly (e.g. task completion), others take longer (e.g. retention). I use leading indicators to steer in the meantime."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Building an **outcome-driven culture** across teams and with leadership.
- Designing **empowered teams** with outcome-based goals.
- Connecting **team outcomes to company impact** through metric trees and OKRs.

#### Practise it
1. Take five items from a roadmap (real or imagined) and rewrite each as an outcome with a metric.
2. For one of them, map output → outcome → impact.
3. Explain the difference **out loud in 90 seconds**.

## Explain Jobs to be Done and how you'd use it
- id: po-product-sense-12
- round: PRODUCT_SENSE
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: jobs to be done, JTBD, customer needs, discovery

### Prompt
What is the Jobs to be Done framework? How have you used it, or how would you use it, in product work?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Familiarity with a **widely used framework** for understanding customer needs.
- Whether you understand the **core idea**: customers "hire" products to make progress in a situation.
- Whether you can **apply** it practically: research, positioning, competition, product decisions.

#### Key points
- **Jobs to be Done (JTBD)**: customers don't buy products for their own sake. They "**hire**" a product to get a "**job**" done: to make progress in a particular **situation**.
- Popularised by **Clayton Christensen** (the "milkshake" story) and developed by others, e.g. Bob Moesta and Tony Ulwick.
- A job has **functional**, **emotional** and **social** dimensions.
- **Job statement** format: "**When** [situation], **I want to** [motivation], **so I can** [expected outcome]."
- JTBD reveals the **real competition**, which is often not similar products but any alternative, including doing nothing.
- Useful for **discovery interviews**, **positioning**, **prioritisation** and **innovation**.

#### Model answer
"Jobs to be Done is a way of understanding why customers choose products. The core idea is that people don't buy products for their own sake. They **'hire' them to make progress** in a particular situation, and they **'fire'** them when something better comes along.

A famous example from Clayton Christensen is the **milkshake** study. A fast-food chain wanted to sell more milkshakes and initially asked customers how to improve them (thicker? more flavours?), which didn't help. Researchers then looked at *when* and *why* people bought them, and found many were bought early in the morning by commuters. Their 'job' was: 'When I'm driving to work alone and bored, I want something that keeps me occupied and full until lunch, and that I can consume with one hand.' The milkshake competed with bananas, bagels and boredom, not other milkshakes. Making it thicker (so it lasted longer) and quicker to buy served that job better.

**Job statement format:**
> When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome].

**Three dimensions of a job:**
- **Functional**: the practical task (get paid on time).
- **Emotional**: how they want to feel (not anxious about cash flow).
- **Social**: how they want to be seen (professional to clients).

**How I use it:**
1. **Discovery interviews**: I ask about the moment people started looking for a solution ('What was going on when you first started looking for an invoicing tool?'), what they tried, and what made them switch. These **switch interviews** reveal the real job.
2. **Understanding competition**: for an invoicing app, the real competition includes spreadsheets, Word templates, an accountant, or doing nothing.
3. **Prioritisation**: features that help customers get the core job done better rise to the top.
4. **Positioning and messaging**: speaking to the job ('Get paid faster without awkward chasing') rather than features ('Automated reminder workflows').

**Example.** For our invoicing product, interviews revealed a key job: 'When I've finished a piece of work for a client, I want to get paid quickly without feeling awkward or unprofessional, so I can keep my cash flow healthy and focus on my work.' This shifted our priorities toward payment reminders and one-click payment links, and away from advanced reporting, which mattered much less for that job."

#### Another worked example: a consumer product
"For a meal-kit service:
- **Job**: 'When it's a busy weekday evening, I want to cook something healthy and interesting without thinking about what to make or shopping for it, so I can feel good about feeding my family well.'
- **Real competition**: takeaways, ready meals, cooking the same five meals on repeat, supermarket delivery.
- **Insight**: the emotional part (feeling like a good parent; avoiding decision fatigue) mattered as much as the functional part.
- **Product decisions**: quicker recipes for weekdays, a 'family favourites' option, and less focus on exotic recipes."

#### JTBD at a glance
| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Job | Progress a person wants to make in a situation | Get paid on time without awkwardness |
| Hire / fire | Choose / abandon a product for the job | Switch from spreadsheets to an app |
| Functional dimension | Practical task | Send invoices, track payments |
| Emotional dimension | How they want to feel | Confident, not anxious |
| Social dimension | How they want to be seen | Professional |
| Real competition | Any alternative for the job | Spreadsheets, accountant, doing nothing |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Explain the core idea** (hire and fire; progress in a situation).
2. **Give a memorable example** (milkshake or your own).
3. **Show the job statement format** and the three dimensions.
4. **Explain how you use it** (interviews, competition, prioritisation, messaging).
5. **Share a real or realistic example** of a decision it changed.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "It's about what customers need." | Explains hire/fire and progress in a situation |
| No example | Uses the milkshake story or a personal example |
| Only functional needs | Includes emotional and social dimensions |
| Theory only | Shows how it changed a product decision |

#### Useful phrases
- "Customers hire products to make progress in a specific situation."
- "When did you first start looking for a solution, and what was going on at the time?"
- "Our real competition isn't just similar apps, it's spreadsheets and doing nothing."
- "The emotional side of the job turned out to be just as important."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Describing JTBD as just "user needs"** without the situation and progress elements.
- **Writing jobs that are really features** ("When I use the app, I want reminders").
- **Ignoring emotional and social dimensions.**
- **Not showing practical application.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How is JTBD different from personas?"**
  "Personas describe *who* the user is. JTBD describes *what progress* they're trying to make in a situation. Different personas can share the same job, and one person can have different jobs at different times."
- **"What's a switch interview?"**
  "An interview with someone who recently started using (or stopped using) a product, tracing the timeline from the first thought of change to the decision, to understand the forces that drove the switch."
- **"What are the 'forces of progress'?"**
  "Push of the current situation, pull of the new solution, anxiety about the new solution, and habit of the present. Products succeed when push and pull outweigh anxiety and habit."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Using JTBD to shape **product strategy** and identify **new opportunities**.
- Aligning **marketing, sales and product** around customer jobs.
- Combining JTBD with **quantitative** validation.

#### Practise it
1. Write job statements (functional, emotional, social) for three products you use.
2. For one, list the real competition, including non-obvious alternatives.
3. Think about a time you switched products, and map the forces of progress.
4. Explain JTBD **out loud in 3 minutes** with your own example.

## How do you create and use personas?
- id: po-product-sense-13
- round: PRODUCT_SENSE
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: personas, user research, segmentation

### Prompt
Do you use personas? How do you create them, and how do you make sure they're actually useful?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you build personas from **real research**, not assumptions.
- Whether you know how to make personas **useful for decisions**, not just posters on a wall.
- Awareness of **limitations** and alternatives (JTBD, segments).

#### Key points
- A **persona** is a research-based representation of a key **user type**: goals, behaviours, frustrations and context.
- Build from **real data**: interviews, analytics, surveys, support conversations.
- Focus on **behaviours, goals and needs**, not irrelevant demographics.
- Keep the number **small** (typically 3–5) and identify a **primary persona**.
- **Use them actively**: in prioritisation, design reviews, user stories, and testing recruitment.
- Be aware of **limitations**: they can become stereotypes or stale. Update them, and combine with JTBD.

#### Model answer
"Yes, when they're grounded in research and used actively. Badly made personas, based on assumptions, can do more harm than good.

**How I create them:**
1. **Gather data**: customer interviews (ideally 15–20 across segments), product analytics (how different groups behave), surveys, and support and sales conversations.
2. **Look for behavioural patterns**: I group users by **goals, behaviours and needs**, not by demographics. For an invoicing product, the meaningful differences might be invoicing frequency, technical confidence, and whether they have an accountant, not age or gender.
3. **Define 3–5 personas**, each with:
   - A name and short description (for memorability).
   - **Goals**: what they're trying to achieve.
   - **Behaviours**: how they work today, tools used, frequency.
   - **Frustrations and needs.**
   - **Context**: device, environment, time pressure.
   - A few **real quotes** from research.
4. **Choose a primary persona**: the one we optimise for when trade-offs arise.

**Example personas for an invoicing product:**
- **'Sam the sole trader'** (primary): a freelance designer sending about 8 invoices a month, mostly on a phone between jobs. Goal: get paid quickly with minimal admin. Frustrations: chasing late payments, losing track of who has paid. Quote: 'I just want to do the work, not be an accountant.'
- **'Priya the small business owner'**: runs a 6-person agency, 40 invoices a month, works with an accountant. Goal: accurate records and easy handover to the accountant. Frustrations: reconciling payments, VAT.
- **'Alex the accountant'**: manages books for 30 small clients. Goal: clean, consistent data across clients. Frustrations: inconsistent formats and missing information.

**How I keep them useful:**
- **In prioritisation**: 'Which persona does this help, and how much?'
- **In user stories**: 'As Sam, I want…', which keeps the context concrete.
- **In design reviews**: 'Would Sam be able to do this on a phone between jobs?'
- **In research recruitment**: making sure we test with each key persona.
- **Updating them** at least yearly, or when the market or product changes.

**Limitations.** Personas can become stereotypes or outdated, and they describe *who* but not always *why* users choose a product. I combine them with **Jobs to be Done** and real data."

#### Another worked example: when personas were misleading
"In one team, personas had been created in a workshop without research, and the primary persona was a 'tech-savvy young professional'. Analytics showed most active users were actually small business owners over 45 using desktop computers. We re-did the personas from interviews and data, which shifted our design priorities toward clarity and desktop workflows, and improved satisfaction scores among our largest group."

#### Good versus bad personas
| Bad persona | Good persona |
|---|---|
| Based on assumptions | Based on research and data |
| Lots of irrelevant demographics | Focus on goals, behaviours and frustrations |
| Ten personas | 3–5, with a clear primary persona |
| Created once, forgotten | Used in decisions and updated regularly |
| Stereotypes | Real quotes and observed behaviour |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Gather research data** (qualitative and quantitative).
2. **Find behavioural patterns.**
3. **Define 3–5 personas** with goals, behaviours, frustrations, context and quotes.
4. **Choose a primary persona.**
5. **Use them in decisions**, stories, designs and testing.
6. **Update** regularly and combine with JTBD.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "We made personas in a workshop." | Built from interviews and data |
| Age, gender and hobbies | Goals, behaviours and frustrations |
| Not used after creation | Used in prioritisation, stories and design reviews |
| No awareness of limitations | Knows the risks and combines with JTBD |

#### Useful phrases
- "Our personas are based on 20 interviews and behavioural data."
- "We segment by behaviour and goals, not demographics."
- "When trade-offs arise, we optimise for our primary persona."
- "Would Sam be able to do this on a phone between jobs?"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Assumption-based personas.**
- **Irrelevant demographic details.**
- **Too many personas**, so none guide decisions.
- **Never using or updating them.**
- **Stereotyping** users.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"How do personas relate to segments in analytics?"**
  "Ideally they're linked: each persona maps to a behavioural segment we can measure, so we can track how each group uses the product."
- **"What's an anti-persona?"**
  "A type of user we're deliberately *not* designing for, e.g. large enterprises for a small-business product. It helps say no to requests that don't fit."
- **"Personas or JTBD?"**
  "Both. Personas help with empathy and context. JTBD explains the progress people want. They work well together."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Research rigour** and linking personas to **measurable segments**.
- Using personas to **align teams** across product, design, marketing and sales.
- Knowing when **other tools** fit better.

#### Practise it
1. Create two personas for a product you know, using reviews and your own observations as "research".
2. Include goals, behaviours, frustrations, context and a quote for each.
3. Write one anti-persona.
4. Explain **out loud in 3 minutes** how you'd use them in a prioritisation decision.
