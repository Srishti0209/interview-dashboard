# Product Owner — Stakeholders & Agile Delivery

## What is the role of a Product Owner in Scrum, and how is it different from a Product Manager?
- id: po-agile-01
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: scrum, role definition, product manager

### Prompt
What does a Product Owner actually do in a Scrum team? How is that different from a Product Manager?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you understand the **official definition** of the role (the Scrum Guide).
- Whether you understand how the role works in **real companies**, which often differs.
- Your view of what makes a **great** Product Owner, beyond the minimum.
- Whether you know how the PO relates to the **Scrum Master** and **developers**.

#### Key points
- In the **Scrum Guide**, the Product Owner is **accountable for maximising the value** of the product resulting from the Scrum Team's work.
- The PO is accountable for **Product Backlog management**: developing and communicating the **Product Goal**, creating and ordering backlog items, and making sure the backlog is visible and understood.
- The PO is **one person, not a committee**. They take input from many, but the decisions on backlog order are theirs, and the organisation must respect them.
- **Product Manager** is a **job title**, often broader and more outward-facing (market, strategy, pricing, go-to-market). **Product Owner** is a **Scrum accountability**.
- In practice, one person often does both. In larger organisations they may be split.

#### Model answer
"According to the Scrum Guide, the Product Owner is accountable for **maximising the value** the team delivers. In practice, that breaks down into a few responsibilities:

1. **Setting direction**: defining and communicating the **Product Goal**, a longer-term objective the team works toward, and making sure each sprint contributes to it.
2. **Owning the Product Backlog**: creating clear backlog items, ordering them by value, and making sure everyone understands what's at the top and why.
3. **Representing customers and the business**: being the voice of users and stakeholders in the team, available to answer questions quickly so the team isn't blocked.
4. **Making decisions**: deciding what gets built and in what order, and saying no to things that don't fit.
5. **Inspecting the work**: reviewing increments against acceptance criteria in the Sprint Review, and gathering feedback from stakeholders.
6. **Managing stakeholders**: collecting input, setting expectations and communicating progress.

The Scrum Guide is explicit that the PO is one person. That matters because a team needs one clear voice for priorities, not five stakeholders giving conflicting instructions.

**Relationship with the rest of the Scrum Team:**
- **Developers** decide **how** to build things and how much they can take on in a sprint. The PO doesn't assign tasks or manage the team.
- The **Scrum Master** helps the team and organisation use Scrum effectively: coaching, facilitating and removing impediments.
- The PO owns the **what** and the **why**.

**Product Owner versus Product Manager.** 'Product Manager' is a job title, not a Scrum role, and it usually has a broader, more outward-facing scope: market research, competitive analysis, product strategy, pricing, go-to-market and business outcomes over a longer horizon. 'Product Owner' focuses on working with the delivery team to maximise value, sprint by sprint.

How it works in reality varies:
- In many tech companies, the **Product Manager is the Product Owner** for their team: one person does both.
- In larger or SAFe organisations, a **Product Manager** might own strategy for several teams, with a **Product Owner** per team handling the backlog and day-to-day decisions.
- In some companies, especially in banking or the public sector, the PO is a business expert who knows the domain deeply.

In my view, the best Product Owners don't act as backlog administrators or proxies passing messages along. They understand customers, the business and the strategy deeply, so they can make good decisions quickly and explain them."

#### Another worked example: a typical week as a PO
"To make it concrete, a typical week for me might include:
- **Monday**: sprint planning. Present the sprint goal and top backlog items, answer questions, and agree the plan with developers.
- **Daily**: join the stand-up as needed to answer questions and unblock decisions.
- **Tuesday and Thursday**: backlog refinement with the team. Clarify upcoming items and split large ones.
- **Wednesday**: two customer interviews and a review of product data.
- **Thursday**: stakeholder meeting to share progress, gather input and discuss trade-offs.
- **Friday**: preparation for the next sprint review and planning.
- **Every two weeks**: Sprint Review with stakeholders, and a Sprint Retrospective with the team."

#### Scrum accountabilities at a glance
| | Product Owner | Scrum Master | Developers |
|---|---|---|---|
| Focus | What and why (value) | How well Scrum works | How to build it |
| Owns | Product Backlog, Product Goal | Team effectiveness, impediments | Sprint Backlog, the increment's quality |
| Key events | Planning, Review, Refinement | Facilitates events, coaches | Daily Scrum, Planning |
| Not responsible for | Assigning tasks | Making product decisions | Deciding priorities |

#### Step-by-step framework for answering
1. **Give the official definition** (maximise value; Product Backlog accountability).
2. **List the key responsibilities** with examples.
3. **Explain the relationship** with the Scrum Master and developers.
4. **Contrast with Product Manager**, then explain the reality in companies.
5. **Share your view** of what makes a great PO.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "The PO writes requirements for developers." | Describes the PO as accountable for maximising value |
| "The PO manages the team." | Knows developers self-manage and the PO doesn't assign tasks |
| "PO and PM are completely different." | Explains the title-versus-accountability difference with nuance |
| Only theory | Gives concrete examples of what the PO does each week |
| No point of view | Shares a view of what makes a great PO |

#### Useful phrases
- "In the Scrum Guide, the Product Owner is accountable for maximising the value of the product."
- "The PO owns the what and the why; the developers own the how."
- "Product Manager is a job title; Product Owner is a Scrum accountability. In many companies, one person does both."
- "A great PO is empowered to make decisions, not just a messenger between stakeholders and the team."

#### Common pitfalls
- Describing the PO as a **requirements writer** or **proxy**.
- Getting the **Scrum Guide wrong**, e.g. saying the PO manages the team or assigns work.
- Saying PO and PM are **completely different** without nuance.
- Forgetting the **Product Goal**, part of the Scrum Guide since 2020.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What's the difference between the PO and the Scrum Master?"**
  "The PO is accountable for value and the backlog: what we build and why. The Scrum Master is accountable for the team's effectiveness: coaching on Scrum, facilitating events and removing impediments."
- **"How much time do you spend with the team versus stakeholders and customers?"**
  "Roughly balanced, but it shifts. During discovery, I spend more time with customers. Close to a release, more with the team."
- **"What would you do if you weren't empowered to make decisions?"**
  "I'd build trust by showing good decisions backed by evidence, agree clear decision rights with my manager and stakeholders, and escalate when the lack of empowerment is slowing the team down."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- A clear view on **empowerment**: a PO who has to ask permission for every decision can't maximise value.
- How the role **scales**: multiple teams, Chief Product Owner models, alignment across teams.
- How the PO and PM roles should be **set up** in an organisation for the best results.

#### Practise it
1. Write a 60-second definition of the PO role in your own words, mentioning value, the Product Backlog and the Product Goal.
2. Write a 60-second explanation of PO versus PM, with one example from a company you know.
3. Practise both **out loud**, then answer: "What makes a great Product Owner?" in under 90 seconds.

## Write a user story with acceptance criteria
- id: po-agile-02
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: easy
- levels:
- tags: user stories, acceptance criteria, INVEST, gherkin

### Prompt
Walk me through how you'd write a user story for a "reset password" feature, including acceptance criteria. What makes a good user story?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you can write a **clear, valuable, testable** user story.
- Whether you think about **edge cases, errors and security**, not just the happy path.
- Your knowledge of **INVEST** and **acceptance criteria** formats.
- Whether you see stories as a **conversation with the team**, not a specification handed over.

#### Key points
- Format: **"As a [type of user], I want [goal], so that [benefit]."** The *so that* is the most important part, because it explains **why**.
- **Acceptance criteria** define when the story is done. They're often written as **Given / When / Then** (Gherkin format).
- A good story follows **INVEST**: Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable.
- Think of the **3 Cs** (Ron Jeffries): **Card** (the short story), **Conversation** (the discussion with the team), **Confirmation** (acceptance criteria).
- Always include **unhappy paths, security and non-functional** aspects.

#### Model answer
"Here's how I'd write it.

**User story:**
> As a registered user who has forgotten my password, I want to reset it using my email address, so that I can get back into my account without having to contact support.

The *so that* matters, because it tells the team the goal is **self-service access**, which also reduces support costs. If a solution meets the goal better, the team can propose it.

**Acceptance criteria (Given / When / Then):**
1. **Requesting a reset**: *Given* I'm on the login page, *when* I click 'Forgot password' and enter my registered email, *then* I receive an email with a reset link within 2 minutes.
2. **Unknown email**: *Given* I enter an email that isn't registered, *when* I submit, *then* I see the same message as a registered user ('If an account exists, we've sent a link'), so attackers can't discover which emails have accounts.
3. **Setting a new password**: *Given* I open a valid reset link within 60 minutes, *when* I enter a new password that meets the password policy, *then* my password is updated and I'm signed in.
4. **Password policy**: *Given* I enter a password that doesn't meet the policy, *then* I see clear guidance about what's required, and the password isn't changed.
5. **Expired or used link**: *Given* the link is older than 60 minutes or has already been used, *when* I open it, *then* I see an 'expired link' message with a button to request a new one.
6. **Security after reset**: *Given* I've reset my password, *then* all my other active sessions are signed out and I receive an email confirming the change ('If this wasn't you, contact us').
7. **Rate limiting**: *Given* someone requests more than 5 resets for the same email within an hour, *then* further requests are blocked for an hour.

**Notes for the conversation with the team:**
- Link to designs for the forgot-password form, email and new-password screen.
- Analytics events: reset requested, email opened, reset completed.
- Accessibility: the form works with screen readers and keyboard navigation.
- Open questions: should we also support resetting by SMS? (I'd suggest a separate story.)

**Why this is a good story (INVEST):**
- **Independent**: it can be built and released without other stories.
- **Negotiable**: details like link expiry time are discussed with the team, not dictated.
- **Valuable**: clear benefit for users and reduced support costs.
- **Estimable**: the team understands it well enough to size it.
- **Small**: fits in a sprint. SMS reset is split out into a separate story.
- **Testable**: every acceptance criterion can be verified."

#### Another worked example: a business-focused story
"For an e-commerce product:
> As a returning customer, I want to reorder a previous order with one click, so that I can save time on repeat purchases.

Acceptance criteria:
1. *Given* I'm viewing a past order, *when* I click 'Reorder', *then* all items that are still available are added to my basket with the same quantities.
2. *Given* some items are out of stock, *then* I see which items couldn't be added, with suggested alternatives.
3. *Given* prices have changed since my original order, *then* the basket shows current prices, with a note that prices have changed.
4. *Given* I already have items in my basket, *then* the reordered items are added to them rather than replacing them.

Again the *so that* (save time on repeat purchases) guides the details, and the edge cases (stock, prices, existing basket) are where bugs and complaints usually come from."

#### Ways to split a large story (SPIDR)
| Technique | Split by | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **S**pikes | Research first | "Investigate SMS providers" before building SMS reset |
| **P**aths | Different user flows | Reset by email first, by SMS later |
| **I**nterfaces | Platforms or devices | Web first, then mobile app |
| **D**ata | Types of data | Support UK phone numbers first, international later |
| **R**ules | Business rules | Basic password policy first, advanced rules later |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Identify the user** and their **goal**.
2. **Write the story** with a clear *so that*.
3. **Write acceptance criteria**: happy path, errors, edge cases, security.
4. **Add notes**: designs, analytics, accessibility, open questions.
5. **Check INVEST**, and split it if it's too big.
6. **Refine with the team** before the sprint.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "As a user, I want a reset password button." | Clear user, goal and benefit (*so that*) |
| Only the happy path | Covers errors, expiry, security and rate limiting |
| "It should work properly." | Concrete, testable acceptance criteria |
| Technical task ("create API endpoint") | User-focused story; technical tasks are the team's choice |
| Handed over as a finished spec | Refined together with the team |

#### Useful phrases
- "The *so that* is the most important part, because it explains why we're building it."
- "I always include unhappy paths and security in acceptance criteria, because that's where incidents come from."
- "This story feels too big, so I'd split it by path: email first, SMS as a separate story."
- "A story is a placeholder for a conversation with the team, not a full specification."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Writing tasks instead of stories** ("Create reset password API").
- **Missing the *so that*.**
- **Vague acceptance criteria** ("works correctly", "user-friendly").
- **Forgetting unhappy paths** and security.
- **Stories that are too big** to finish in one sprint.
- **Writing stories alone** and handing them to the team.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"Who writes the acceptance criteria?"**
  "I draft them, then refine them with developers and QA. Engineers often spot edge cases and technical constraints I've missed."
- **"What's the difference between acceptance criteria and the Definition of Done?"**
  "Acceptance criteria are specific to one story: what it must do. The Definition of Done applies to every story: quality standards like code review, tests and deployment."
- **"How detailed should a story be before sprint planning?"**
  "Detailed enough that the team understands the goal and the acceptance criteria and can estimate it, but not so detailed that there's no room for the team's ideas."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Including **non-functional requirements**: security, performance, accessibility, analytics.
- Knowing when user stories are the **wrong tool**, e.g. platform work framed as "enablers" with clear outcomes.
- Coaching teams to write and refine stories **together**.

#### Practise it
1. Write user stories with full acceptance criteria for:
   - "Change delivery address after placing an order."
   - "Export my transactions to a spreadsheet."
   - "Invite a teammate to my workspace."
2. For each, include at least 2 unhappy paths and 1 security or privacy criterion.
3. Check each story against INVEST, and split one using a SPIDR technique.

## How do you run backlog refinement, and what are the Definition of Ready and Definition of Done?
- id: po-agile-03
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: refinement, definition of done, definition of ready, scrum

### Prompt
How do you run backlog refinement with your team? What do "Definition of Ready" and "Definition of Done" mean, and how do you use them?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Practical experience of **running refinement** well.
- Understanding of the **Definition of Done** (an official Scrum commitment) versus the **Definition of Ready** (an optional team practice).
- Whether you **collaborate** with the team or just hand over requirements.
- How you keep the backlog **ready** without creating bureaucracy.

#### Key points
- **Refinement** is an ongoing activity to make upcoming backlog items clear, small and estimated. A common guideline is up to about **10%** of the team's capacity.
- **Definition of Done (DoD)**: the formal description of the quality an increment must meet. It's a **commitment** in the Scrum Guide. If an item doesn't meet the DoD, it isn't done.
- **Definition of Ready (DoR)**: an optional team agreement about when an item is ready to be pulled into a sprint. Useful guidance, but it shouldn't become a rigid gate.
- Aim to have about **1.5–2 sprints** of ready work at the top of the backlog.

#### Model answer
"**How I run refinement:**
- **Cadence**: one or two sessions a week, 45–60 minutes each, plus ongoing informal conversations.
- **Who attends**: the whole team, or a representative group (e.g. tech lead, a frontend and a backend engineer, QA and the designer), depending on team size.
- **Preparation is my responsibility.** Before each session, the top items have:
  - The problem and why it matters, with data or customer quotes where possible.
  - A draft user story and acceptance criteria.
  - Designs or wireframes if needed.
  - Known constraints and open questions.
- **In the session:**
  1. I explain the *why* and the goal of each item.
  2. The team asks questions and challenges the approach. They often suggest simpler solutions.
  3. We identify risks, dependencies and unknowns.
  4. We split large items into smaller ones.
  5. We estimate, using story points or t-shirt sizes. The real value is the discussion: if one person says 2 points and another says 13, they understand the work differently, and talking it through reveals hidden complexity.
- **Outcome**: about 1.5–2 sprints of ready items, so sprint planning is quick and focused.

**Definition of Ready**, a team agreement. For example, an item is ready when:
- The user value and acceptance criteria are clear.
- Designs are available, if needed.
- Dependencies are identified and not blocking.
- It's small enough to finish within a sprint.
- The team has estimated it.

I treat the DoR as **guidance, not a rigid gate**. Sometimes we start an item with one open question, if we know who will answer it in time. A strict DoR can become an excuse never to start anything.

**Definition of Done**, the quality standard for every item. For example:
- Code reviewed and merged.
- Automated tests written and passing.
- Acceptance criteria met and verified.
- Accessibility checks done.
- Analytics events implemented.
- Documentation and release notes updated.
- Deployed to production, or at least to a production-like environment, behind a feature flag if needed.

The DoD protects quality and gives everyone the same understanding of 'done'. An item that doesn't meet it **isn't done**: it doesn't get shown as complete in the Sprint Review, and it goes back into the backlog. Over time, I'd work with the team to **raise** the DoD, e.g. toward deploying to production on every merge."

#### Another worked example: improving poor refinement
"I once joined a team whose refinement sessions took 2 hours and still left stories unclear. I changed three things:
1. **Preparation**: I shared items 2 days before the session so engineers could read and comment in advance.
2. **Time-boxing**: a maximum of 10 minutes per item. If it wasn't clear by then, we'd schedule a short follow-up with the right people.
3. **Spikes**: for very uncertain items, we agreed a short technical investigation instead of guessing estimates.

Within a month, sessions were down to 50 minutes, sprint planning was faster, and work carried over between sprints dropped noticeably."

#### Definition of Ready versus Definition of Done
| | Definition of Ready | Definition of Done |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | When an item can start | When an item is complete |
| In the Scrum Guide? | No (optional practice) | Yes (a formal commitment) |
| Applies to | Backlog items before the sprint | Every increment |
| Risk if misused | Becomes a bureaucratic gate | Weak DoD hides unfinished work |
| Example | "Acceptance criteria agreed, designs ready" | "Tested, reviewed, deployed" |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Prepare** the top items (problem, story, criteria, designs, questions).
2. **Explain the why** in the session.
3. **Discuss, challenge and split** with the team.
4. **Estimate** to surface misunderstandings.
5. **Check readiness** against the DoR (as guidance).
6. **Keep 1.5–2 sprints ready.**
7. **Apply the DoD** strictly, and improve it over time.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "The PO writes stories and the team estimates them." | Refinement is a collaborative discussion that shapes solutions |
| No preparation | PO prepares the problem, data and designs in advance |
| Uses the DoR as a hard gate | Uses the DoR as guidance |
| Doesn't know the DoD is in the Scrum Guide | Knows the DoD is a formal commitment |
| Weak DoD (no testing or deployment) | Strong DoD that protects quality |

#### Useful phrases
- "I come to refinement with the problem and the why. The team helps shape the how."
- "When estimates differ widely, it's a sign we understand the work differently, so we talk it through."
- "The Definition of Done is non-negotiable. If it isn't met, the item isn't done."
- "I use the Definition of Ready as guidance, not a gate, so it doesn't slow us down."

#### Common pitfalls
- **The PO writing everything alone**, with the team seeing stories for the first time at planning.
- **Unprepared refinement sessions** that waste the team's time.
- **Using the DoR as an excuse** not to start work.
- **A weak DoD** that hides unfinished work (no tests, no deployment).
- **Refining too far ahead**: detailed stories for work six months away will change.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if refinement sessions are long and unproductive?"**
  "Better preparation, sharing items in advance, smaller groups, time-boxing per item, and spikes for very uncertain work."
- **"How do you handle items that keep being re-estimated?"**
  "They're usually too big or too uncertain. Split them, or run a time-boxed spike to reduce uncertainty first."
- **"Should designers be in refinement?"**
  "Yes, especially for user-facing work. Designers and engineers exploring solutions together leads to better and more feasible designs."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- Refinement as **collaborative discovery**, with engineers involved early.
- **Raising the DoD** over time (e.g. toward continuous deployment).
- **Aligning DoD** across multiple teams that release together.
- Keeping the backlog lean and **outcome-focused**.

#### Practise it
1. Write a Definition of Done for a team you've worked in (or would like to work in), with at least 7 items.
2. Write a Definition of Ready with at least 5 items, and note which ones you'd treat as flexible.
3. Describe **out loud in 4 minutes** how you'd run a refinement session, including what you'd prepare beforehand.

## The team keeps missing its sprint goals — what do you do?
- id: po-agile-04
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: sprint goals, retrospectives, team health, predictability

### Prompt
For the last four sprints, your team has finished only about 60% of what they planned. Stakeholders are losing confidence. What do you do as Product Owner?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you treat it as a **system problem** to diagnose, rather than blaming the team.
- Whether you take **ownership** of causes on the PO side.
- Your use of **data and retrospectives** to understand what's happening.
- How you **rebuild stakeholder trust** while protecting the team.

#### Key points
- **Don't blame the team** or push them to work harder.
- **Diagnose with data**: planned versus completed, unplanned work, scope growth, blocked items, work in progress.
- **Look at your own role**: unclear stories, changing priorities, mid-sprint requests.
- **Discuss openly in a retrospective** and agree experiments.
- Focus on a **sprint goal**, not a list of items.
- **Reset expectations** with stakeholders: predictability first, then speed.

#### Model answer
"First, I'd avoid assuming anyone isn't working hard. When a team consistently misses, the cause is almost always the system around them.

**1. Look at the data with the Scrum Master and tech lead:**
- **Unplanned work**: how much capacity goes to bugs, incidents and urgent requests mid-sprint?
- **Scope growth**: do stories get bigger after the sprint starts? That's a sign of poor refinement.
- **Blocked work**: are items waiting on other teams, designs or decisions?
- **Planning approach**: are we planning based on past velocity, or on hope and stakeholder pressure?
- **Work in progress**: is the team starting many items at once and finishing few?

Say we find that about 25% of each sprint goes to production support, and stories regularly grow by 50% once work starts.

**2. Discuss it openly in a retrospective.** I'd share the data and ask the team what they think is causing it. In this example, they might say:
- 'Stories often aren't clear when the sprint starts. We discover requirements halfway through.' That's **my responsibility** to fix.
- 'Stakeholders go directly to developers with urgent requests.' I need to protect the sprint.
- 'Production support isn't planned for.'
- 'We feel pressured to commit to more than we can do.'

**3. Agree experiments for the next 2–3 sprints:**
- **Plan to about 80% of average velocity**, leaving room for the unexpected.
- **Reserve capacity for support**, or set up a rotation where one engineer handles support each sprint.
- **One clear sprint goal** (e.g. 'Customers can pay with Apple Pay'), with the backlog items as a plan to reach it. The team can then make trade-offs within the sprint while still meeting the goal.
- **Limit work in progress**: finish items before starting new ones.
- **Improve refinement**: smaller stories, clearer acceptance criteria and designs ready before planning.
- **All requests come through me**, so I can weigh them against the sprint goal.

**4. Reset expectations with stakeholders.** I'd explain what we found and what we're changing, and communicate commitments as a **sprint goal** plus a few 'stretch' items. I'd tell them we're prioritising predictability first. Once the team reliably meets its goals, we can look at speed.

**5. Inspect and adapt.** After 2–3 sprints, we review: are we meeting sprint goals more often? If not, we look again.

**Example result.** In a similar situation, after making these changes, the team went from meeting 1 in 4 sprint goals to meeting 5 in 6 over the next quarter. Stakeholders began trusting the forecasts again."

#### Another worked example: dependency-driven misses
"In another team, the main cause was **dependencies**: half our stories needed changes from the platform team, who had their own priorities.
- I worked with the platform team's PO to align our roadmaps a quarter ahead.
- We agreed a shared planning session at the start of each sprint.
- For items with dependencies, we only committed once the platform work was scheduled.
- Where possible, the team learned to make small platform changes themselves, with the platform team reviewing.

Completion rates improved, and both teams spent less time waiting."

#### Common causes and fixes
| Cause | Signs | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Poor refinement | Stories grow mid-sprint | Better preparation; smaller stories; spikes |
| Unplanned work | Bugs and incidents eat capacity | Reserve capacity; support rotation; fix root causes |
| Over-commitment | Planning above historical velocity | Plan to ~80% of average velocity |
| Changing priorities | Mid-sprint requests | All requests through the PO; protect the sprint |
| Too much in progress | Many items started, few finished | Limit WIP; swarm on items |
| Dependencies | Waiting on other teams | Align plans earlier; reduce dependencies |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Avoid blame**; treat it as a system problem.
2. **Gather data** on planning, unplanned work, scope growth, blockers and WIP.
3. **Run a retrospective** to discuss causes openly.
4. **Own PO-side issues.**
5. **Agree experiments** for 2–3 sprints.
6. **Reset stakeholder expectations.**
7. **Review and adapt.**

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I'd push the team to commit and deliver." | Diagnoses the system with data and the team |
| Blames estimates | Looks at unplanned work, refinement and priorities |
| Doesn't take ownership | Owns PO-side issues (clarity, protecting the sprint) |
| Treats velocity as performance | Uses velocity for planning only |
| No stakeholder communication | Resets expectations transparently |

#### Useful phrases
- "When a team keeps missing, it's almost always the system, not the people."
- "Let's look at where the time actually goes."
- "Part of this is on me. Our stories haven't been clear enough at the start of the sprint."
- "We'll commit to a sprint goal, and treat everything else as a bonus."
- "Predictability first, then speed."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Pressuring the team** to commit to more or work longer hours.
- **Blaming estimates** without looking at unplanned work and changing priorities.
- **Not owning** PO-side problems.
- **Treating velocity as a performance metric** or comparing teams' velocities.
- **Changing too many things at once**, so you can't tell what helped.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What's the difference between a sprint goal and the sprint backlog?"**
  "The sprint goal is the single objective for the sprint. The sprint backlog is the plan of items to reach it. The plan can change during the sprint, but the goal stays fixed."
- **"How do you handle urgent requests mid-sprint?"**
  "Assess them honestly. If it's truly urgent and more valuable than the sprint goal, swap something out rather than just adding it, and tell stakeholders what's been displaced."
- **"What if the Scrum Master isn't helping?"**
  "Raise it with them directly first, focusing on the team's goals. If it continues, involve our shared manager. The team needs a functioning Scrum Master to improve."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Systems thinking** across team, organisation and dependencies.
- **Psychological safety**: creating an environment where the team can raise problems openly.
- Looking at **organisational causes** (structure, dependencies, incentives).
- Improving **forecasting** with data (e.g. probabilistic forecasting based on throughput).

#### Practise it
1. Think of a team you've worked with that struggled to deliver (or invent one).
2. List three possible causes, the data that would confirm each, and one experiment for each.
3. Practise explaining the plan **out loud in 4 minutes**, first to the team and then to a frustrated stakeholder.

## An engineer's estimate is three times what you expected — how do you respond?
- id: po-agile-05
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: medium
- levels:
- tags: estimation, engineering partnership, trade-offs

### Prompt
You thought a feature would take about a week, but the engineers estimate it at three weeks. Stakeholders were expecting it soon. How do you handle it?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- Whether you **respect engineering expertise**, or push back to get the answer you want.
- Your ability to **explore options and trade-offs** with the team.
- How you **communicate changes** to stakeholders.
- Whether you **learn from it** and improve how you work.

#### Key points
- **Trust the estimate first**, then explore it with curiosity. The engineers know the system.
- Find out **what drives the cost**: hidden complexity, integrations, data, testing or security.
- Look for a **smaller version** that delivers most of the value (often 80% of the value from 20–30% of the work).
- **Communicate quickly** to stakeholders with options and a recommendation.
- **Involve engineers earlier** next time, before anything is promised.

#### Model answer
"First, I'd remind myself that the engineers almost certainly know something I don't, so I'd approach it with curiosity, not pushback.

**1. Understand the estimate.** I'd ask the team to walk me through what makes it three weeks. Typical hidden costs:
- An old part of the codebase with no automated tests, so every change needs careful manual testing.
- A third-party integration with poor documentation.
- Data migration for existing customers.
- Security or compliance reviews.
- Edge cases I hadn't thought of, e.g. different currencies, time zones or user permissions.

Say the feature is **'scheduled payments'** in a banking app, and the breakdown is:
- Core scheduling for one-off future payments: 4 days.
- Recurring payments (weekly, monthly), with rules for weekends and bank holidays: 6 days.
- International payments: 4 days.
- Notifications and editing scheduled payments: 2 days.

**2. Look for options together:**
- Which parts deliver most of the value? If data shows 80% of customers only want one-off future payments to UK accounts, we can start there.
- Can we drop or defer expensive edge cases?
- Is there a simpler technical approach, e.g. reusing an existing scheduling service?
- Can we deliver in slices, releasing something useful sooner?

The result might be: 'Release one-off scheduled UK payments in about 5 days, then recurring payments in the following sprint, and international payments later if demand justifies it.'

**3. Decide on the trade-off.** If the full scope is genuinely needed by the deadline, I accept three weeks and re-prioritise other work around it. If a smaller version delivers enough value, we go with that.

**4. Communicate quickly.** I'd tell stakeholders the same day:
> 'The full feature is about three weeks, mainly because of recurring rules and international payments. We can release one-off scheduled UK payments, which covers most customers' needs, in about a week, with recurring payments following two weeks later. My recommendation is to release in slices.'

**5. Learn from it.** If my expectations are often far off, the fix is to involve engineers earlier, ideally during discovery, before anything is communicated to stakeholders. I'd also avoid sharing my own guesses as if they were estimates."

#### Another worked example: when the estimate seems padded
"Sometimes stakeholders suspect estimates are padded. Instead of challenging the team, I'd:
- Look at **historical accuracy**: how have past estimates compared with actual time?
- Ask about **uncertainty**: 'Is this 3 weeks because it's complex, or because there are unknowns?' If it's unknowns, a short spike can reduce them.
- Ask for a **range**: 'What's the best case and worst case?' That often reveals where the risk is.

Trust is built when estimates are treated as honest forecasts, not negotiations."

#### Estimation approaches
| Approach | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Story points | Relative size (e.g. 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13) | Sprint planning; comparing items |
| T-shirt sizes | XS, S, M, L, XL | Rough roadmap planning |
| Time ranges | Best case to worst case | Communicating uncertainty to stakeholders |
| Throughput forecasting | Based on how many items the team finishes per week | Data-driven forecasts without estimating every item |
| Spikes | Time-boxed investigation | Reducing uncertainty before estimating |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Assume the team knows something you don't.**
2. **Understand the drivers** of the estimate.
3. **Find the high-value core** and options for slicing.
4. **Decide** on scope versus time.
5. **Communicate** to stakeholders quickly with a recommendation.
6. **Improve** how early engineers are involved.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "I'd ask them to reduce the estimate." | Explores what drives the estimate with curiosity |
| Accepts it without question | Looks for options and slices with the team |
| Already promised a date to stakeholders | Avoids promising before involving engineers |
| Treats estimates as commitments | Treats estimates as forecasts with uncertainty |
| No learning | Involves engineers earlier next time |

#### Useful phrases
- "Can you walk me through what's driving the estimate? I want to understand where the complexity is."
- "Which part gives customers most of the value? Could we release that first?"
- "What's the best case and worst case? Where's the uncertainty?"
- "Here's what we can deliver next week, and what follows."
- "Next time, I'll bring you in earlier, before we talk dates with stakeholders."

#### Common pitfalls
- **Pushing engineers to lower their estimate**, which leads to cut corners and missed dates.
- **Promising dates** to stakeholders without consulting the team.
- **Accepting estimates without exploring options.**
- **Treating estimates as commitments** rather than forecasts.
- **Hiding the change** from stakeholders until it's too late.

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if you think the team is padding estimates?"**
  "I'd look at historical accuracy and ask about uncertainty and ranges, rather than accusing anyone. If there's a pattern, I'd discuss it openly in a retrospective."
- **"Story points or hours?"**
  "Story points or t-shirt sizes for relative sizing and planning, because they capture complexity and uncertainty, not just time. For stakeholders, I translate them into date ranges based on our historical throughput."
- **"How do you give stakeholders dates?"**
  "As ranges with confidence levels, based on the team's actual throughput, and updated as we learn."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- A **true partnership** with engineering, involving them early in discovery.
- **Coaching stakeholders** to understand estimates as forecasts and ranges.
- Using **data-driven forecasting** (throughput, cycle time) rather than negotiation.

#### Practise it
1. Pick a feature you know and break it into 4–5 parts, guessing which part is most complex and why.
2. Define a "thin slice" that delivers most of the value in about a third of the time.
3. Practise telling a stakeholder **out loud in 2 minutes** that the full feature will take three times longer than expected, while offering the thin slice.

## A senior executive changes priorities mid-sprint — how do you handle it?
- id: po-agile-06
- round: STAKEHOLDER_AGILE
- difficulty: hard
- levels:
- tags: stakeholder management, sprint protection, executives

### Prompt
Halfway through a sprint, a senior executive asks the team to drop everything and build something new for a demo next week. What do you do?

### Answer
#### What the interviewer is really testing
- **Executive presence**: can you handle a senior person's urgent request calmly and confidently?
- Whether you **balance flexibility with focus**, neither blindly agreeing nor rigidly refusing.
- Your understanding of **who decides scope changes** in Scrum.
- Whether you **address the underlying pattern** if it keeps happening.

#### Key points
- **Don't refuse outright, and don't just say yes.** Understand the urgency and value first.
- In Scrum, the **Product Owner** negotiates scope with the developers during the sprint, and **only the PO can cancel a sprint** (when the sprint goal becomes obsolete).
- Make the **cost of switching visible**: what won't get done, and its impact.
- **Offer options**: a prototype, a smaller version or a swap.
- **Inform affected stakeholders** immediately.
- **Address the pattern** afterwards if it happens often.

#### Model answer
"I'd treat it as a legitimate request that needs a fast, informed decision.

**1. Understand the request.** I'd speak to the executive the same day and ask:
- What's the demo for, and who's the audience?
- What's at stake: a major investor, a strategic client, a board meeting?
- What exactly needs to be shown, and does it need to be working software or would a realistic prototype do?

A demo for a strategic client that could bring in significant revenue might genuinely outweigh the current sprint goal. But often a demo needs a **convincing prototype**, not production-quality software.

**2. Assess the impact with the team.** I'd quickly check with the tech lead and designer:
- How much work is it to build properly? A prototype? A smaller version?
- What would we have to stop, and what's the impact?

For example: 'Building it properly takes the whole team a week and means missing the fraud-detection release we promised the risk team, which reduces fraud losses by about £15k a week.'

**3. Offer options to the executive:**
- **Option A, clickable prototype**: the designer and one engineer build a realistic Figma or coded prototype in 3 days. Most of the sprint continues. Ideal if the audience needs to see the concept.
- **Option B, smaller working version**: reuse existing functionality for a limited demo. Takes half the team for a week; the fraud release slips by a few days.
- **Option C, full build**: the whole team switches. The sprint goal is dropped and the fraud release slips by a full sprint.

I'd give a recommendation, usually Option A, unless the audience genuinely needs working software.

**4. Decide and communicate.**
- If it's genuinely more valuable, I'd **swap** work out of the sprint, never just add it on top, and agree the change with the developers.
- I'd tell the affected stakeholders right away, e.g. the risk team about the delayed fraud release.
- If the sprint goal becomes obsolete (e.g. a major strategic change), I might **cancel the sprint** and re-plan, though that's rare.

**5. Address the pattern.** If this happens regularly, I'd talk to the executive afterwards, constructively:
> 'I want to make sure we can always respond when something important comes up. Could we have a short fortnightly sync, so I hear about upcoming demos early and can plan for them? We could also keep a small buffer each sprint for urgent requests.'

Throughout, I'd keep the tone collaborative: 'I want to help you get the best possible demo. Here are the options, and here's what each one costs us.'"

#### Another worked example: when the answer is yes
"Once, the CEO asked mid-sprint for a feature to support a partnership announcement that would bring in a large new customer segment. After assessing it, the value clearly outweighed our sprint goal (a set of smaller usability improvements). I agreed it with the team, cancelled the remaining sprint scope, re-planned around the partnership feature, and told stakeholders what had been displaced and when it would return. The partnership launched on time, and the usability work followed in the next sprint. Saying yes to the right things quickly is as important as saying no to the wrong things."

#### Options for urgent mid-sprint requests
| Option | When it fits | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Prototype or mock-up | The audience needs to see a concept | Low; sprint mostly continues |
| Smaller working version | Needs real software, limited scope | Medium; some sprint work slips |
| Swap work in the sprint | Truly more valuable than displaced work | Displaced work delayed |
| Cancel the sprint | The sprint goal is obsolete | High; full re-plan |
| Say no / defer | Low value or can wait | Possible tension; explain clearly |

#### Step-by-step framework
1. **Understand** the request, audience and stakes.
2. **Assess impact** with the team.
3. **Offer options** with costs and a recommendation.
4. **Decide**, swapping rather than adding work.
5. **Inform affected stakeholders.**
6. **Address the pattern** if it recurs.

#### Strong versus weak answer
| Weak answer | Strong answer |
|---|---|
| "Of course, we'll drop everything." | Assesses value and impact before deciding |
| "No, we're mid-sprint; it has to wait." | Recognises legitimate urgency and offers options |
| Adds the work on top of the sprint | Swaps work and makes the trade-off visible |
| Forgets the displaced stakeholders | Informs them immediately |
| Lets it keep happening | Addresses the pattern with the executive |

#### Useful phrases
- "Help me understand what the demo needs to achieve, and who'll be watching."
- "Here are three options, and what each one means for our current commitments."
- "My recommendation is a prototype. It gives you what you need for the demo without delaying the fraud release."
- "If we do this, here's what moves out of the sprint."
- "How can we make sure I hear about these earlier next time?"

#### Common pitfalls
- **Saying yes immediately** and overloading the team.
- **Flatly refusing** to protect "the process". Scrum exists to deliver value, not to be defended for its own sake.
- **Letting the executive go directly to developers**, bypassing the PO.
- **Not telling stakeholders** whose work was displaced.
- **Adding work without removing anything.**

#### Follow-ups to expect (with sample answers)
- **"What if the executive goes directly to an engineer?"**
  "I'd speak to the executive privately, explain why requests should come through me (so trade-offs are visible and the team isn't pulled in different directions), and agree a quick way for them to reach me."
- **"When would you cancel a sprint?"**
  "Only when the sprint goal becomes obsolete, such as a major change in strategy or market. It's rare, because it's disruptive."
- **"What if you suspect the demo isn't really important?"**
  "I'd ask about the stakes and audience. If the value is unclear, I'd recommend the cheapest option (a prototype), so we don't sacrifice important commitments for an uncertain benefit."

#### What senior interviewers look for
- **Executive presence**: calm, confident, solution-focused conversations with senior leaders.
- **Protecting team focus** over time, without being rigid.
- **Building relationships** with executives so you hear about things early.
- Setting up **mechanisms** (buffers, regular syncs) that handle urgency without chaos.

#### Practise it
1. Role-play with a friend acting as an impatient executive who wants something "by Friday".
2. Practise asking three clarifying questions, presenting three options with costs, and making a recommendation, all **within 4 minutes**.
3. Then practise the follow-up conversation about preventing last-minute requests in future.
